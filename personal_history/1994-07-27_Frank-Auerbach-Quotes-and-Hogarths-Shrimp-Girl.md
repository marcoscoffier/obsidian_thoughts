# Frank Auerbach Notes, Crowds & Hogarth's Shrimp Girl (Page 81)

Notes on **Frank Auerbach** (`FA:`) interwoven with Marco's own bracketed reflections (`[...]`):

### Making a New Thing & the "Heart of Hearts" Ambition (pp. 80v–81)
- Summarizes Auerbach's aim not simply to add another picture to the world (`[true, true...]`), but to make "a new thing for the world" like a new species of living thing—a wish Auerbach calls *grandiloquent* (`[pompous or bombastic eloquence - Amer. Heritage]`)—by starting from something specifically known and having more material than one can cope with.
- Lists 5 of 20 great self-contained images Auerbach names from memory:
  - Kenwood Rembrandt
  - Picasso *Head*
  - Matisse blue cut-out acrobats
  - Dürer — bent nose (*Conrad Vernell*)
  - Philips Koninck *View in Holland*
- Auerbach's closing remark about hoping in one's heart of hearts to surpass them prompts Marco's note:
  > `[My heart of hearts dream has been raising its head a lot lately — actually I guess it is always there driving. (shudder to think how enormous it can get.]`

### Consciousness, Crowds & Radical Redoing (p. 81v)
- **T.S. Eliot / working without self-consciousness:** Cites Auerbach quoting Eliot (*"to be conscious is not to be in time"*) on working without self-consciousness and adapting to whatever room or space is available.
- **Marco's reflection on crowd gesture drawings:**
  > `[I really like the gesture drawings of the crowds I have done—or rather the experience of making them, knowing that it didn't matter if they moved or tried to get up. It's a good feeling knowing that it is impossible to ruin something — any change is a change motivated by something inside you. If that motivation is powerful the change will be good.]`
- **Physical tension, scraping off & Hogarth's *Shrimp Girl*:** Notes Auerbach's account of large pictures demanding weeks of physical tension, repeatedly "finishing" them until crucial, radical decisions happen at the end—scraping off months or years of work and redoing from top to bottom quickly—and admiring William Hogarth's *The Shrimp Girl* as a "frozen instant/moment" where thick, liquid oil paint miraculously preserves a fleeting instant across 200 years.
