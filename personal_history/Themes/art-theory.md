## [[1994-07-28_Hollander-Bloom-Ashbery-and-Yales-Library|Writing Style, Hollander/Ashbery Paper & the Yale Library Stacks]]
*1994-07-28 · Yale, New Haven · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p013.jpg|p. 13]]*

on writing style w/ me this summer rather than all this art theory that I'm going to be incapable of doing anything w/ b/c I can't write well—I'm always sloppy & have trouble verbalising my thoughts. It feels better to have gotten that down.

I wish I could have found out what Hollander thought of my paper. I felt that for the first time I had brought something out of a text which I had not been led to by hand. The part about Merill's [above: *Merrill's*] poem was all Hollander & Bloom: Merill [above: *Merrill*] is the poet's poet, I am not a poet. The part about Ashbery was mine. It somehow combined everything I had been thinking about in the studio & in sculpture class but was unable to turn into artworks. Ashbery was talking my language. I knew from where the metonomies for his metaphors came (I'll probably be throwing these terms around based on the cursory understanding I got out of Eco's essay. For a while)

I thought a lot this year. Too much really; I didn't do. I have back-tracked, realizing that it is too ambitious for me to want to make paintings about stereoptics or 180° vision at this point in my development. I have to learn about paint, about thinking, seeing & loving in a painterly way, which doesn't necessarily imply becoming stupid about other things. Frank Auerbach said all of the Greatest Painters (why caps?) he can think of read a lot. It is impossible to say anything worthwhile without reading. Joyce was an insatiable reader.

My anxiety has cleared. I know that I love expanding my network of fixed (appropriated) points in the ocean of knowledge (WW) that makes up the stacks of books in the Yale Library system and in the Library system of the world. What does a library contain? Information? Knowledge? Art? Life? Humanity? Reproductions of all the above? traces of all the above? Documentation of...?

## [[1995-01-11_Art-Is-Not-Dead-Cezannes-Letters|Art Is Not Dead; Cézanne's Letters]]
*1995-01-11 · Final Semester Yale 1995 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1995_p010.jpg|p. 10]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1995_p011.jpg|p. 11]]*

1/11/95. I think it is stupid for someone to say art is dead. It has been exploded open. No matter how far we go, there will always be that unknown place where the artist must wearily take a step to that ephemeral tingling sensation where, if he is lucky, the light shines on him and his step for a second or so and he becomes frozen in ~~[?]~~ tradition - and his step seems, <u>to his successors, as obvious</u>, as it did seem extreme to his contemporaries.
To Emile Zola, Aix n.d. ~19 Oct 1866 ~~[?]~~ Paul Cezanne Letters Rewald (London: Cassirer, 1941)
"But you know all pictures ~~inside~~ painted inside, in the studio, will never be as good as the things done outside. ~~[?]~~ p. 74.
I am progressing very slowly, for nature reveals herself to me in very complex forms; and the progress needed is incessant. One must see one's model correctly and experience it in the right way; and furthermore express oneself forcibly and with distinction.
Taste is the best judge. It is rare. Art only addresses itself to an excessively small number of individuals.
The artist must scorn all judgement that is not based on an intelligent observation of character. He must beware of the literary spirit (narrative painting?) which so often causes painting to deviate from its true path - the concrete study of nature - to lose itself ~~out~~ too long in the intangible speculations.
The Louvre is a good book to consult but it must only be an intermediary. The real and immense study that must be taken up is the manifold picture of nature. (235-6)

**Next page:**

To achieve progress nature alone counts, and the eye is trained through contact with her. It becomes concentric by looking and working. I mean to say that in an orange, an apple, a bowl, a head, there is a culminating point; and this point is always in spite of the tremendous effect of light and shade and colorful sensations - the closest to our eye; the edges of the object recede to a center on our horizon. With a small temperament one can be very much a painter. One can do good things w/out being very much of a hormonist or a colorist. It is sufficient to have a sense of art - and this sense is doubtless the horror of the bourgeois. Therefore institutions, pensions, honors can only be made for cretins, rogues and rascals. <u>Do not be an art critic, but paint, therein lies salvation.</u> p. 239.
Now being nearly 70 years old, the sensations of color, which give light, are the reasons for the abstractions which prevent me from either covering my canvas or continuing the delimitation of the objects when their points ~~drop off~~ of contact are fine and delicate; from which it results that my image or picture is incomplete. ~~On the other hand,~~ p. 251-2.
I am becomming more clearsighted in front of nature, but that with me the realization of my sensations is always very difficult. I cannot attain the intensity that is unfolded before my senses. I have not the magnificent richness of coloring that animates nature. ~~Here on the~~ Here on the edge of the river, the motifs are very plentiful, the same subject seen from a different angle gives a subject for study of the highest interest and so varied that I think I could be occupied for months without changing my place, simply bending a little more to the right or left. p. 262.
