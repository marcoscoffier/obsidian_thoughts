## [[1994-06-19_Notes-on-Visual-Language|Notes on Visual Language]]
*1994-06-19 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 40 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p066.jpg|p. 66]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p068.jpg|p. 68]]*

A language names things—organizes the world by naming parts of it. The same can be said for a visual language: when asked to draw tranquility, a huge majority of people draw horizontal lines (reminiscent of sleep, when the body is horizontal). Femininity equals curved lines and mysterious crossed lines (reminiscent of crossed legs, or Aphrodite's hand across the breast). Diebenkorn's abstractions are so reminiscent of New Mexico—the patterns of land as seen from a plane—versus Ocean Park. One can't confuse an Ocean Park for a New Mexico landscape.

## [[1994-06-22_Critique-of-Earthworks|Critique of Earthworks (Heizer, De Maria, Smithson)]]
*1994-06-22 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 39 · scan [[attachments/Screenshot_2026-11-18_at_10.27.18_AM.png|Screenshot_2026-11-18_at_10.27.18_AM]]*

I think that the earthworks sculptors—Heizer, De Maria, [Smithson]—[seem] absolutely trite when compared to the enormous [works built] out of necessity: highways, over-passes, cities, t[unnels]... These three have trouble finding the virgin l[andscape] because they are being crowded out by society... [Their] sculpture is so dependent on the remote locati[on—does] the object itself have something to do w/ this... of the art object becomes gross when it is [placed in a setting] of greater beauty. What aesthetic is being [served by] heavy construction equipment connecting their works to highways & cities & the earth materials which makes *Complex One* not very different from a highway underpass? The mirrors which Smithson uses seem to be a little more interesting to me, but he doesn't do anything w/ them.
*(Bracketed note: `[see "Existentially, the human cannot leave the field" — Gregory Bateson "Double Bind" p. 10]`)*

## [[1994-07-22_Michael-Heizer-vs-James-Turrell|Michael Heizer vs. James Turrell]]
*1994-07-22 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 74 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p129.jpg|p. 129]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p130.jpg|p. 130]]*

There is something I don't like about Michael Heizer's constructions—his holes and objects. He doesn't have a respect for the land or the place where he is building *Double Negative* (exploding huge areas of earth). I am sure that if I were to go visit that work, I would prefer a view further down the canyon where no one could imagine the object, so I could enjoy nature for its own sake.

Count Panza described how the approach to a Greek temple purposely limits your view so that when you finally get to the temple, you turn around (or go beyond it) and are confronted by the view which inspired its construction. Turrell seems in line with this type of effect—his Roden Crater is not so much about leaving his mark on the landscape as it is about bringing the viewer to experience the landscape's magical properties. Heizer is scarring the landscape, leaving his mark like a circle in the desert made with a motorcycle track.

I remember a quote by action/adventure photographer Galen Rowell, something like: *"Go into nature, leave it untouched, return with nothing"*—only taking a fraction of a second's worth of light. As Calvino wrote in *Invisible Cities*: *"Memories' images, once they are fixed in words, are erased."*
