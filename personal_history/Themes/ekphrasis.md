## [[1994-07-09_Painting-on-Chapel-York-Garage|Painting on Chapel-York Garage & How Art Transforms Space]]
*1994-07-09 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 58 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p100.jpg|p. 100]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p102.jpg|p. 102]]*

I spent the day, Saturday, painting on top of the Chapel-York Garage. I almost gave up early in the day, exasperated at how quickly the acrylics would dry in the sun. I couldn't get the right color for the shadows a sand colored building—everything was too dark or too green. But stuck with it and changed to a flexible small flat brush, soft like a watercolor brush. Finally, I forgot about the time while I was  painting. That hasn't happened in the longest time. I mixed cerulean blue and cadmium orange to get a light grey for the sandy shadows. Hopefully it will be sunny again tomorrow so I can go finish it.

While I was out there I thought of what in the world someone else would see if they came up. The top of a parking garage—not very exciting. I remembered Harold Bloom telling us about when Archie [A.R.] Ammons took him to the part of the Jersey Shore which inspired *"Corson's Inlet."* Bloom, in his usual exasperated sarcasm, said that his trek through those bleak sand-dunes added nothing to the poem; all he wanted was to find the closest coffee house. No wonder the man's body is about to fail him completely at 64.

This brings up a similarity between painting and poetry. Both transform the space which inspired them. They change this space for the viewer/reader, or present them with something that doesn't need the association to the space, though the artist needed this association to inform the work. This sounds like it could be validation for abstract poetry, but even the most "realistic" painting or photograph changes the space. One cannot escape the mediation of the artist—especially a good artist, who will tend to leave his imprint on his representation. This imprint is recognized and cherished.

## [[1994-07-29_Lighthouse-Point-with-Amy-and-Literary-Sea-Memory|Pier at Lighthouse Point w/ Amy & Literary Sea Memory (Page 89)]]
*1994-07-29 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 89 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p018.jpg|p. 18]]*

I was standing on the end of a Pier w/ Amy at Lighthouse Point. We were on large rectangular rocks which had obviously been placed there by men but long enough ago that they were covered in thick slimy green and any indentation was filled w/ life and shells. I was reminded of V. Woolf's *"Kew Gardens"*. Some children playing in such a puddle could enlarge it until it became as big as the famous English gardens. I am always reminded of a picture in *Outside* magazine (I should buy a macro adaptor-filter?)

We then got up and faced the wind on the sea. It was very hazy so the sun wasn't coming from any particular direction though it was strong and made us squint. We were talking about literature. "Have you read...?" "No, Have you read...?" All my perceptions of the sea have been formed by novels about a young man who goes off to sea—*Horatio Hornblower*, *Nostromo*—I know there are others but I can't remember their names. My image of the sea has been formed by these books. I've never spent much time near ports, weathered boards, barnacles, heavy ropes & pulleys, wet canvas. I know of these things only through an image I have created in conjunction with the books I have read. These books impregnate the image of any of these objects, say the barnacles on the rocks w/ Amy, with a memory which is not my own, but one in me which was created by these books.

## [[1994-07-31_Alps-Space-Stereoscopic-Boxes-and-Sound-Bike-Room|Alps Space, Stereoscopic Boxes & Sound-Bike Octagon Room (Page 93)]]
*1994-07-31 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 93 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p029.jpg|p. 29]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p030.jpg|p. 30]]*

**Visual experience in Literature beyond the ekphrastic.**

A 2D picture traps space w/in it. the viewer is kept from experiencing the space in which the photographer, or painter, was standing by the edges of his image. The edges, the frame defines it as an image but the viewer cannot fly through the space on that flat plane; He is constantly grounded by the frame. It is impossible for me to convey my feeling about the Alps on paper with photo emulsion, pencil or paint b/c that experience has everything to do with the feeling of space I experience in the mountains.

`((` Writers can tap into this space b/c their medium is narrative. In order to experience space as space, one has to move through it. The visual experience of a flat image is static in time & space. One cannot move through it, into it, but only around it. It is frozen in time.

I did a sculpture project about stereoscopic vision last semester and the culmination of a series of investigations was a set of boxes where I cut up "Stereoscopic" photographs — I had taken 2 views of a still-life, right eye/left eye, I xeroxed one onto acetate on a color copier, then pasted the clear one over its partner. — I cut these photographs by object and placed them in a box at the relative distances these objects had had in the original still life. *[Cube box sketch with layered cutout planes inside]* I was left w/ a lot of empty space btw. these slices of picture. There was no way of looking at the box so that the pictures would come back together as one.

Maybe it would be possible to make such a box, the viewer would have to be directed towards the particular initial viewing spot — sees flat image. Then, when he approaches it, he realizes that they are forced into space.
