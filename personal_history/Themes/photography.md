## [[1994-07-22_Michael-Heizer-vs-James-Turrell|Michael Heizer vs. James Turrell]]
*1994-07-22 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 74 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p129.jpg|p. 129]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p130.jpg|p. 130]]*

There is something I don't like about Michael Heizer's constructions—his holes and objects. He doesn't have a respect for the land or the place where he is building *Double Negative* (exploding huge areas of earth). I am sure that if I were to go visit that work, I would prefer a view further down the canyon where no one could imagine the object, so I could enjoy nature for its own sake.

Count Panza described how the approach to a Greek temple purposely limits your view so that when you finally get to the temple, you turn around (or go beyond it) and are confronted by the view which inspired its construction. Turrell seems in line with this type of effect—his Roden Crater is not so much about leaving his mark on the landscape as it is about bringing the viewer to experience the landscape's magical properties. Heizer is scarring the landscape, leaving his mark like a circle in the desert made with a motorcycle track.

I remember a quote by action/adventure photographer Galen Rowell, something like: *"Go into nature, leave it untouched, return with nothing"*—only taking a fraction of a second's worth of light. As Calvino wrote in *Invisible Cities*: *"Memories' images, once they are fixed in words, are erased."*

## [[1994-08-02_Self-Disclosure-and-Marrying-Katherine|Painting vs. Camera, Self-Disclosure & Thinking of Marrying Katherine]]
*1994-08-02 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p047.jpg|p. 47]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p053.jpg|p. 53]]*

Today, taking pictures, I thought it's silly for the painter to fight to represent exactly what a camera sees. A painting is a painting—everyone knows this, so I might as well enjoy it for what it is.

The first 28 pages of this book (not including the notes I have since added) took me a year to write, and I have almost finished the rest in two months. Somehow I am glad that I wrote down this beginning—I write down too much. Maybe it would be more mysterious if I hid these things, like I think I should hide some things in conversation, and not make myself so crystal clear.

*(Later annotation: I still feel these things, probably even stronger—if that is possible, because they weren't feelings that had to do with strength or measurement of any kind; they are good and solid. I've been thinking about living with Katherine—marrying?—a lot lately.)*

## [[1994-08-02_Studio-Painting-Ansel-Adams-and-Textures|Studio Painting, Ansel Adams & Mixed Textures]]
*1994-08-02 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p051.jpg|p. 51]]*

I have now realized that all of these painting ideas require a technical prowess that I do not yet have. The technical level has to complement the idea, or the work becomes silly. Who knows, maybe I'll get there?

I thought today that I might actually try to make a painting "in the studio" when I return to New Haven. In the studio the painter is aware of his relationship to the painting; nature is not present. I also thought that setting up a photograph *à la* Ansel Adams is similar to setting up a painting—Polaroid sketches, trying different filters.

I was excited by my first try with textures today. I'm going to definitely try mixing anything with medium: sawdust, fine sand, dirt, grass. Gesso is a kind of adhesive—I wonder if I could glue grass, leaves, or branches onto a surface with it and then paint on it. I also thought of building a diorama-like 3D painting by layering successive levels on receding planes.
