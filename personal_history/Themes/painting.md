## [[1994-06-06_Goodbye-to-Katherine|Katherine Leaves & Emotional Restraint]]
*1994-06-06 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 29 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p048.jpg|p. 48]]*

Well, Katherine left today. I choked as I tried to say "Bye, Babe." I came so close to crying as I walked back to Daria's from the curb, from where Lisa's blue station wagon pulled Katherine's ever-smiling face away from me. I can't remember ever having felt so natural and happy with someone. 

*(Later annotation: That kind of statement is always problematic—I wonder if it will sound like the 10th grader who wrote about Nira to me someday.)*

I always wonder why I never cry. Do I rationalize my way out of it, or am I stunted emotionally? If I am deprived of the crying extreme, I must also be kept from extreme happiness. I know I don't explode into the exuberant, unrestrained laughter that Katherine does. I love that about her; she is often as emotionally free as a 5-year-old, her voice sliding through octaves and registers over which she has as little control as her toddler counterpart.

I want to start to write regularly, so that I may work out the kinks and awkwardnesses of transcribing my thoughts. I want to try to get this process as free as Katherine's laughter, and as that loose painting stroke she so appreciates.

## [[1994-06-27_Time-in-Text-vs-Space-in-Painting|Time in Text vs. Space in Painting]]
*1994-06-27 · Yale, New Haven · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p046.jpg|p. 46]]*

The viewing of a painting happens in a particular place, though its viewing is not through time like the processing of a text. The appreciation of a text is all about time—the buildup—so often it is hard to pinpoint the passage that moved you because to be moved, you had to have been put in a certain mood by the text which preceded it. If you try to return to that passage, it no longer has the same effect because you no longer have the same baggage, or you have added to it by reading beyond the quote.

If one tries to pinpoint the exact part of a painting one likes, this too is impossible because the instant that the part is removed from the whole, it is no longer the same place—it is removed, transported to another space, and its meaning changes (synecdoche).

## [[1994-08-01_Dream-of-Showing-Hull-Four-Wall-Cityscape|Dream of Showing Hull a Four-Wall Cityscape Room (Page 95)]]
*1994-08-01 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 95 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p033.jpg|p. 33]]*

*(Circled dream entry at bottom of page 95, linked visually to the painted-room sketches above it):*

I had this crazy dream about bringing Hull in to see a room where I had painted a cityscape on all 4 walls. I had built the whole room, you could see the frame work on the outside. I hadn't told anyone what I was doing. I wasted incredible numbers of hours doing it. I wasn't even taking painting, and here it was. It was crazy of me to have done this. It was too much, I had sacrificed too many other things but here it was, and I was looking for acceptance. I was laughing out of nervousness and excitement. It was wonderful.

## [[1994-10-05_Conversion-on-Twombly-and-Pollock|Change of Heart on Cy Twombly & Jackson Pollock]]
*1994-10-05 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 52 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p088.jpg|p. 88]]*

A key moment this year was my conversion of judgment on Cy Twombly—his strokes can be quite amazing, reminiscent of the delicate flowing Japanese cursive style (like a blackboard on which you can still see previous classes). At the same time, an early Pollock painting I saw at the Guggenheim moved me more than any work in memory (*Ocean Greyness*, 1953: in the actual painting it is much more obvious that the grey is painted on top of the colored stuff; he has selected out parts he liked and unified the whole).
