## [[1994-06-07_Writing-as-a-Companion-for-Memory|Writing as a Companion for Memory]]
*1994-06-07 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 30 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p049.jpg|p. 49]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p050.jpg|p. 50]]*

Well, this is my second straight day of recording my thoughts. I feel like this book has become a sort of companion for my brain. It records the flotsam and jetsam of my thoughts that would otherwise disappear into the ocean of forgotten things. When these thoughts are collected and kept, they can be built upon and stimulate other thoughts. I can then start the rewriting, revising, rethinking which Valéry claimed was essential to making a work of art. There is no one author, he claimed, because the author returns to the work (latent as a closed book) and revises it. The work cannot be isolated from the ongoing flow of the author's mind and life.

I wonder if this process will be similar to what I read in *The Idea of Music* about some Greek philosopher [Socrates] who opposed writing things down because that would mean one wouldn't have to remember—writing, he said, was the first step toward weakening one's memory. I wonder if the narrator of *The Name of the Rose* could actually remember all the lists and details which he restates in his retelling of conversations he witnessed, or if these lists were a means of scholastic pyrotechnics, which Eco says can lead to endless contemplation.

## [[1994-06-27_Mental-Range-DC-to-New-Haven-and-Atomic-Scale|Mental Range, D.C. to New Haven & Atomic Scale]]
*1994-06-27 · Yale, New Haven · scan p. 34*

...change in [...] as any sharpness; [...] allowed to occur over a wider range than mental lines can keep:

- Mental range must frame off a section of the world/Universe. It cannot grasp it all. If one tries to conceive of the trip from D.C. to New Haven, one can recall particular images of rest stops, toll booths, bridges, but in no way can remember each sequence; instead one has to resort to the image of a map or a diagram. The range is too great—it must be simplified to fit w/in the scope of what can be understood.

The same goes for imagining the atoms that make up the molecules in our hand, or this pen which is recording my thoughts. I can start w/ the atom or cell, but there is a large bridge to be gapped before I can imagine the solidity of my hand, or the different cells which make up the muscle, bone, skin etc. Even from the atom to the cell, one diagram to the other, from the nucleus w/ e⁻'s to any solid is a conceptual leap too great for the limited range of our conceptions.

## [[1994-07-22_Michael-Heizer-vs-James-Turrell|Michael Heizer vs. James Turrell]]
*1994-07-22 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 74 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p129.jpg|p. 129]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p130.jpg|p. 130]]*

There is something I don't like about Michael Heizer's constructions—his holes and objects. He doesn't have a respect for the land or the place where he is building *Double Negative* (exploding huge areas of earth). I am sure that if I were to go visit that work, I would prefer a view further down the canyon where no one could imagine the object, so I could enjoy nature for its own sake.

Count Panza described how the approach to a Greek temple purposely limits your view so that when you finally get to the temple, you turn around (or go beyond it) and are confronted by the view which inspired its construction. Turrell seems in line with this type of effect—his Roden Crater is not so much about leaving his mark on the landscape as it is about bringing the viewer to experience the landscape's magical properties. Heizer is scarring the landscape, leaving his mark like a circle in the desert made with a motorcycle track.

I remember a quote by action/adventure photographer Galen Rowell, something like: *"Go into nature, leave it untouched, return with nothing"*—only taking a fraction of a second's worth of light. As Calvino wrote in *Invisible Cities*: *"Memories' images, once they are fixed in words, are erased."*

## [[1994-07-29_Lighthouse-Point-with-Amy-and-Literary-Sea-Memory|Pier at Lighthouse Point w/ Amy & Literary Sea Memory (Page 89)]]
*1994-07-29 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 89 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p018.jpg|p. 18]]*

I was standing on the end of a Pier w/ Amy at Lighthouse Point. We were on large rectangular rocks which had obviously been placed there by men but long enough ago that they were covered in thick slimy green and any indentation was filled w/ life and shells. I was reminded of V. Woolf's *"Kew Gardens"*. Some children playing in such a puddle could enlarge it until it became as big as the famous English gardens. I am always reminded of a picture in *Outside* magazine (I should buy a macro adaptor-filter?)

We then got up and faced the wind on the sea. It was very hazy so the sun wasn't coming from any particular direction though it was strong and made us squint. We were talking about literature. "Have you read...?" "No, Have you read...?" All my perceptions of the sea have been formed by novels about a young man who goes off to sea—*Horatio Hornblower*, *Nostromo*—I know there are others but I can't remember their names. My image of the sea has been formed by these books. I've never spent much time near ports, weathered boards, barnacles, heavy ropes & pulleys, wet canvas. I know of these things only through an image I have created in conjunction with the books I have read. These books impregnate the image of any of these objects, say the barnacles on the rocks w/ Amy, with a memory which is not my own, but one in me which was created by these books.

## [[1994-07-31_Mortality-Andre-Diard-and-Tignes|Mortality, André Diard & Summers in Tignes]]
*1994-07-31 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · scan [[attachments/Screenshot_2026-11-18_at_10.26.11_AM.png|Screenshot_2026-11-18_at_10.26.11_AM]]*

- This journal and any art possibly can be seen as a fight against death. I want to maximize my time before death—do the most w/ my life & thoughts, & possibly leave something worthwhile behind.

**7/31/94**  
André Diard died this winter. He was a key figure in the town of Tignes and a fixed memory since he ran the rental of our apartment at Les Chaudes Almes. I will always remember being on the American team golfing into the inflatable baby pools anchored off the dock of Le Terrachu *(name of the almost lost dialect of the region)*. His ringing voice (and laugh), his sharply pointed eyebrows & permanent dark tan will never be completely gone.

I remember how sad we were when the Diards gave up running Le Terrachu. All the wonderful nights we had spent there (and delicious dinners we had been served) would be a memory. Our summers were never quite the same, but we would still see the Diards now and then and Mr. Diard would ring out w/ *"Salut les Américains"* and always have a chum(b)ly relationship w/ Richie.

As we were coming up the stairs just now I mentioned my plane flight last summer w/ Laurent's Dad (Mr. Pèremezelle(?)). *"I never went up w/ André,"* Mom said—Mr. Diard took passengers for sight-tours in his small 4-seater. *"That is something I will always regret..."*

Il est allé chercher le tomb de son père plusieurs fois mais ne l'a jamais trouver.

Le père d'Albert Camus est mort dans de circonstances similaire personne ne savait où il était enterré. Camus a finalement trouvé le tombeaux, mais dès qu'il se trouvait devant, il ne savait pas quoi faire.
