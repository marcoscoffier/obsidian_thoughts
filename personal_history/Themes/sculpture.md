## [[1994-06-26_Rooms-Mirrors-and-Installation-Concepts|Thoughts During a Reading of *Languages of Art*]]
*1994-06-26 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 48 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p080.jpg|p. 80]]*

Make a room, or set of walls with openings—make them nicely with drywall, sharp corners, putty, painted. Surround each opening with a picture frame (only on one side). Could I use one-way mirrors? From one side people make their own image; from the other they see the same.

My other idea: a box, a setup still life (with Playmobil?). Two openings in the box: on one end have a picture frame with an empty opening inside to see the actual setup; from the other, have a photograph of the setup from that side. 

What about two-way mirrors, one on each side with the mirror side facing inwards? How about an entire box with all sides made with one-way mirrors facing inwards—you see an infinite space of which you are not a part. Could I put an object inside these reflections? Another form of infinity: a frame, painting of a frame, of a photograph of a framed painting of a photograph.

## [[1994-07-26_Richard-Serra-on-Weight-and-History|Richard Serra on Weight, Vulcan's Forge & the Residue of History]]
*1994-07-26 · Yale, New Haven · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p001.jpg|p. 1]]*

Pasted printed text clipping signed **"RICHARD SERRA, Summer 1988, Cape Breton"** inserted at the opening of the notebook:

- Serra reflects on choosing *weight* over lightness—not because weight is inherently more compelling, but because he knows more about its balancing, rigging, propping, concentration, rotation, disequilibrium, gravity, and the processing of steel in the forge, rolling mill, and open hearth.
- Contrasts Sisyphus's endless boulder pushing with **Vulcan's tireless labor** at the bottom of a smoking crater hammering out raw material—preferring constructive daily concentration and effort over "the light fantastic" or "the quest for the ethereal," noting that things chosen for lightness soon reveal unbearable weight.
- Lists the "unbearable weight" of repression, constrictions, government, tolerance, resolution, responsibility, destruction, suicide, and history dissolving weight into a "calculated construction of palpable lightness."
- Closes by describing the residue of history—the printed page and the flicker of the image—as fragmentary and always saying less than the weight of experience.

## [[1994-08-03_Arnheim-Form-as-Visible-Shape-of-Content|Arnheim: Form as Content, Illusion, Equivalence & Spatial Images (Pages 102–102v)]]
*1994-08-03 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 102 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p043.jpg|p. 43]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p044.jpg|p. 44]]*

Notes from *Art & Visual Perception* **pp. 80, 96, 98, 105, 107, 130, 132** paired with Marco's annotations and spatial installation thought:

##### Summary of Arnheim Excerpts (pp. 80–132)
- **p. 80 (Single vs. narrative images):** Contrasts a film man pushing through a crowd vs. a still photograph where the artist must rupture still verticals visually: `"Still verticals ruptured by triangular shape?..."`
- **p. 96 (`"Form is the visible shape of content"` — Ben Shahn):** Summarizes how external shape, color, and behavior express inner nature (tea cup vs. knife; rabbit), Wittgenstein's drawn triangle seen as geometric figure, mountain, wedge, or pointer, and marble sculpture referencing living forms rather than stone. Includes Marco's contemporary addition:
  > `[Modern sculpture is more aware that the material speaks about the object — Ron Jones' sculpture made out of wood which it is illegal to export from South Africa, about S. Africa. Sand from..., each w/ its own colors etc.]`
- **Critique of "false naturalness" (pp. 96–97):** Notes Dürer's rabbit, Classic Maya stylized imagery, and Goethe's remark about transforming artifice into nature (`[Goethe's quote seems to sound like Zeuxsis or Giotto comments... I think I see what Arnheim is getting at b/c of the Maya reference above, but the Goethe quote is anything but clear.]`), plus Marco questioning Arnheim's wording on why a "draftsman" depicts what he knows (`Arny often falls into language traps... what are these rules?`).
- **pp. 98, 105, 107, 130, 132 (Projection, Equivalence & Rotating Shadows):** Summarizes that image-making renders an observed equivalent within a medium's properties rather than mechanical optical projection (`[à la grid sketch]`), explains perceptual constancy vs. retinal amoebic shifts (and Marco's note: `[This means that when a person sees a rotating shadow on a flat sheet he very well may see the precept conceived by the brain in 3D...]`), contrasts mobile film/stage sequences against frozen painting (`p. 105`, Marco noting: `again Arnheim's language becomes troublesome. I think it might serve me best to take ideas from him w/out quoting.`), notes `2D = translation of 3D p. 107`, merging partial impressions (`p. 130`), and modern artists recovering the innocent eye against fleeting glimpses (`p. 132`).

##### Marco's Closing Question (p. 102v)
- `- I return to my idea of creating a place by a bunch of images hung in spacial relation to each other which repeats the spacial relation the objects represented had to each other. Is this taking art forward or backwards?`

## [[1994-08-12_Sculpture-Garden-and-West-Rock-Cliff|The Sculpture Garden & West Rock Cliff]]
*1994-08-12 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 70 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p122.jpg|p. 122]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p123.jpg|p. 123]]*

After a long exposed walk down the alley that runs by Skull & Bones and leads only to the bronze gate to the sculpture garden, I pull out my key and slip into a dark corridor. Turning right into an unroofed space—walls of dark burgundy shooting upwards like a medieval European street—a narrow band of sunlight illuminates the entire right-hand wall with orange brightness, picking out every bit of textural detail in the stone while the left wall remains in impenetrable blackness. Past the Henry Moore statue and the lantern, I find the shade of a Louise Nevelson wall made of bronze boxes to put on my climbing shoes.

I quit rugby two years ago because I wanted to combine my athletic endeavors with some connection to nature. I will always remember one of my first days mountain biking up West Rock. I pushed through a small tree and some bushes and found myself, bike in hand, on the edge of the cliff facing the sunset. Below me was a massive lake, with a tiny boat and a sole fisher on its bright surface. I was 25 minutes from my dorm room in dirty, motorcycle- and fire-truck-ridden New Haven, and I couldn't hear a thing, let alone see a house.

Dad has taught me something I will cherish and enjoy the rest of my life: there is something beautiful and necessary in the immensity of nature. Reading about James Turrell and Count Panza makes me believe there can be art which evokes this feeling—a feeling found in the bowl of mountain and sky.

## [[1995-01-10_Charkow-on-Carriage-and-Freshness|Charkow on Carriage and Freshness]]

1/10/95 Natalie Charkow mentioned an Italian sculpture professor who had been a child prodigy - but was terrible. Carriage is so important to a likeness - I remember getting excited about recognizing Paul in my 1st notebook and I remember how I captured katherine somehow that 1st time I painted her in Susannah's class - ~~that~~ ~~were~~ ~~[?]~~ was an inspired ~~wonderful~~ moment or was it, I don't remember. I think they are easily equated to times in athletics when you are just <u>on</u>. Everything is in synch. (I had an awesome bike ride yesterday - cleared all the downhills except the last two which I walked rather than risk my luck - and rode the top trail well - what an enjoyable relief.)
Charkow mentioned Auerbach and Matisse as two artists who were willing to change their entire images to maintain some of the initial freshness or immediacy, ~~but~~ she described a movie of Matisse painting where the pose collapses on one arm, then over compensates in the other direction and then gels. I don't know if Auerbach gels - or does he just stop. It seems to me that the best moments are fleeting throughout the series not particularly the final one. - not like Matisse's blue nude series.
