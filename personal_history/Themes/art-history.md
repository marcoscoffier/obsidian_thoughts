## [[1995-03-06_Leonard-on-Wordsworth-Ruskin-and-Reynolds|Leonard on Wordsworth, Ruskin and Reynolds]]
*1995-03-06 (estimated) · Final Semester Yale 1995 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1995_p042.jpg|p. 42]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1995_p043.jpg|p. 43]]*

Wordsworth & Ruskin: speak of conferring "power" on the audience their famous training for the eye. It was, in fact, a counter-training.
the Zeuxis parable (all those Croatian maidens compared) had become, in the 18th centuries a training for the eye.
But what a training. Wordsworth & Ruskin would later send you out into the world with the new power to transfigure the commonplace; idealist art training would disfigure even the "most beautiful forms" you previously admired, train you in their "weakness" and "imperfection." I use the word "disfigure," to emphasize that ideal art did not merely leave the common place alone. Young people were being trained to look at life and find it ugly... Wordsworth and Ruskin could not let that go unchallenged. They did not. G. J. Leonard Into the Light of Things. p. 77.
To speed up the student's power to discover "deformity" in the common day, Reynolds says he knows of only "one method" that will "shorten the road": judge nature by art. Compare flawed nature with the ideal creations, in this line, of previous thinkers who themselves reached the heights by 'careful study of classical sculpture. The "ancient sculptors" left "models" of "perfect form" by being indefagitable in the school of nature" - that is, indefagitable in finding fault w/ nature [I'm not sure that this is right at all - or the generalisations are far to simplified - Athenian sculpture of the age of Pericles may have sought this perfection but certainly not Hellenistic Sculpture.
What is frightening about this whole passage and mode of thinking (of Reynolds etc) is that it is so inclusive. It does not allow for difference. Must humanity always go through cycles of Athenian Hellenistic 1950's - 1990's
`[bracketed:]` It would be Wordsworth's task, Colderidge wrote, to produce a feeling "analogous to the supernatural by awakening the mind's attention to the "wonders of the world before us."
Most kantian lines: Recluse & TA ½ creates mind ½ perceives

**Next page:**

Forerunner to Wordsworth: Archibald Alison 1790 Essays on the Nature and Principles of Taste. p. 1
' "Sublimity" and even "beauty" are not properties of objects but "emotions" we have about objects. There is a difference between ordinary sight and awakened sight, for the "simple perception" of an object isn't enough to "excite" those desirable emotions (that is, beauty, sublimity) unless sight is "accompanied with" an "operation of the mind" ' Leonard p. 58.
'As proof that unless we "excersize" our "imagination", we don't feel the 'emotions of beauty and sublimity," think how much "difference" there is in the feelings we have at different times about "the same objects" In the course of making that point, Alison quite casually assumes the beauty in the simple produce of the common day and makes it our fault that we don't find it: "In the scenery of external nature", the beautiful and the sublime are almost constantly before us; and not a day passes, without presenting us with appearances, fitted both to charm and to elevate our minds; yet it is in general with a heedless eye that we regard them, and only in particular moments that we are sensible of their power."
I am exited by the Ruskin & Muir descriptions of nature - of water. They are very much like the descriptions I would make in my journal.
I want to make my traveling stand & put my description on the stand & on the advertisement for the stand, document it all very well and present several such set ups (6) at my opening a painting, photograph and description making clear that the art work (reproductions) are less that the real thing. Have the real thing on the roof
Ruskin: art is "less beautiful than the realities" it depicts. `[bracketed:]` [Works ed. E.T. Cook & Alex. Wedderburn 22:222]
We are to look <u>through</u> artworks, not <u>at</u> them.
Ruskin hustles us out of the artworld into the real one.
"Now ~~leap~~ water will leap a little way, it will leap down a weir or over a stone, but it <u>tumbles</u> over a high fall like this; and it is when we have lost the parabolic line, and arrived at the catenary, when we have lost the <u>spring</u> of the fall, and arrived at the <u>plunge</u> of it, that we begin really to feel its weight and wildness. Where water takes its first leap from the top, it is cool, and collected, and uninteresting, and mathematical; but it is when it finds that it has got into a scrape, and has farther to go than it thought, that its character comes out; it is then that it begins to writhe, and twist, and sweep out, zone after zone, in wilder stretching as it falls; and to send down the rocket-like lance pointed, whizzing shafts at its sides, sounding for the bottom [Works Cook 3:553,554.]
I could have written this - the journal of an observant person Leonardo
→ woah ego!

> [!note]- Transcription notes
> Undated; it follows the March 6th entry on the same page. Quotations from George J. Leonard's *Into the Light of Things* (1994), p. 77; the story of Zeuxis painting Helen from the best features of five maidens is usually set in Croton, hence "Croatian" is probably "Crotonian". "Recluse & TA" are probably Wordsworth's *The Recluse* and "Tintern Abbey", whose lines on "what they half create, / And what perceive" are the "most Kantian". Page 43 goes on with Leonard's chapters on Archibald Alison and Ruskin; Ruskin's waterfall passage is from *Modern Painters*. > [!note]- Transcription notes
> Undated. Notes from a talk by Bernice Rose, the MoMA drawings curator, on Joseph Beuys's drawings.
