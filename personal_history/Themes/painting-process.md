## [[1994-07-09_Painting-on-Chapel-York-Garage|Painting on Chapel-York Garage & How Art Transforms Space]]
*1994-07-09 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 58 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p100.jpg|p. 100]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p102.jpg|p. 102]]*

I spent the day, Saturday, painting on top of the Chapel-York Garage. I almost gave up early in the day, exasperated at how quickly the acrylics would dry in the sun. I couldn't get the right color for the shadows a sand colored building—everything was too dark or too green. But stuck with it and changed to a flexible small flat brush, soft like a watercolor brush. Finally, I forgot about the time while I was  painting. That hasn't happened in the longest time. I mixed cerulean blue and cadmium orange to get a light grey for the sandy shadows. Hopefully it will be sunny again tomorrow so I can go finish it.

While I was out there I thought of what in the world someone else would see if they came up. The top of a parking garage—not very exciting. I remembered Harold Bloom telling us about when Archie [A.R.] Ammons took him to the part of the Jersey Shore which inspired *"Corson's Inlet."* Bloom, in his usual exasperated sarcasm, said that his trek through those bleak sand-dunes added nothing to the poem; all he wanted was to find the closest coffee house. No wonder the man's body is about to fail him completely at 64.

This brings up a similarity between painting and poetry. Both transform the space which inspired them. They change this space for the viewer/reader, or present them with something that doesn't need the association to the space, though the artist needed this association to inform the work. This sounds like it could be validation for abstract poetry, but even the most "realistic" painting or photograph changes the space. One cannot escape the mediation of the artist—especially a good artist, who will tend to leave his imprint on his representation. This imprint is recognized and cherished.

## [[1994-08-06_Letting-Go-and-Chris-Loffts-B17|Letting Go in the Mountains & Chris Lofft's B-17]]
*1994-08-06 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p076.jpg|p. 76]]*

I think I am way too hung up on what the artwork looks like. I would be in a much better situation if I had fun making—if I let myself go. I have been getting so hung up because I am feeling like my images are so important, rather than enjoying making them. I should run with the ideas of mixing stuff into the paint.

Eventually I will make something which someone will fixate upon and love the way Chris Lofft fixated on the B-17 model I made. I will never know exactly what he loved about it, or why he picked it up every time he came over even very late after we were in college, but I know that plane was made at the height of my model-building activities.

## [[1994-08-06_Dads-Cards-Katherines-Colors-and-Mountain-Textures|Dad's Cards, Katherine's Colors & Mountain Textures]]
*1994-08-06 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 47 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p078.jpg|p. 78]]*

Dad is not too artistic, but he cares so much. His constant cards break my heart when I think about how hard it is to communicate with him and how much he loves us.

Katherine's card puts the reddish (earth) where one would expect the skyline, and the blue sky is reflected in the white earth of the foreground. The textures and details refine the visual impact this card has on me—I wanted to say "beauty," any more, though I don't know what that means. "Visual effect" is more precise, maybe, but does that imply how much I was moved by this image?

My comment about textures rings ever more true now that I have been trying to paint up in the mountains. I have taken many pictures of texture. It is much harder to throw texture in with all the other things I have to think about when making a painting.

## [[1995-01-10_Charkow-on-Carriage-and-Freshness|Charkow on Carriage and Freshness]]
*1995-01-10 · Final Semester Yale 1995 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1995_p010.jpg|p. 10]]*

1/10/95 Natalie Charkow mentioned an Italian sculpture professor who had been a child prodigy - but was terrible. He could make a likeness of anyone in the class w/ an oval, a cylinder and a wedge - he could ~~get~~ capture their carriage w/ those abstract shapes. Carriage is so important to a likeness - I remember getting excited about recognizing Paul in my 1st notebook and I remember how I captured katherine somehow that 1st time I painted her in Susannah's class - ~~that~~ ~~were~~ ~~[?]~~ was an inspired ~~wonderful~~ moment or was it, I don't remember. I think they are easily equated to times in athletics when you are just <u>on</u>. Everything is in synch. (I had an awesome bike ride yesterday - cleared all the downhills except the last two which I walked rather than risk my luck - and rode the top trail well - what an enjoyable relief.)
Charkow mentioned Auerbach and Matisse as two artists who were willing to change their entire images to maintain some of the initial freshness or immediacy, ~~but~~ she described a movie of Matisse painting where the pose collapses on one arm, then over compensates in the other direction and then gels. I don't know if Auerbach gels - or does he just stop. It seems to me that the best moments are fleeting throughout the series not particularly the final one. - not like Matisse's blue nude series.

> [!note]- Transcription notes
> Natalie Charkow was a sculptor who taught at the Yale School of Art. Auerbach is Frank Auerbach.
