## [[1994-07-26_Hulls-Knife-Edge-Between-Artist-and-Nature|Hull's Knife Edge Between the Painter and Nature]]
*1994-07-26 · Yale, New Haven · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p004.jpg|p. 4]]*

Hull mentioned in class yesterday—about having a relationship w/ nature—no relationship is interesting if one party always wins—the relationship will either end altogether or be terribly unhappy.

The relationship of a painter w/ nature is that between that which is going on in his head—gestures, expression & that which he gathers from nature. If nature always wins (trompe l'oeil [or] other forms of direct copying)—then the work gets boring—as does Abstract expressionism or another form which is all about the artist. I like to try to walk this knife edge, mixing the gesture, small recognitions or reminiscences w/ a careful comparison to what I see before me.

I'm not quite sure what I am saying, my argument kind of unraveled as I wrote it down & I feel that I am left w/ a handful of string.

- Summarizes Auerbach's aim not simply to add another picture to the world (`[true, true...]`), but to make "a new thing for the world" like a new species of living thing—a wish Auerbach calls *grandiloquent* (`[pompous or bombastic eloquence - Amer. ##### Consciousness, Crowds & Radical Redoing (p. 81v)
- **T.S. Eliot / working without self-consciousness:** Cites Auerbach quoting Eliot (*"to be conscious is not to be in time"*) on working without self-consciousness and adapting to whatever room or space is available.
- **Marco's reflection on crowd gesture drawings:**
  > `[I really like the gesture drawings of the crowds I have done—or rather the experience of making them, knowing that it didn't matter if they moved or tried to get up. It's a good feeling knowing that it is impossible to ruin something — any change is a change motivated by something inside you. If that motivation is powerful the change will be good.]`
- **Physical tension, scraping off & Hogarth's *Shrimp Girl*:** Notes Auerbach's account of large pictures demanding weeks of physical tension, repeatedly "finishing" them until crucial, radical decisions happen at the end—scraping off months or years of work and redoing from top to bottom quickly—and admiring William Hogarth's *The Shrimp Girl* as a "frozen instant/moment" where thick, liquid oil paint miraculously preserves a fleeting instant across 200 years.

## [[1994-08-03_Instantaneous-Seeing-Valery-Page-100|Instantaneous Seeing, Valéry & Travel Scars (Page 100)]]
*1994-08-03 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 100 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p039.jpg|p. 39]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p040.jpg|p. 40]]*

A picture or image isn't a complete stop in time. One's eye (the viewer's and the creator's) must move around it, stopping on points of interest, comparison. The initial effect which adumbrates the rest of the viewing is instantaneous.

I liked going to museums w/ Katherine, b/c both of us would walk through at the same speed, stopping when something caught our eye. There is no reason to go through a museum slowly. The image is all about this kind of eye-catching—there isn't too much reason for the artist to go beyond that. I would probably spend as much time in front of a David which he had spent 3 years to paint as I would before a Frank Auerbach whose entire painting had been completed in a couple hours, though it was the culmination of many sittings piled one on the other. Painting made instantaneous, like a photograph—gesture. I am much prouder of any painting I have made than any photograph.

I look around and I see small clips of images, but often it is just what happens to be around me. I think that the circumstances surrounding the creation of an artwork of which Valéry spoke are even more ingrained in a painter's painting than in the poet's poem, because the image is about a connection to the outside world whereas the poem... I suppose this statement depends on what kind of paintings & poems I am talking about: a painting about the artist's inner world is less attached to the place where it is made (I have a tendency to want to attach images to the place: collect the dirt, rocks, or plants of the place to add to the image—paint on a mirror—on the sky). Poems, too, can be about place, or position of the artist before something.

I have always liked the collection of travel scars an object or a person can collect. People embody the place or circumstance from which they come—the carpenter's/workman's hand, the mountain guide's weather-beaten hands & face are far more beautiful to me than the soft white skin of the city or of luxury which has no evidence of having done anything. The letter or the passport which collects stamps or scuff-marks from the places it has been is beautiful, as is the path which collects the footprints of the travelers who make it and maintain it. I like to look at the rock formations which fit together like puzzle pieces because any rocks that don't fit are moved on by the melting snow or gravity. The history of a rock is fascinating and beyond comprehension like Camus clouds in the beginning of *"Le premier homme"*.

- *Sou Uma Rocha*

## [[1994-08-03_Arnheim-on-Death-Instinct-Entropy-and-Gesture|Arnheim: Anti-Entropy, Musical Scale & Double Process of Seeing (Page 101)]]
*1994-08-03 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 101 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p040.jpg|p. 40]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p041.jpg|p. 41]]*

*Art & Visual Perception* quotes & Marco's bracketed commentary (`[...]`):

##### Summary of Arnheim Excerpts (pp. 17, 37, 40, 44)
- **p. 17 (Perceived image as the artwork):** Notes Arnheim's insistence that the perceived image—not physical pigment—is the work of art: perceptual forces pulling an unbalanced disk `[center-square sketch]` are artistically real even if physically "illusory."
- **p. 37 (Critique of Freud's death instinct & economy principle):** Summarizes Arnheim's rejection of Freud's claim that organisms strive toward inorganic rest—arguing instead that healthy human life fulfills itself in doing, moving, growing, and creating, waging an uphill battle against entropy.
  - Marco's marginal additions inside brackets:
    > `[Freud] assigned a fundamental role to the "death instinct,"... Man is lazy by nature [some men maybe. But maybe the most active find that life is easiest in activity. It is much easier for the marathoner to run 25 miles if his car breaks down, than to sit by the road and try to flag someone down]`
    > `[grass and trees are the only things which keep the rocks and dirt from falling off a mountain. They have to fight against the wind, sun, rain, melting snow & freezing ice which all loosen the rocks & dirt & send them down, closer to the center of gravity of the planet.]` — p. 37.
- **p. 40 (Music & Image — composition):** Compares the musical scale and framed visual composition in both combining rising intensity from base to peak with symmetry returning to a base/rest.
- **p. 44 (Gesture & caricature):** Summarizes Arnheim on seeing essential structural features (blueness of sky, curve of swan's neck, simple face strokes, Köhler's chimpanzees startled by button eyes, caricature catching a person's likeness through few lines). Closes with Marco's added note:
  > `- making art is the double process of seeing; then of making others see.` — p. 44.
