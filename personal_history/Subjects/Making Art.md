## [[1994-06-06_Goodbye-to-Katherine|Katherine Leaves & Emotional Restraint]]
*1994-06-06 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 29 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p048.jpg|p. 48]]*

Well, Katherine left today. I choked as I tried to say "Bye, Babe." I came so close to crying as I walked back to Daria's from the curb, from where Lisa's blue station wagon pulled Katherine's ever-smiling face away from me. I can't remember ever having felt so natural and happy with someone. 

*(Later annotation: That kind of statement is always problematic—I wonder if it will sound like the 10th grader who wrote about Nira to me someday.)*

I always wonder why I never cry. Do I rationalize my way out of it, or am I stunted emotionally? If I am deprived of the crying extreme, I must also be kept from extreme happiness. I know I don't explode into the exuberant, unrestrained laughter that Katherine does. I love that about her; she is often as emotionally free as a 5-year-old, her voice sliding through octaves and registers over which she has as little control as her toddler counterpart.

I want to start to write regularly, so that I may work out the kinks and awkwardnesses of transcribing my thoughts. I want to try to get this process as free as Katherine's laughter, and as that loose painting stroke she so appreciates.

## [[1994-06-07_Painting-Idea-Infinite-Reflection|Painting Idea: Infinite Reflection]]
*1994-06-07 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 31 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p050.jpg|p. 50]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p052.jpg|p. 52]]*

Onto the most exciting thoughts I had today: I was picturing a painting of the artist's portrait with an eye of the subject blown up on the curved reflecting surface—or two such paintings: one of the artist's reflection, the second of the artist's eye with the reflection of the subject. If these two paintings were set up facing each other in a viewing space at a distance appropriate to their size, the viewer could become the same size as the reflections and could thus be associated with either the artist or the subject. He would somehow be stepping into the imaginary rays of reflection between the paintings.

It belongs to the situation which would occur in the event of a self-portrait: a double-sided mirror could be placed between the two eyes to maximize the size of the reflection. Would it be possible to repeat this infinite mirroring even if the viewer couldn't actually see it—reduced onto a slide or microfilm?

## [[1994-06-27_Time-in-Text-vs-Space-in-Painting|Time in Text vs. Space in Painting]]
*1994-06-27 · Yale, New Haven · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p046.jpg|p. 46]]*

The viewing of a painting happens in a particular place, though its viewing is not through time like the processing of a text. The appreciation of a text is all about time—the buildup—so often it is hard to pinpoint the passage that moved you because to be moved, you had to have been put in a certain mood by the text which preceded it. If you try to return to that passage, it no longer has the same effect because you no longer have the same baggage, or you have added to it by reading beyond the quote.

If one tries to pinpoint the exact part of a painting one likes, this too is impossible because the instant that the part is removed from the whole, it is no longer the same place—it is removed, transported to another space, and its meaning changes (synecdoche).

## [[1994-07-09_Painting-on-Chapel-York-Garage|Painting on Chapel-York Garage & How Art Transforms Space]]
*1994-07-09 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 58 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p100.jpg|p. 100]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p102.jpg|p. 102]]*

I spent the day, Saturday, painting on top of the Chapel-York Garage. I almost gave up early in the day, exasperated at how quickly the acrylics would dry in the sun. I couldn't get the right color for the shadows a sand colored building—everything was too dark or too green. But stuck with it and changed to a flexible small flat brush, soft like a watercolor brush. Finally, I forgot about the time while I was  painting. That hasn't happened in the longest time. I mixed cerulean blue and cadmium orange to get a light grey for the sandy shadows. Hopefully it will be sunny again tomorrow so I can go finish it.

While I was out there I thought of what in the world someone else would see if they came up. The top of a parking garage—not very exciting. I remembered Harold Bloom telling us about when Archie [A.R.] Ammons took him to the part of the Jersey Shore which inspired *"Corson's Inlet."* Bloom, in his usual exasperated sarcasm, said that his trek through those bleak sand-dunes added nothing to the poem; all he wanted was to find the closest coffee house. No wonder the man's body is about to fail him completely at 64.

This brings up a similarity between painting and poetry. Both transform the space which inspired them. They change this space for the viewer/reader, or present them with something that doesn't need the association to the space, though the artist needed this association to inform the work. This sounds like it could be validation for abstract poetry, but even the most "realistic" painting or photograph changes the space. One cannot escape the mediation of the artist—especially a good artist, who will tend to leave his imprint on his representation. This imprint is recognized and cherished.

## [[1994-07-13_Ways-to-Make-a-Space-Feel-Huge|Ways to Make a Space Feel Huge]]
*1994-07-13 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 61 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p104.jpg|p. 104]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p105.jpg|p. 105]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p106.jpg|p. 106]]*

* **Cut off the viewer's body:** Just the head [visible]—smaller makes the space look bigger.
* **Skylights & Illusion of Space:** Putting a window high up near the ceiling in my room, painting the sky on the wall behind the window.
* **Water-Plane Effect:** Like walking on water in I.M. Pei's Louvre fountains—if you look across, you can't see the grooves, it looks like a flat plane, yet people's heads are passing through this plane.
* **Casement Window & Grid Depth:** A window with a grid where, rather than erase an inch of painting everywhere the lattice covers it, I would split the painting—emulating a stereoscopic effect. Because with both eyes you can see around a bar or a pole when you focus beyond it, each eye sees a little on each side and the pole effectively vanishes.
* **Concealed Fluorescent Mirror:** Put a mirror in front of a fluorescent light that can't be seen; the light comes around the mirror, and the mirror looks suspended in mid-air.

## [[1994-07-15_The-Chopin-Nocturne-Room|Installation Idea: The Chopin Nocturne Room]]
*1994-07-15 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 65 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p110.jpg|p. 110]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p112.jpg|p. 112]]*

My next idea was to create a piece for the Chopin Nocturne:
* **Entrance:** You enter a room and have to go around a wall upon which I have written a poem about my experience of going to sleep with this music—my mother would play it to calm me down when I was a baby.
* **Architecture:** A ramp leads you down into a dark room with a low ceiling and mattresses or cushions on the ground.
* **Lighting & Sound:** Venetian blinds with daylight-like lights behind them to simulate windows so the light disperses in stripes, lights diffusing gently along the walls, and hidden speakers.
* **Threshold:** A secret passageway—something you could pass right by without noticing, with no door, just a subtle light above a doorway. It doesn't scream *ENTRANCE*, just a place you notice and sneak into to find magic: *"Am I supposed to be coming back here? Is this off-limits?"* Inside, an unobstructed lifeline back out which you choose not to take.

## [[1994-07-22_Michael-Heizer-vs-James-Turrell|Michael Heizer vs. James Turrell]]
*1994-07-22 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 74 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p129.jpg|p. 129]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p130.jpg|p. 130]]*

There is something I don't like about Michael Heizer's constructions—his holes and objects. He doesn't have a respect for the land or the place where he is building *Double Negative* (exploding huge areas of earth). I am sure that if I were to go visit that work, I would prefer a view further down the canyon where no one could imagine the object, so I could enjoy nature for its own sake.

Count Panza described how the approach to a Greek temple purposely limits your view so that when you finally get to the temple, you turn around (or go beyond it) and are confronted by the view which inspired its construction. Turrell seems in line with this type of effect—his Roden Crater is not so much about leaving his mark on the landscape as it is about bringing the viewer to experience the landscape's magical properties. Heizer is scarring the landscape, leaving his mark like a circle in the desert made with a motorcycle track.

I remember a quote by action/adventure photographer Galen Rowell, something like: *"Go into nature, leave it untouched, return with nothing"*—only taking a fraction of a second's worth of light. As Calvino wrote in *Invisible Cities*: *"Memories' images, once they are fixed in words, are erased."*

## [[1994-08-03_Instantaneous-Seeing-Valery-Page-100|Instantaneous Seeing, Valéry & Travel Scars (Page 100)]]

A picture or image isn't a complete stop in time. One's eye (the viewer's and the creator's) must move around it, stopping on points of interest, comparison. The initial effect which adumbrates the rest of the viewing is instantaneous.

I liked going to museums w/ Katherine, b/c both of us would walk through at the same speed, stopping when something caught our eye. There is no reason to go through a museum slowly. The image is all about this kind of eye-catching—there isn't too much reason for the artist to go beyond that. I would probably spend as much time in front of a David which he had spent 3 years to paint as I would before a Frank Auerbach whose entire painting had been completed in a couple hours, though it was the culmination of many sittings piled one on the other. I look around and I see small clips of images, but often it is just what happens to be around me. I have always liked the collection of travel scars an object or a person can collect. People embody the place or circumstance from which they come—the carpenter's/workman's hand, the mountain guide's weather-beaten hands & face are far more beautiful to me than the soft white skin of the city or of luxury which has no evidence of having done anything. The letter or the passport which collects stamps or scuff-marks from the places it has been is beautiful, as is the path which collects the footprints of the travelers who make it and maintain it. I like to look at the rock formations which fit together like puzzle pieces because any rocks that don't fit are moved on by the melting snow or gravity. The history of a rock is fascinating and beyond comprehension like Camus clouds in the beginning of *"Le premier homme"*.

I am controlling his viewing & making him see, he does not see till he shines his light on an object. —
