## [[1992-12-24_The-Christian-Paradox|The Christian Paradox]]
*1992-12-24 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 1 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p003.jpg|p. 3]]*

Most educated Christians don't believe in the physical reality of Christ as God's son, which is fine. The religion still holds together on the tenet that it is spreading the word of the Lord—the message in the stories. I ask: why do we all have to go through the elaborate charade of convincing each other that we believe, when in fact we don't? You might say that by forcing everyone to follow the word, you get fringe people to do things—good things—which they would not have done had there not been this elaborate charade. 

I'm glad I do know something of these stories, so that the church is a common reference frame for much of the Western world. The problems I have with most church services are the same that I have with a lot of mass culture in general.

## [[1994-06-07_Writing-as-a-Companion-for-Memory|Writing as a Companion for Memory]]
*1994-06-07 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 30 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p049.jpg|p. 49]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p050.jpg|p. 50]]*

Well, this is my second straight day of recording my thoughts. I feel like this book has become a sort of companion for my brain. It records the flotsam and jetsam of my thoughts that would otherwise disappear into the ocean of forgotten things. When these thoughts are collected and kept, they can be built upon and stimulate other thoughts. I can then start the rewriting, revising, rethinking which Valéry claimed was essential to making a work of art. There is no one author, he claimed, because the author returns to the work (latent as a closed book) and revises it. The work cannot be isolated from the ongoing flow of the author's mind and life.

I wonder if this process will be similar to what I read in *The Idea of Music* about some Greek philosopher [Socrates] who opposed writing things down because that would mean one wouldn't have to remember—writing, he said, was the first step toward weakening one's memory. I wonder if the narrator of *The Name of the Rose* could actually remember all the lists and details which he restates in his retelling of conversations he witnessed, or if these lists were a means of scholastic pyrotechnics, which Eco says can lead to endless contemplation.

The Quillian model: a mass of nodes interconnected by different types of associative links. For the meaning of *[continues onto yellow sticky note, left]*: every lexeme, memory should contain a node which has as its 'patriarch' the term to be defined here called *type*. The configuration of the meaning of the lexeme is given by the multiplicity of its links with several tokens each of which becomes, in its turn, a *type B*. Type B is the patriarch of a new configuration.... "The overall structure of the complete memory forms an enormous aggregation of planes, each consisting entirely of token nodes except for its 'head' node." This model therefore anticipates the definition of every sign, thanks to its interconnection with the universe of all other signs that function as its interpretants, each of which is ready to become *[continues onto yellow sticky note, right]* a sign interpreted by all the others. The model, in its complexity, is based on a process of unlimited semiosis. Starting with a sign that is considered a 'type', one can retrace, from the center to the extreme periphery, the entire universe of cultural units. Each of these can in turn become the center and generate infinite peripheries.

## [[1994-06-27_Time-in-Text-vs-Space-in-Painting|Time in Text vs. Space in Painting]]
*1994-06-27 · Yale, New Haven · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p046.jpg|p. 46]]*

The viewing of a painting happens in a particular place, though its viewing is not through time like the processing of a text. The appreciation of a text is all about time—the buildup—so often it is hard to pinpoint the passage that moved you because to be moved, you had to have been put in a certain mood by the text which preceded it. If you try to return to that passage, it no longer has the same effect because you no longer have the same baggage, or you have added to it by reading beyond the quote.

If one tries to pinpoint the exact part of a painting one likes, this too is impossible because the instant that the part is removed from the whole, it is no longer the same place—it is removed, transported to another space, and its meaning changes (synecdoche).

## [[1994-07-09_Painting-on-Chapel-York-Garage|Painting on Chapel-York Garage & How Art Transforms Space]]
*1994-07-09 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 58 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p100.jpg|p. 100]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p102.jpg|p. 102]]*

I spent the day, Saturday, painting on top of the Chapel-York Garage. I almost gave up early in the day, exasperated at how quickly the acrylics would dry in the sun. I couldn't get the right color for the shadows a sand colored building—everything was too dark or too green. But stuck with it and changed to a flexible small flat brush, soft like a watercolor brush. Finally, I forgot about the time while I was  painting. That hasn't happened in the longest time. I mixed cerulean blue and cadmium orange to get a light grey for the sandy shadows. Hopefully it will be sunny again tomorrow so I can go finish it.

While I was out there I thought of what in the world someone else would see if they came up. The top of a parking garage—not very exciting. I remembered Harold Bloom telling us about when Archie [A.R.] Ammons took him to the part of the Jersey Shore which inspired *"Corson's Inlet."* Bloom, in his usual exasperated sarcasm, said that his trek through those bleak sand-dunes added nothing to the poem; all he wanted was to find the closest coffee house. No wonder the man's body is about to fail him completely at 64.

This brings up a similarity between painting and poetry. Both transform the space which inspired them. They change this space for the viewer/reader, or present them with something that doesn't need the association to the space, though the artist needed this association to inform the work. This sounds like it could be validation for abstract poetry, but even the most "realistic" painting or photograph changes the space. One cannot escape the mediation of the artist—especially a good artist, who will tend to leave his imprint on his representation. This imprint is recognized and cherished.

##### Key Concepts Summarized from Eco
- **Semiotic vs. metasemiotic judgments:** Codes do not only produce tautological utterances; they let us form metasemiotic judgments that invent new semantic couplings and call the code itself into question. Every metaphor traces back to a subjacent chain of metonymic connections (even in everyday messages and philosophy, whenever culture must name something unassimilated).
- **Joyce, *Finnegans Wake*, and Picasso (p. 83):** *Finnegans Wake* exemplifies unlimited semiosis through phonetic contiguity preceding semantic similitude. Summarizes Eco on *meander tale* / *Neanderthal* (phonetic + semantic resonance vs. "empty modern art" `'Oh yeah, so?'`) and Picasso's bicycle seat + handlebars forming a bull's head (`"becomes rewarding or does it become trite?"`), plus Eco's multidimensional *Ersatz* field diagram (*Graph p. 259*).

Contrasts Dante's *"dark wood"*, Achillini's fire-heat-sweat factual comparisons, Galileo's condemnation for *semiotic* rather than logical contradiction with Scripture, and Joyce's *etyma + atom = 'abnihilation of the ethym'* ("before nuclear fission").

## [[1994-07-30_Bateson-Theory-of-Play-and-Fantasy|Notes on Semiotics: Bateson's Theory of Play & Fantasy (Page 90)]]
*1994-07-30 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 90 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p024.jpg|p. 24]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p025.jpg|p. 25]]*

**Notes on Semiotics: Bateson, *Theory of Play & Fantasy***

##### Summary of Bateson Excerpts
- Contrasts involuntary animal mood-signs (sexual odor) with human cosmetics and perfumes worn voluntarily, which nevertheless produce automatic/auto-suggestive effects even on the wearer; links this evolutionary threshold—eating of the Tree of Knowledge to discover that *signals are signals*—to human language, empathy, identification, and multiple levels of abstraction.
