## [[1994-06-27_Mental-Range-DC-to-New-Haven-and-Atomic-Scale|Mental Range, D.C. to New Haven & Atomic Scale]]
*1994-06-27 · Yale, New Haven · scan p. 34*

...change in [...] as any sharpness; [...] allowed to occur over a wider range than mental lines can keep:

- Mental range must frame off a section of the world/Universe. It cannot grasp it all. If one tries to conceive of the trip from D.C. to New Haven, one can recall particular images of rest stops, toll booths, bridges, but in no way can remember each sequence; instead one has to resort to the image of a map or a diagram. The range is too great—it must be simplified to fit w/in the scope of what can be understood.

The same goes for imagining the atoms that make up the molecules in our hand, or this pen which is recording my thoughts. I can start w/ the atom or cell, but there is a large bridge to be gapped before I can imagine the solidity of my hand, or the different cells which make up the muscle, bone, skin etc. Even from the atom to the cell, one diagram to the other, from the nucleus w/ e⁻'s to any solid is a conceptual leap too great for the limited range of our conceptions.

## [[1994-06-27_Gibrans-Mountain-and-The-Problem-of-Quoting|Gibran's Mountain & The Problem of Quoting]]
*1994-06-27 · Yale, New Haven · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p044.jpg|p. 44]]*

From Khalil Gibran (which I quoted from Cara's yearbook page):
> *"When you part from your friend, you grieve not; for that which you love most in him may be clearer in his absence, as the mountain to the climber is from the plain."*

Gibran has reversed what I normally think: the plain is clearer from the mountain. Even the climber must spend more time at his home than on the mountain, and I don't know if the mountain really becomes clearer—it becomes conceivable as a block rather than as a series of images of hand- and footholds, or of paths through changing territory. The view of the city from the plane (not plain) does not add anything to the city-goer's knowledge of the flowers in the shop or the trash in the drain; rather, it creates a map where these events can be put in a relationship.

## [[1994-07-22_Michael-Heizer-vs-James-Turrell|Michael Heizer vs. James Turrell]]
*1994-07-22 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 74 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p129.jpg|p. 129]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p130.jpg|p. 130]]*

There is something I don't like about Michael Heizer's constructions—his holes and objects. He doesn't have a respect for the land or the place where he is building *Double Negative* (exploding huge areas of earth). I am sure that if I were to go visit that work, I would prefer a view further down the canyon where no one could imagine the object, so I could enjoy nature for its own sake.

Count Panza described how the approach to a Greek temple purposely limits your view so that when you finally get to the temple, you turn around (or go beyond it) and are confronted by the view which inspired its construction. Turrell seems in line with this type of effect—his Roden Crater is not so much about leaving his mark on the landscape as it is about bringing the viewer to experience the landscape's magical properties. Heizer is scarring the landscape, leaving his mark like a circle in the desert made with a motorcycle track.

I remember a quote by action/adventure photographer Galen Rowell, something like: *"Go into nature, leave it untouched, return with nothing"*—only taking a fraction of a second's worth of light. As Calvino wrote in *Invisible Cities*: *"Memories' images, once they are fixed in words, are erased."*

## [[1994-07-27_Dialogues-with-Nature-Smoke-Fly-and-Squirrels|Dialogues with Nature: Smoke, Fly & Squirrels (7/27)]]
*1994-07-27 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 80 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p004.jpg|p. 4]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p005.jpg|p. 5]]*

**7/27** — I remember several dialogues with nature:

I was lying on my back in the Saybrook grass court yard listening to Dvořák's cello concerto. There was a flume of smoke rising from the dark roof, illuminated by the moon which was full, and close behind it. The plume seemed to dance as it rose. It would go from heavy to almost non-existent. The Music was happening inside my head between the two earpieces of my headphones & the plume was outside in the cool night. There were times when the plume seemed to react to the music I was listening to; almost vanish with a decrescendo or dance wildly in a more vigorous part. Those moments of syncrony were wonderful and random, and wonderful for being random.

I had the same sort of feeling watching a fly in my room. For some reason flies circle around in the very center of the room where a string hangs down from the ceiling light. Its motions were generally much quicker than the smoke's had been, but it too seemed to get some kind of rhythm or pattern from the music.

I will never forget a wonderful day at the end of exams when I went for a long run & lay down under a chestnut tree near the Divinity school. Two squirrels were playing or flirting near me & at times it seemed I understood what they were communicating to each other and they felt my presence in their world.

I think it is interesting to think of these interior/exterior running dialogues. When are they synchronized? Can they be synchronized? or are we just experiencing random connection patterns which happen to seem similar?

## [[1994-07-29_Flying-Over-Clouds-Diebenkorn-Turrell-and-Earth-Flatness|Flying Washington–NY: Clouds, Diebenkorn, Turrell & Earth's Flatness (Page 88)]]
*1994-07-29 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 88 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p019.jpg|p. 19]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p020.jpg|p. 20]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p021.jpg|p. 21]]*

Flying from Washington to NY I had a window seat. It had been storming and there were many clouds of every shape and kind and color in the sky. It was really quite beautiful. The plane was flying inside these strips. There were thin black strips (pollution?—I thought they were kind of beautiful), puffy cottonny, mountainous ones and large flat scrim like ones. The earth was mostly a dark kind of greyed out purple color because for the most part someone from the ground would see complete cloud cover, he could not see the differentiation that we could from our privaledged, airborne position. We would however often see breaks in the clouds above us and the blue sunny sky (Is it comforting to think that no matter how bad the weather, it is always a sunny day if you could just fly high enough?)

I remember Hollander mentioning that he was surprised that painters or artists had not reacted to views or visual stimulation which has been made possible by technological advances which allow humans all sorts of different views of the world. I am happy to mention that two of the artists about whom I have been most interested in the recent past, Diebenkorn and Turrell, both claim the visual experience of flying as a direct influence to their work. Diebenkorn vividly remembered his first plane flight, from New Mexico to the Bay Area (I believe) and the evidence can be seen in some of the later Berkley abstractions (50's before he switched to figuration) like the yellow one with black lines which Katherine copied. Turrell openly claims that his job as a small airplane pilot which has given him frequent views of the split heaven and earth is a direct influence to his pieces. He also mentions the multiple colors of the changing sunlight at twilight. He flew up and down the flat—and I think this is important—regions of Eastern California & the West to search for Roden Crater—he seeks to make a sculpture which directly confronts the viewer with a feeling of ½ heavens ½ earth.

> *[Diagram, top of p. 88]:* Cross-section of `Crater ->` bowl with a tiny standing viewer at the bottom and dotted sightlines forming a huge circle with the caption: `optical effect the sky "curves" creates a sphere w/ the earth. new horizon`.

I think it may be important that Diebenkorn doesn't admit to being openly influenced by anything `[above crossed-out: I thought of Diebenkorn's abstractions which critics have connected to what]`. He even would attack paintings, and "ruin" them to try to remove elements of figuration or representation which had "appeared" in them. Eventually, he stopped ruining pictures like that and would leave these elements as in *"Motorcycle Crash"* and *"The Archer"*. Diebenkorn doesn't want to think consciously about what goes into his abstractions and why. It is the critics who see evidences of the Ocean Park Architecture and landscape in the Ocean Park Series. I don't know if Diebenkorn is being completely honest about not thinking but I don't think one can completely eliminate the evidence of previous visual stimulae, a painter "names" visually even if he does not "name" things verbally. `[p. 40 *] [see the Wilson quote about memory & stars]`

Flying the other day, I was thinking about how flat the landscape really is, human beings can't do anything yet to affect this phenomena (word choice?—I don't have a definite definition in mind, though I know a thorough definition would be several pages long.) This flatness is similar to a painting's flatness—there is only so thick a painting can get—Frank Stella's (3ft?), Anselm Keiffer (1.5ft?) and likewise for the landscape, the Himalayan mountain range is the largest rupture in this flatness, 28,000m = 28km how does this compare to 40,000 km (diameter or circumference) of the earth. The earth is flatter than the paintings I have mentioned. Thus the earth's surface as seen from the sky could be the perfect subject matter for an abstract painting and the obvious reason why someone like Diebenkorn, who is somehow more true to his own visual experience than other painters who have painted abstractly, comes to make paintings reminiscent of such a landscape, which he admits having been excited by at the time. He cannot shake himself from the visual baggage which he is carrying and representing it. He is representing an image which exists only in his head, but I believe he is representing it & it does exist. The same sort of thing happens w/ Rothko over Still or Newman. People claim to see the sunset or the horizon or the sky in his works. He aims at a specific visual effect, his paint colors & tones are so carefully balanced. When he achieves the effect he desires that could very well be when the painting has matched an image he has, no matter how vague, of a large visual effect like a sunset, or the horizon.

## [[1994-07-31_Mortality-Andre-Diard-and-Tignes|Mortality, André Diard & Summers in Tignes]]
*1994-07-31 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · scan [[attachments/Screenshot_2026-11-18_at_10.26.11_AM.png|Screenshot_2026-11-18_at_10.26.11_AM]]*

- This journal and any art possibly can be seen as a fight against death. I want to maximize my time before death—do the most w/ my life & thoughts, & possibly leave something worthwhile behind.

**7/31/94**  
André Diard died this winter. He was a key figure in the town of Tignes and a fixed memory since he ran the rental of our apartment at Les Chaudes Almes. I will always remember being on the American team golfing into the inflatable baby pools anchored off the dock of Le Terrachu *(name of the almost lost dialect of the region)*. I remember how sad we were when the Diards gave up running Le Terrachu. All the wonderful nights we had spent there (and delicious dinners we had been served) would be a memory. Our summers were never quite the same, but we would still see the Diards now and then and Mr. Diard would ring out w/ *"Salut les Américains"* and always have a chum(b)ly relationship w/ Richie.

As we were coming up the stairs just now I mentioned my plane flight last summer w/ Laurent's Dad (Mr. Pèremezelle(?)). *"I never went up w/ André,"* Mom said—Mr. Diard took passengers for sight-tours in his small 4-seater. *"That is something I will always regret..."*

## [[1994-08-03_Instantaneous-Seeing-Valery-Page-100|Instantaneous Seeing, Valéry & Travel Scars (Page 100)]]
*1994-08-03 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 100 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p039.jpg|p. 39]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p040.jpg|p. 40]]*

A picture or image isn't a complete stop in time. One's eye (the viewer's and the creator's) must move around it, stopping on points of interest, comparison. The initial effect which adumbrates the rest of the viewing is instantaneous.

I liked going to museums w/ Katherine, b/c both of us would walk through at the same speed, stopping when something caught our eye. There is no reason to go through a museum slowly. The image is all about this kind of eye-catching—there isn't too much reason for the artist to go beyond that. I would probably spend as much time in front of a David which he had spent 3 years to paint as I would before a Frank Auerbach whose entire painting had been completed in a couple hours, though it was the culmination of many sittings piled one on the other. Painting made instantaneous, like a photograph—gesture. I am much prouder of any painting I have made than any photograph.

I look around and I see small clips of images, but often it is just what happens to be around me. I think that the circumstances surrounding the creation of an artwork of which Valéry spoke are even more ingrained in a painter's painting than in the poet's poem, because the image is about a connection to the outside world whereas the poem... I suppose this statement depends on what kind of paintings & poems I am talking about: a painting about the artist's inner world is less attached to the place where it is made (I have a tendency to want to attach images to the place: collect the dirt, rocks, or plants of the place to add to the image—paint on a mirror—on the sky). Poems, too, can be about place, or position of the artist before something.

I have always liked the collection of travel scars an object or a person can collect. People embody the place or circumstance from which they come—the carpenter's/workman's hand, the mountain guide's weather-beaten hands & face are far more beautiful to me than the soft white skin of the city or of luxury which has no evidence of having done anything. The letter or the passport which collects stamps or scuff-marks from the places it has been is beautiful, as is the path which collects the footprints of the travelers who make it and maintain it. I like to look at the rock formations which fit together like puzzle pieces because any rocks that don't fit are moved on by the melting snow or gravity. The history of a rock is fascinating and beyond comprehension like Camus clouds in the beginning of *"Le premier homme"*.

- *Sou Uma Rocha*

## [[1994-08-12_Mont-Pourri-Climb-and-Flight-Over-Mont-Blanc|On the 4,000-Meter Crest & Flight Over Mont Blanc]]
*1994-08-12 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 69 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p115.jpg|p. 115]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p116.jpg|p. 116]]*

The climb was excruciating. Our guide was determined to get us, his group, up the mountain before another *cordée* that had started at about the same time. I was walking with hot dogs [crampons?]. I was so tired. The last stretch was along a knife-like crest of ice; we felt the wind for the first time, ready to blow us off. Beyond my fatigue, I was conscious only of the incredible distance below on either side of our trail of footsteps.

The top was one of the most incredible memories I have. The summit was no bigger than a queen-sized bed. We could not see any of the mountain we had just climbed, but could see for miles in every direction. This was the highest altitude I had ever been—we were just below the ceiling of 4,000 meters, my dad said. Another 50 meters and we would be over. I remember wanting to reach up or jump to get just those couple meters higher. The ceiling was right there above us.

Last summer I had another incredible experience: Laurent invited me and Julien to go on a flight with his dad. He gave us a tour of the Massif du Mont Blanc. From the air, you can see the folds in the layers of earth that make up the land we live on. The mountains and their valleys are tears through this terrain where its history of evolution can be clearly seen. Being inside that tiny plane, tossed by every turbulence caused by the mountains—irritations in the smooth passage of the layers of wind—one of the most exciting moments for me was recognizing the famous climbs which I had studied in the climbing guides: Grand Dru, Petit Dru, Dent du Géant. There is something magical about looking at these vertical walls and imagining the climber's path.

I think what "grosses me out" about the girls who would flock to preppy Yale singers is that they were deluded by an image, a dream or hope, without knowing the person inside. All this style is such a fad.
