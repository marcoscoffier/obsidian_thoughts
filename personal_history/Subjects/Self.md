## [[1992-12-24_The-Christian-Paradox|The Christian Paradox]]
*1992-12-24 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 1 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p003.jpg|p. 3]]*

Most educated Christians don't believe in the physical reality of Christ as God's son, which is fine. The religion still holds together on the tenet that it is spreading the word of the Lord—the message in the stories. I ask: why do we all have to go through the elaborate charade of convincing each other that we believe, when in fact we don't? You might say that by forcing everyone to follow the word, you get fringe people to do things—good things—which they would not have done had there not been this elaborate charade. 

I'm glad I do know something of these stories, so that the church is a common reference frame for much of the Western world. The problems I have with most church services are the same that I have with a lot of mass culture in general.

## [[1994-06-07_Writing-as-a-Companion-for-Memory|Writing as a Companion for Memory]]
*1994-06-07 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 30 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p049.jpg|p. 49]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p050.jpg|p. 50]]*

Well, this is my second straight day of recording my thoughts. I feel like this book has become a sort of companion for my brain. It records the flotsam and jetsam of my thoughts that would otherwise disappear into the ocean of forgotten things. When these thoughts are collected and kept, they can be built upon and stimulate other thoughts. I can then start the rewriting, revising, rethinking which Valéry claimed was essential to making a work of art. There is no one author, he claimed, because the author returns to the work (latent as a closed book) and revises it. The work cannot be isolated from the ongoing flow of the author's mind and life.

I wonder if this process will be similar to what I read in *The Idea of Music* about some Greek philosopher [Socrates] who opposed writing things down because that would mean one wouldn't have to remember—writing, he said, was the first step toward weakening one's memory. I wonder if the narrator of *The Name of the Rose* could actually remember all the lists and details which he restates in his retelling of conversations he witnessed, or if these lists were a means of scholastic pyrotechnics, which Eco says can lead to endless contemplation.

## [[1994-07-22_Michael-Heizer-vs-James-Turrell|Michael Heizer vs. James Turrell]]
*1994-07-22 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 74 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p129.jpg|p. 129]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p130.jpg|p. 130]]*

There is something I don't like about Michael Heizer's constructions—his holes and objects. He doesn't have a respect for the land or the place where he is building *Double Negative* (exploding huge areas of earth). I am sure that if I were to go visit that work, I would prefer a view further down the canyon where no one could imagine the object, so I could enjoy nature for its own sake.

Count Panza described how the approach to a Greek temple purposely limits your view so that when you finally get to the temple, you turn around (or go beyond it) and are confronted by the view which inspired its construction. Turrell seems in line with this type of effect—his Roden Crater is not so much about leaving his mark on the landscape as it is about bringing the viewer to experience the landscape's magical properties. Heizer is scarring the landscape, leaving his mark like a circle in the desert made with a motorcycle track.

I remember a quote by action/adventure photographer Galen Rowell, something like: *"Go into nature, leave it untouched, return with nothing"*—only taking a fraction of a second's worth of light. As Calvino wrote in *Invisible Cities*: *"Memories' images, once they are fixed in words, are erased."*

## [[1994-07-27_Dialogues-with-Nature-Smoke-Fly-and-Squirrels|Dialogues with Nature: Smoke, Fly & Squirrels (7/27)]]
*1994-07-27 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 80 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p004.jpg|p. 4]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p005.jpg|p. 5]]*

**7/27** — I remember several dialogues with nature:

I was lying on my back in the Saybrook grass court yard listening to Dvořák's cello concerto. There was a flume of smoke rising from the dark roof, illuminated by the moon which was full, and close behind it. The plume seemed to dance as it rose. It would go from heavy to almost non-existent. The Music was happening inside my head between the two earpieces of my headphones & the plume was outside in the cool night. There were times when the plume seemed to react to the music I was listening to; almost vanish with a decrescendo or dance wildly in a more vigorous part. Those moments of syncrony were wonderful and random, and wonderful for being random.

I had the same sort of feeling watching a fly in my room. For some reason flies circle around in the very center of the room where a string hangs down from the ceiling light. Its motions were generally much quicker than the smoke's had been, but it too seemed to get some kind of rhythm or pattern from the music.

I will never forget a wonderful day at the end of exams when I went for a long run & lay down under a chestnut tree near the Divinity school. Two squirrels were playing or flirting near me & at times it seemed I understood what they were communicating to each other and they felt my presence in their world.

I think it is interesting to think of these interior/exterior running dialogues. When are they synchronized? Can they be synchronized? or are we just experiencing random connection patterns which happen to seem similar?

## [[1994-08-03_Instantaneous-Seeing-Valery-Page-100|Instantaneous Seeing, Valéry & Travel Scars (Page 100)]]
*1994-08-03 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 100 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p039.jpg|p. 39]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p040.jpg|p. 40]]*

A picture or image isn't a complete stop in time. One's eye (the viewer's and the creator's) must move around it, stopping on points of interest, comparison. The initial effect which adumbrates the rest of the viewing is instantaneous.

I liked going to museums w/ Katherine, b/c both of us would walk through at the same speed, stopping when something caught our eye. There is no reason to go through a museum slowly. The image is all about this kind of eye-catching—there isn't too much reason for the artist to go beyond that. I would probably spend as much time in front of a David which he had spent 3 years to paint as I would before a Frank Auerbach whose entire painting had been completed in a couple hours, though it was the culmination of many sittings piled one on the other. Painting made instantaneous, like a photograph—gesture. I am much prouder of any painting I have made than any photograph.

I look around and I see small clips of images, but often it is just what happens to be around me. I think that the circumstances surrounding the creation of an artwork of which Valéry spoke are even more ingrained in a painter's painting than in the poet's poem, because the image is about a connection to the outside world whereas the poem... I suppose this statement depends on what kind of paintings & poems I am talking about: a painting about the artist's inner world is less attached to the place where it is made (I have a tendency to want to attach images to the place: collect the dirt, rocks, or plants of the place to add to the image—paint on a mirror—on the sky). Poems, too, can be about place, or position of the artist before something.

I have always liked the collection of travel scars an object or a person can collect. People embody the place or circumstance from which they come—the carpenter's/workman's hand, the mountain guide's weather-beaten hands & face are far more beautiful to me than the soft white skin of the city or of luxury which has no evidence of having done anything. The letter or the passport which collects stamps or scuff-marks from the places it has been is beautiful, as is the path which collects the footprints of the travelers who make it and maintain it. I like to look at the rock formations which fit together like puzzle pieces because any rocks that don't fit are moved on by the melting snow or gravity. The history of a rock is fascinating and beyond comprehension like Camus clouds in the beginning of *"Le premier homme"*.

- *Sou Uma Rocha*

## [[1994-08-12_Mont-Pourri-Climb-and-Flight-Over-Mont-Blanc|On the 4,000-Meter Crest & Flight Over Mont Blanc]]
*1994-08-12 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 69 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p115.jpg|p. 115]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p116.jpg|p. 116]]*

The climb was excruciating. Our guide was determined to get us, his group, up the mountain before another *cordée* that had started at about the same time. I was walking with hot dogs [crampons?]. I was so tired. The last stretch was along a knife-like crest of ice; we felt the wind for the first time, ready to blow us off. Beyond my fatigue, I was conscious only of the incredible distance below on either side of our trail of footsteps.

The top was one of the most incredible memories I have. The summit was no bigger than a queen-sized bed. We could not see any of the mountain we had just climbed, but could see for miles in every direction. This was the highest altitude I had ever been—we were just below the ceiling of 4,000 meters, my dad said. Another 50 meters and we would be over. Last summer I had another incredible experience: Laurent invited me and Julien to go on a flight with his dad. He gave us a tour of the Massif du Mont Blanc. From the air, you can see the folds in the layers of earth that make up the land we live on. The mountains and their valleys are tears through this terrain where its history of evolution can be clearly seen. Being inside that tiny plane, tossed by every turbulence caused by the mountains—irritations in the smooth passage of the layers of wind—one of the most exciting moments for me was recognizing the famous climbs which I had studied in the climbing guides: Grand Dru, Petit Dru, Dent du Géant. There is something magical about looking at these vertical walls and imagining the climber's path.
