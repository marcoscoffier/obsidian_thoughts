## [[1992-12-24_The-Christian-Paradox|The Christian Paradox]]
*1992-12-24 · Yale, New Haven · notebook p. 1 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_1_p003.jpg|p. 3]]*

Most educated Christians don't believe in the physical reality of Christ as God's son, which is fine. The religion still holds together on the tenet that it is spreading the word of the Lord—the message in the stories. I ask: why do we all have to go through the elaborate charade of convincing each other that we believe, when in fact we don't? You might say that by forcing everyone to follow the word, you get fringe people to do things—good things—which they would not have done had there not been this elaborate charade. 

I'm glad I do know something of these stories, so that the church is a common reference frame for much of the Western world. The problems I have with most church services are the same that I have with a lot of mass culture in general.

## [[1994-07-29_Flying-Over-Clouds-Diebenkorn-Turrell-and-Earth-Flatness|Flying Washington–NY: Clouds, Diebenkorn, Turrell & Earth's Flatness (Page 88)]]
*1994-07-29 · Summer 1994, Travel and Alps · notebook p. 88 · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p019.jpg|p. 19]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p020.jpg|p. 20]], [[attachments/pages/scan_1994_2_p021.jpg|p. 21]]*

Flying from Washington to NY I had a window seat. It had been storming and there were many clouds of every shape and kind and color in the sky. It was really quite beautiful. The plane was flying inside these strips. There were thin black strips (pollution?—I thought they were kind of beautiful), puffy cottonny, mountainous ones and large flat scrim like ones. The earth was mostly a dark kind of greyed out purple color because for the most part someone from the ground would see complete cloud cover, he could not see the differentiation that we could from our privaledged, airborne position. We would however often see breaks in the clouds above us and the blue sunny sky (Is it comforting to think that no matter how bad the weather, it is always a sunny day if you could just fly high enough?)

I remember Hollander mentioning that he was surprised that painters or artists had not reacted to views or visual stimulation which has been made possible by technological advances which allow humans all sorts of different views of the world. I am happy to mention that two of the artists about whom I have been most interested in the recent past, Diebenkorn and Turrell, both claim the visual experience of flying as a direct influence to their work. Diebenkorn vividly remembered his first plane flight, from New Mexico to the Bay Area (I believe) and the evidence can be seen in some of the later Berkley abstractions (50's before he switched to figuration) like the yellow one with black lines which Katherine copied. Turrell openly claims that his job as a small airplane pilot which has given him frequent views of the split heaven and earth is a direct influence to his pieces. He also mentions the multiple colors of the changing sunlight at twilight. He flew up and down the flat—and I think this is important—regions of Eastern California & the West to search for Roden Crater—he seeks to make a sculpture which directly confronts the viewer with a feeling of ½ heavens ½ earth.

> *[Diagram, top of p. 88]:* Cross-section of `Crater ->` bowl with a tiny standing viewer at the bottom and dotted sightlines forming a huge circle with the caption: `optical effect the sky "curves" creates a sphere w/ the earth. new horizon`.

I think it may be important that Diebenkorn doesn't admit to being openly influenced by anything `[above crossed-out: I thought of Diebenkorn's abstractions which critics have connected to what]`. He even would attack paintings, and "ruin" them to try to remove elements of figuration or representation which had "appeared" in them. Eventually, he stopped ruining pictures like that and would leave these elements as in *"Motorcycle Crash"* and *"The Archer"*. Diebenkorn doesn't want to think consciously about what goes into his abstractions and why. It is the critics who see evidences of the Ocean Park Architecture and landscape in the Ocean Park Series. I don't know if Diebenkorn is being completely honest about not thinking but I don't think one can completely eliminate the evidence of previous visual stimulae, a painter "names" visually even if he does not "name" things verbally. `[p. 40 *] [see the Wilson quote about memory & stars]`

Flying the other day, I was thinking about how flat the landscape really is, human beings can't do anything yet to affect this phenomena (word choice?—I don't have a definite definition in mind, though I know a thorough definition would be several pages long.) This flatness is similar to a painting's flatness—there is only so thick a painting can get—Frank Stella's (3ft?), Anselm Keiffer (1.5ft?) and likewise for the landscape, the Himalayan mountain range is the largest rupture in this flatness, 28,000m = 28km how does this compare to 40,000 km (diameter or circumference) of the earth. The earth is flatter than the paintings I have mentioned. Thus the earth's surface as seen from the sky could be the perfect subject matter for an abstract painting and the obvious reason why someone like Diebenkorn, who is somehow more true to his own visual experience than other painters who have painted abstractly, comes to make paintings reminiscent of such a landscape, which he admits having been excited by at the time. He cannot shake himself from the visual baggage which he is carrying and representing it. He is representing an image which exists only in his head, but I believe he is representing it & it does exist. The same sort of thing happens w/ Rothko over Still or Newman. People claim to see the sunset or the horizon or the sky in his works. He aims at a specific visual effect, his paint colors & tones are so carefully balanced. When he achieves the effect he desires that could very well be when the painting has matched an image he has, no matter how vague, of a large visual effect like a sunset, or the horizon.

## [[1999-06-15_Genius-in-Science-and-in-Art|Genius in Science and in Art]]
*1999-06-15 (estimated) · Paris, Beaux-Arts · scan [[attachments/pages/scan_1997_2_p072.jpg|p. 72]]*

In science genius needs no masquerade one can say one is a scientist - it is a daily activity, a result of successful completion of a type of education. Too often I confound the word artist w/ the word genius where I should say an artist of genius. In fact only an artist of genius is interesting. An artist "tout court" is pathetic, no use, no interest. This for me is a fundamental problem in my manuvering in my small art world. And is a fundamental chauvanism I must get over or hide.
\_\_\_
My idea of genius is closer to the scientist's I think. Many people are working for a similar goal, sharing ideas & results after the fact, or through constant interaction one person is considered to be a genius by the others
The results, the advances are elsewhere.

> Undated. A single diagonal line runs across the page from the middle of the first paragraph to the bottom left; it does not seem to cross anything out. The writing showing through is page 73.
