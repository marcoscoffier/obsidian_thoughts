# A Reading of Wordsworth (First Draft)

> --First Draft--  
> Oct. 31, 1994  
> A Reading of Wordsworth  

As I reach out my neck to cut my teeth on one of the founding pillars of the discipline of English Literature (if one can still call it a discipline), I worry that this grinding wheel might take off my face. These are the battles on which the Giants have erected and destroyed their discipline. The small intuitions I might have, have already been commented upon and dismissed in a weight of academic writings I am too weak to lift. Here I emerge, with a mortal academic career, to risk my tender mug. What on earth has brought me to this battle field? There are moments when the distant Titans, whom today's Pantheon wield in their battles, talk to me. And there are moments when today's scholars are more lucid and talk to me as well. These are the moments when they don't refer to previous battles, grudges they might have, but to their own personal feelings--their gut reactions. One of the key problems of poetry is : how does one communicate the most personal experience. My experience is as personal as anyone's, so I fly into the battlefield on the coattails of a primarily modern belief that everyone's opinion is somewhat valid. Hegel describes it as such:

...lyric is not restricted to certain epochs in the spiritual development of a people but can flourish abundantly in the most different epochs and is especially opportune in modern times when every individual claims the right of having his own personal point of view and mode of feeling.# Bloom begins his essay "Internalization of a Quest Romance," by quoting Freud:

Freud thought that even romance, with its element of play, probably commenced in some actual experience whose "strong impression on the writer had stirred up a memory of an earlier experience, generally belonging to childhood, which then arouses a wish fulfillment in the work in question, and in which elements of the recent event and the old memory should be discernible." This quote forms our concept of the differences between people--their inner most feelings refer to their own childhood experiences, and thus a person's deepest feelings will reflect the situation of her upbringing. People of different upbringings will have different childhood experiences and thus a universal art seems impossible momentarily. But, people are universally similar before they have had any experience, thus there are moments when a universal understanding can be posited. I am hard pressed to understand why Harold Bloom admits a great fondness for A R Ammons (in private). When Ammons invited him to visit the spot on the New Jersey shore which inspired "Corson's Inlet" however, Bloom claims that it was an exasperating experience and that he only wanted to find the nearest coffee shop. His walk, he claims, did nothing to add to his understanding of the poem. Bloom is also notorious for having refused to take a walk through the country side at the Wordsworth Convention at Dove cottage.

Harold Bloom loves literature. He is well known not only as a prolific reader but a precocious one. He read many of the Western Classics at a younger age than even his most elite academic rivals. The marking moments of his childhood are books which must have affected his love of later books, not Wordsworthian walks in nature. There are authors who resonate for him because of the particular history of his own literary progress. I don't know what particular combination of experiences leads him to a selection of most important writers, but it seems that his choice has much to do with the influence a writer has had on other writers, which means that Bloom is not really making his own decisions, but rather allowing previous generations of writers to lead him to those who have created the greatest impact on their successors' words. I have allowed myself this rather long tangent to discuss a concept of universality and make clear that if I say I understand, or feel something in Wordsworth, it is me, my personal self, who is reporting this feeling. I don't want to relate some kind of convention. If someone writes a text whose defining characteristics are its relation to other texts and conventions-- arguments of the day-- then that work remains forever grounded in a framework which must always be referenced to understand the work. The work which throws conventions to the wind and concentrates on the deepest feeling of the author can be eternally resonant.

The next question one has to ask is : if the experience to be related in the poem is to be universal, how general is it? All human beings were born, walk on two feet and sleep lying down: How interesting is this short list as subject matter for poetry? I think it must be emphasized, how short this list is. Nature itself cannot be assumed as a common ground for someone who has lived in New York City his entire life. The nostalgia I feel when the seasons change can't be shared by a Southern Californian. Someone who lives on a farm or in the mountains will have different associations with the surrounding natural environment from someone who merely vacations there. I want to believe, the way Wordsworth does in his idolization of Michael, that the person who lives in a natural environment always remains extremely receptive to its beauty, whereas the city dweller is quickly jaded by the particularities of his environment--but I myself have always preferred the country, have vacationed in it in a very Wordsworthian way, and must realize that there are those who are impassioned by the excitement of a big city. Theodoro Adorno refers to this problem, though he does not claim to solve it, when he writes in his essay "On Lyric Poetry and Society" that:

The lyric work hopes to attain universality through unrestrained individuation.

and a bit later:

> Only one who hears the voice of humankind in the poem's solitude can understand what the poem is saying....(p.38)

These two statements of a contradiction outline the main problem in understanding the lyric, and my claim to access it. Adorno's phrases outline a two step process. How does one verbalize unrestrained individuation or solitude? Then, how is this verbalization understood, or made universal?

Wordsworth's main problem can be summed by the Zen aphorism: "He who knows does not speak. He who speaks does not know." Being a poet, unfortunately, Wordsworth must speak. Often, Wordsworth seems to lack faith in the first step, he backs out of descriptions, choosing to repeat rather than elaborate, allowing his words to fall into clichés or convention which is the antithesis of the work of art. But, I believe that there moments when he does freely describe his inner workings. We are then left with the second question: can this verbalization be universally understood?

Hegel in a section of "The Romantic Arts" entitled "Lyric Poetry" maps out many of the more specific problems I wish to address concerning the lyric in Wordsworth. He begins the essay: The poetic imagination, as the activity of a poet, does not, as plastic art does, set before our eyes the thing itself in its external reality (even if that reality be produced by art) but gives us on the contrary an inner vision and feeling of it.#

A poem does not present the reader with an object of external reality. Its medium is words that are concepts, part of the inner self of the poet, which is precisely why Hegel places poetry above the other arts on his quest for the best expression of Geist. The poem can represent however, and this poses a problem for Wordsworth. Hegel later claims that: what the poet aims at producing in his hearer is the same mood which has been created in him by the event related and which he has therefore introduced entirely into his poem.

Here the problem becomes more evident, how does the poet reproduce the same emotion in the reader which he felt in the event, when the event, because the poem is not a plastic art, cannot be presented itself? The artist and his work of art basically become a satellite for the transferal of a feeling which the poet received from nature to the reader who receives it from the poem.

Wordsworth's describes his motivations for writing Resolution and Independence in a letter to Sara Hutchinson:

I consider the manner in which I was rescued from my dejection and despair almost as an interposition of Providence.... It is in the character of the old man to tell his story in a manner which the impatient reader must necessarily feel as tedious. But good God! such a figure, in such a place, a pious self respecting, miserably infirm old man telling such a tale!

The encounter he had with the old man had quite an effect upon him. Already in his letter he describes a discrepancy between what he felt and what he could convey to the reader. In the poem, Wordsworth's discussion with the old man is in fact very strange. He approaches him with an opaque "nice day isn't it" at the end of verse XII. The man responds with a "gentle answer" which isn't even described. Then Wordsworth asks him "what occupation do you here pursue?" and, rather than quote the man's tale, proceeds to describes the man's "flash of mild surprise," and "vivid sable eyes". The "feeble words" like his "feeble chest," which are " above the reach/ Of ordinary men" are never recited, only paraphrased: He told that to these waters he had come
To gather leeches, being old and poor:

Employment hazardous and wearisome!

He had many hardships to endure:

From pond to pond he roamed from moor to moor;

Housing, with God's good help, by choice or chance;

And in this way he gained an honest maintenance.

One has to wonder what is so great about such a tale. Whatever Wordsworth finds to be great about it is not being communicated. This is not the first time Wordsworth has employed such a strategy: The greatness of the tale is absent from this poem the way Lucy is absent from her poems and Tintern Abbey is absent from the poem bearing its name (though those two cases pose very different problems which I won't get into). The man's tale-- "such a tale"-- remains a mystery. Rather than try to make original observations, "lively representations" as Hegel calls them, he gives up. A few stanzas later after he has drifted away from contact with the man, he repeats the same question and again partially relates the man's identical answer. This is the second time he has repeated an already conventional description in this poem The first two stanzas of Resolution and Independence describe a scene: "There was a roaring.../" "But now... /" "All the things that love the sun are out of doors.../ The hare is running races in her mirth.../" In the third stanza he places himself in this world and repeats the images:

I was a Traveler then upon the moor;

I saw the hare that raced about with joy;

I heard the woods and distant rivers roar;...

One has to ask, when do the first two stanza's take place? How does he know of this scene if he is not before it yet? If a tree falls in the forest...He repeats the description of nature at the beginning of Resolution and Independence with out changing it, and then repeats the leech-gatherer's tale. The repetition does not add to the description of the scene or of the character. He in fact makes himself into a cliché. [Prof. Blood, there is a big conceptual leap here which I haven't exactly reconciled] Convention is not universal Victor Shklovsky says in his essay "Art as Technique".

Habituation devours works clothes ,furniture one's wife, and the fear of war.... And art exists that one may recover the sensation of life; it exists to make one feel things, to make the stone stony. The purpose of art is to impart things as they are perceived and not as they are known. The technique of art is to make objects "unfamiliar" to make forms difficult, to increase the difficulty and length of perception because the process of perception is an aesthetic end in itself and must be prolonged. Art is a way of experiencing the artfulness of an object the object is not important.

Statements like these were taken to the extreme by Dada artists and Surrealists to come up with metaphors and analogies which are so unexpected that, by shocking, they bring one to see that which had become automatic. They were especially key on blending perceptual mechanisms-- like tactility, smell, look, etc.--in one comparison like having something taste blue or smell wet. Wordsworth is at his strongest when he doesn't allow his descriptions of nature to fall into a conventional association-- when he mixes opposing feelings in a single image. Anytime Wordsworth picks either joy or dejection for an image that situation is classified, or labeled--it becomes one or the other. The image of the rock to which the leech gatherer in Resolution and Independence is compared is a decent metaphor for this duality. A rock, since well before Christ founded the church on the shoulders of his apostle Peter, has been a metaphor for stillness--immobility. Yet this stone is on top of an "eminence" and gives quite a different message. Despite its rock-like stillness, it is forever prepared to fall. Wordsworth compares this rock to the leech gatherer, a strange metaphor. But if we have the perseverance which Shklovsky imparts to us that art happens in unexpected comparisons and metaphors, I think we are richly rewarded. The stone's dual message makes it alive in the poet's eyes:

...it could thither come and whence;

So that it seems a thing endured with sense:

> Like a sea beast crawled forth, that on a shelf
> Of rock or sand reposeth, there to sun itself;...

The life comes from the dissonance of these contradicting images that fight each other out of becoming a labeled convention (like a still rock or an object which is perpetually moving like a pendulum). We have represented for us the perpetually still moment before movement. Both stillness and mobility are always present.

In Tintern Abbey Wordsworth describes similar contradictions in the descriptions of nature: the coarser pleasures of his boyish days "--That time is past,/ And all its aching joys are now no more,/ And all its dizzy raptures...." (Italics are mine.) The contradictions continue:

For I have learned

To look on nature, not as in the hour
Of thoughtless youth: but hearing oftentimes
The still, sad music of humanity,
Not harsh or grating, though of ample power
To chasten and subdue. And I have felt
A presence that disturbs me with the joy
Of elevated thoughts; a sense sublime
Of something far more deeply interfused,
Whose dwelling is the light of setting suns,
And the round ocean and the living air,
And the blue sky, and the mind of the man:
A motion and a spirit that impels
All thinking things, all objects of all thought,
And rolls through all things.

These ending images are highly reminiscent of the list of similes in the first stanza of Shelley's "Hymn to Intellectual Beauty" because they aren't describing a particular place, but remain general enough to become universal evocations of the moments when such a feeling is found. Because the images aren't tied to a particular pile of rocks, or tree, or leech gatherer, the universal experience is described here though it is not conveyed. I, the reader don't feel what he is describing, I am reminded of having been in a similar situation where I must have felt the same as he. One can never prove that I felt the same as he before a sunset, but that argument is the same as: Do we all see the same color green, and seems rather futile to me. After paraphrasing the man who simply says that he wanders from pond to pond gathering leeches, Wordsworth drifts out of a discussion with the leech gatherer in verse XVI.

The old Man still stood talking by my side;

But now his voice was like a stream
Scarce heard nor word from word could I divide;...

I believe this drifting attention span is an essential characterization of anyone's thought. The mind goes on ever shifting and which is the most interesting reason to read Joyce's Ulysses shows us at least the unstoppable uncontrollable flow of the imagination. No strength of consciousness can ever clamor aboard this rolling river to control it. The consciousness can at best bluntly change its course, but never all that effectively. Wordsworth's stream in the mind is a thorough metaphor: trying to analyze a piece of the mind is as impossible as grabbing a piece of the stream to analyze it. A piece of a stream no longer has anything to do with the stream, it is at best a handful of muddy water. We are given an fun description of the workings of this poet's mind.

The life I see in Wordsworth is along these lines. He flirts with being able to recreate the ungraspable deepest feelings because they are not locatable, but yet he often undoes his own passion. He gives up on it. Most of the ability to convey such subtle yet deep emotions is in the confidence that they can be communicated through whatever medium you are using. At the end of Peele Castle (or Elegiatic Stanzas), he gives up on any communication of that deepest feeling which he might have had in the earlier poems. It is very strange how he undoes himself, and how he has so little faith in his art. This process becomes quite painful:

> Farewell, farewell the heart that lives alone,
> Housed in a dream, at a distance from the Kind!

Such happiness, wherever it be known,
Is to be pitied; for ‘tis surely blind.

I don't believe that the best poets are those who are isolated from the Kind (academia?, or the world--(though a case could be made for Wallace Stevens)), but those who tap into the meditative aloneness and feel the resonances of this experience in other poets. It is as though the great poets all share a little secret, they have all been able to feel a depth of communication that others claim to understand . I believe that Harold Bloom can feel this presence (if it can be thus called) but he has learned of it only through exposure to the poetry. Because the nature of what is tapped by these poets is universally accessible to anyone receptive to such images. One does not have to know or rely on a specific convention to approach those moments in the poems. Except of course the convention of language. But language has the saving grace that it allows thoughts to become concepts and to be communicated-- an argument made by both Hegel and Adorno. Not everyone is going to underline the same passages in a book. But there is a time when enough of the exterior world can be cleared out of the consciousness that we all can become the same. I found it incredibly relieving as I began to learn about what academia was most concerned with, it was not foreign to anything I had not thought about already. Of course one could say that this has to do with a certain background, but when my intuition served me right for Chinese poems and paintings (of a style I had no familiarity with) I became more convinced. One isn't going to be receptive to the same works of art every day.

I have heard Intimations of Immortality chastised because it isn't memorable--because it has no subject. I will let Hegel take the first step in validating Wordsworth's position:

> ... since the inner subjective life is the proper source of lyric, the poet must retain the right to limit himself to expressing purely inner moods, reflections, etc., without spreading himself into portraying a concrete situation in its external features as well.#

But, wish to add myself that this is the poem to which I have always been attracted. True: I don't remember it the way I do Resolution and Independence or Michael,. The process of reading it however leads to a feeling in me which is much closer to the experience I have before nature than the other poems who have specific subjects or places. In Tintern Abbey, Wordsworth describes the process which he has given up on in Peele castle.

...that blessed mood

In which the burthen of the mystery,
In which the heavy and the weary weight
Of this unintelligible world,
Is lightened: --that serene and blessed mood,
In which the affectations gently lead us on,--
Until the breath of this corporeal frame
And even the motion of our human blood
Almost suspended, we are laid asleep
In body and become a living soul:
While with an eye made quiet by the power
Of harmony, and the deep power of joy,
We see into the life of things.
Rather than describe the situation which has brought him to such a state,
he describes the state itself. The reader is brought to what is very
similar to a form of meditation not by a representation of the situation
which brought Wordsworth to that state, but by a description of that
state itself. Rather than the awkward blank spaces we find in the
description of the leech gatherer, we have a full beautiful description
of the inner life of the poet, at that moment which allows him to see.

These are not lines which anyone can be taught to understand or to feel. If one doesn't then there isn't much to be done. I am in no situation to say that this state of lucid reverie is universally felt, though I deeply hope it is. Those are the moments when I feel most in touch with a common humanity. The non-appreciator of those passages finds himself in the same position as the student of a Zen master, who wishes to find the way and is given a series of unanswerable questions. There are moments when I can feel my own breath, when the wind cools small planes of my face and my thoughts seem to flow with my gusting hair. Trying to describe the physical stimulae of these moments immediately seems cliché, because these aren't moments that can profit from new metaphors of the surrealists or from complicated troping. These moments are simplest at there best. One's life is paced by a few incredible moments like these--most incredible when they sneak up on you: I have found myself reading a book when I completely lose any sense of time and find myself blissfully sad at dusk with the completed book on my lap: Happy for having spent an entire day in a dream state with the book; sad because the world of the book has come to an end and I will never be able to return to it in the same fresh way. Any runner or hiker knows those moments when one forgets all time and bodily fatigue, and suddenly feels beyond the rhythmic churning of one's legs to the wonderful state of reverie and free thought with which we are so familiar, but which is so difficult to achieve. The poet, can at his very best lead us to the experience he had in nature, he cannot, however deliver this experience. It is in this state that anyone finds their love of nature. And of all things. This is what I listen to music for: that moment when I am no longer conscious of my body and I can just float with its rhythms and dynamics. I also find that in repetitive physical exercise the mind becomes unbelievably liberated and inventive. I get my best ideas while jogging.

There is no way that we can have the same experience Wordsworth had because we can't have contact with the man. Wordsworth's poem isn't a recreation of the scene which moved him so-- he doesn't even attempt such a recreation. This is a vital point. The best the poem can do is to describe Wordsworth's own mental state--his feelings, not the perceptual experience which led him to this experience. After all, the state is universal, not the moment which causes the experience. One has only to look at one's own self. I rarely am moved by the same object the same way more than once. That is how a work of art imitates nature. It somehow manages to change with each new reading the way the sunrise changes every morning. The viewer in both cases changes between the readings and somehow the sunrise and the poem can accommodate these changes in the reader. The work of art can never be equivalent to nature, thus the criteria for good works of art are that they sustain the most rereadings, Good art is prepared to reflect changes in your personality each new time you reapproach it. Hence the saying which I believe refers to Keats that reading poetry teaches you about yourself.

Peele castle sets itself apart from the rest of Wordsworth's poems. It marks the furthest separation between the artist and nature:

Ah THEN, if mine had been the painter's hand,
To express what then I saw; and add the gleam,
The light that never was, on sea or land,
The consecration, and the poet's dream
I would have planted thee, thou hoary Pile
Amid a world how different from this!
The "this" could refer to either the world of the painting or that which
he has just recalled. But in either case what is made obvious in these
lines is the extent to which Wordsworth dreams of applying his hand to
this representation: he wants to "add the gleam" "the light that never
was," "the poet's dream."

Such in the fond illusion of my heart,
Such Picture would I at that time had made:
And seen the soul of truth in every part,
A steadfast peace that might not be betrayed.
Farewell, farewell the heart that lives alone,
Housed in a dream, at a distance from the Kind!
Such happiness , wherever it be known,
Is to be pitied for it is surely blind.

Best to pick them

On second thought, the reality of academia isn't as cruel as the battle's of giants picture I painted earlier. My fear isn't that I get my face torn off for a hasty interpretation, but rather that my ideas, my self really, go by without making a stir. I am mortified by the thought that I could drift by another's thoughts without causing a responce--or interest. This is a most selfish fear

I have always harbored a deep love for classical music. My mother has always played the piano. She used to put me to sleep by playing softer pieces herself or by playing slower movements of recordings. Just recently I bought Arthur Rubenstien's Chopin Nocturnes, recorded in the late sixties and I cannot explain the depth at which some of those pieces moved me. I called to tell my mother and she commented that those recordings were compiled into a complete Chopin Collection which she bought in 1972 the years before I was born. She would play the slower Nocturnes to put me to sleep. I want to say that it is Rubenstien's artistry that touches me so, but how nostalgic would I feel about Crosby Stills and Nash if I had been brought up on their music. I am much more drawn to the massiveness of the natural environment, the huge spaces of earth and sky that can be seen from an airplane or from a mountain top

Wordsworth says in a general comment on Intimations Ode whose full title is Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Reollections of Early Childhood:

Many times while going to school have I grasped at a wall or a tree to recall myself from this abyss of idealism to the reality. At that time I was afraid of such processes. In later periods of life I have deplored, as we all have reason to do, a subjugation of an opposite character, and have rejoices over the rememberances.... To that dreamlike vividness and splendour which invest objects of sight in childhood, everyone, I believe, if he would look back could bear testimony.... Wordsworth lives in this "abyss of idealism" which is from where the poems are generated. A poem can never give us the actual tree to grab onto to shake us from the reverie, thus it is rather futile to attempt to relay that concrete experience in a lyric poem that isn't what the gendre is about.

In Intimations Ode Wordsworth claims to have managed to once again tap
into the fleeting
Yet in my heart of hearts I feel your might;
I only have relinquished one delight
To live beneath your more habitual sway.
Wordsworth has trouble with the fact that he is not a native of the land
who can enjoy it without self consciousness.

Hegel supports the slip away from a particular description of place or experience which keyed off the feeling which demands the creation of a poem:

> [The poet] expresses his depression, his melancholy, his cheerfulness, his glow of patriotism, etc., in an analogous event in such a way that the centre of the thing is not the occurence itself but the state of mind which is mirrored in it. #

A lyric poem... has a unity of a totaly different kind from an epic, namely the inwardness of mood or reflection which expatiates on itself, mirrors itself in the external world,...#

...the poet who imagines and feels disappears in his poetic activity before the objectivity of his creation. This alienation of himself can only be completely avoided by the artist on his subjective side if on the one hand he absorbs into himself the entire world of objects and circumstances, and stamps them with his own inner consciousness, and if, on the other hand, he discloses his self-concentrated heart, opens his eyes and ears, raises purely dull feeling into vision and ideas, and gives words and language to this enriched inner life so that as inner life it can find expression.#

But in order that this expression may not remain a merely casual expression of an individual's own immediate feeling and ideas, it becomes the language of the poetic inner life, and therefore however inimately the insights and feelings which the poet describes as his own belong to him as a single individual, they must nevertheless possess a universal vitality, i.e. they must be genuine feelings and meditations for which the poet invents or finds the adequate and lively expression. Wordsworth is trapped by convention. His language became cliché soon after he developed it. His self-consciousness seems to have crippled and killed the enormous feeling. "All bad poetry is sincere," Harold Bloom is ready to remind us. It is precisely Wordsworth's need to make "good" poetry which stifles moments when he surely approaches conveying his original experience with nature. the ninth century Zen master Siubi's answer to the question: what is the secret of Zen? Siubi took the inquirer to a bamboo grove. He pointed at the stalks and said, "See how long these are. See how short those are." We assume that his questioner saw what can't be understood. That he found meaning in the seemingly empty demonstration the master gave him. Describing the reeds any further won't bring about any deeper understanding.As high as we have mounted in delight
In our dejection do we sink as low;...

We poets in our youth begin in gladness;

But thereof come in the end despondency and madness

I think that these contradictions are much more powerful in Tintern Abbey when they are mixed into the same image. Those combined images seem much truer to me and my experience.
