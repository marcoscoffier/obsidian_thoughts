# The Resonance of Lu Chi's Concept of Poetry to a Foreigner

> [!note] About this file
> Undated; it looks back on freshman year, so sophomore year or later.

> Intro to Chinese Poetry  
> The Resonance of Lu Chi's Concept of Poetry to a Foreigner  

My freshman year at Yale a good friend and I decided to explore the true potential of Sterling Memorial Library and went into the stacks to find a book on ancient Chinese jokes. Most of the jokes complied in that volume (which I have not found again since) were dirty. Sitting on the floor, roaring in laughter, too tickled to quiet down for the poor library patrons who were unfortunate enough to have less amusing reasons to be in the stacks at that time, a mythic, universal quality of those anecdotes was revealed to me. Laughter is a genuine response to a certain combination of elements; it cannot be faked. Finding out the mechanisms of a joke always kills it, but can often reveal some very complex and interesting inner workings. I discovered a common bond between me and a man long dead from a culture I new very little about in a country I had never been near, let alone in. What had made him laugh centuries ago was making me laugh then.

My subject for this paper is Chinese poetry not their jokes, but my enjoyment of the poetry can be easily related to my laughter at those jokes. For a foreign reader to have a genuine response to the poetry, it must contain a universal element. Through the inherent inadequacy of any translation and the multiple layers of distortion which time and cultural differences deposit on the freshness of a poet's word, a universal message must be communicated. I have chosen to work with Lu Chi's Wen Fu, a poem in the "fu" form which though it has many poetic images and metaphors is not really a poem but an essay in verse. Its expository form gives the Wen Fu, the ability to describe a more concrete impression of a Chinese ideal of poetry than the short lyric poems of which Lu Chi describes the poetic creation. I am in no position to comment on the nature of the Chinese language, but I have found that the Chinese appreciation of suggestion over exposition, the compactness in the poetry, as well as my difficulty in understanding how the language operates, makes it very difficult for me to extract concrete notions from the poems considered Chinese lyrics themselves. I am furthermore going to refer his ideas to poetic ideals I know, by contrasting his position to a western one held by the German Romantic critic, Theodoro Adorno, in his essay, "On Lyric Poetry and Society"#. These two thinkers are separated by time and culture, a 20th century German is being compared to a 3rd century Chinese, but their agendas prove to be similar in many ways. The juxtaposition of these two essays sheds new light onto each.

Lu Chi begins one of the sections of his poem with the phrase, "writing is in itself a joy." With his passion in my sails, I forge into this paper. Later in the same section he describes a power of poetry:

In a sheet of paper is contained the Infinite,
And evolved from an inch sized heart, an endless panorama. This boundless metaphor is echoed in Adorno:

> It is commonly said that a perfect lyric poem must possess totality or universality, must provide the whole within the the bounds of the poem and the infinite within the poet's finitude.(p.42)

One sees rather quickly that their conceptions of ideal poetry are quite similar. The poem captures or expresses ( a difference which will become crucial) the infinite, the universal. Lu Chi's concept of the universal comes from nature, whereas Adorno's is grounded in society--in a universality between individuals.

Throughout Lu Chi's essay, the inspiration for writing poetry and the infinite natural world are compared and often become synonymous. In the first section a series of equivalences are described:

[The poet's] lament for fleeting life is in observance of the four seasons that ever revolve,
His regard for the myriad growing things inspires in him thoughts as profuse.

As with the fallen leaves in autumn's rigor his heart sinks in grief, So is each tender twig in sweet spring a source of joy. In frost he finds sympathy at moments when his heart is all frigid purity, Or far, far, into the highest clouds he makes his minds abode.# Each line has one of these relations: fleeting life = four seasons that revolve; myriad growing things = thoughts as profuse; fallen leaves = heart sinks with grief... which, I am told, are emphasized in the Chinese. Each phrase or equation compares an emotion of the poet's to its stimulus in nature. The art making process becomes a dialogue between nature and the poet's emotions which I am told comes from a belief in Taoism. Later in the poem in the section entitled " The Working Process," nature becomes a metaphor for the poem. He makes barren twigs put forth luxuriant foliage as they sway, Or by endless waves he traces to the remote fountainhead. Lu Chi is not making words, but the foliage on twigs. He continues a little later:

When the substance of a composition, trunk of a tree, is by truth sustained,
Style aids it to branch into leafy boughs and bear fruit. The poem is described as though it were a natural object. It is interesting to note that the character for nature is the same as that for spontaneity which is one of the most respected qualities in a poem. The closest I have come to being in a Chinese house is having visited the one which can be found in the Metropolitan Museum of Art. I was moved by that imitation to an aspect of Chinese culture (aesthetic really). I first realized it in an object which struck me as phenomenally beautiful. A rock which resembled an old tree branch because it was twisted and veined was perched on a wooden pedestal which was carved into twisting curving forms reminiscent of the same rock. This "art object", displayed on a delicately carved base, is just a rock. It was not created by anyone, it was just a piece of nature. The house was very open, with running water and plants, and the material of the furniture was always quite evident-- the wood of the chairs and the table, did not try to be anything other than wood. I was stuck by how the entire aesthetic of this place was brought in from the outdoors- the paintings were of flowers and branches, the sculpture was an un- manipulated rock and the wood of the furniture was left very near to its own natural shape. Nature was brought into their homes. The house permeated by nature can be regarded as a metaphor for the poet's head--penetrated by nature.

Also in the section "the Working Process," Lu Chi says: "... not until the heart attains calm transparency does thought crystallize." The short phrase "calm transparency" is one of the most resonant I have come across in all the Chinese poems I have read. It is a powerful metaphor for what seems to be a very Chinese conception of art. The poet becomes a window to nature. He does not hold a mirror up to nature, which is a common trope in the West. Instead he becomes transparent to it. Nature can flow through him into his poem. The mediation of the poet or even of his medium is reduced to transparency. Adorno mentions a similar type of transparency: ...immersion in what has taken individual form elevates the lyric poem to the status of something universal by making manifest something not distorted, not grasped, not yet subsumed.(p.38)

"Indeed" Lu Chi continues in the third section:

> ...feeling and expression should never fail to correspond, As each emotional change wears a new complexion on a sensitive face. The calmness is not present in this description of the poetic act. Here the artist's inner thoughts seem to be what is expressed. It seems that Lu Chi contradicts himself here (not knowing enough about Taoism, I am not sure if these are seen as contradictions in China, but I have to ask) how can the poet be at once moved by nature and transparent? If the poet is moved to write, he must derive part of the impetus to make a poem from an internal emotion. This to me implies a mediation, but I am in no place to follow up this line of questioning because I remain outside this culture and language. I t does seem that this belief in the exact correspondence between the poet's feelings and his expression in poetry forms the basis for other ideologies in Chinese thought. I was in fact presented with an example of this belief when a guest lecturer, a young Chinese man, got very angry with a comment he quoted to us from Stephen Owen about Tu Fu. Anger like laughter is often a key to the revelation of inner mechanisms. Owen's comment, as I understood it, was that Tu Fu was always in the company of his poetic imagination and thus not really lonely but rather merely secluded. The gist of his statement being that one can never be truly lonely if one has the creative spirit. I believe that our lecturer was so angry with this statement because he thought Owen had disclaimed something about Tu Fu's poetry; that Owen was saying that Tu Fu was insincere--that he wasn't really lonely. It is a subtle point but an interesting one. Owen, I believe, was not questioning Tu Fu's sincerity but only stressing the vitality of the creative spirit which could actually become company. He emphasized this redefining Tu Fu's situation as not being aloneness but rather seclusion. He did not mean that there were other people out in the woods with Tu Fu. Our guest lecturer was holding fast to a Chinese tradition that the poets directly express their feelings and that if a poem is about loneliness the poet must have been lonely. Questioning a poet's sincerity in our lecturer's mind is equivalent to questioning his poetic ability, which I do not believe Owen was doing. Tu Fu's poetry is about loneliness, our lecturer was adamant in describing the loneliness he had felt reading Tu Fu, but that loneliness was full of poetic imagination.#

Our lecturer's anger seems to be a case of mis-translation. The subtle change in language Owen was using to make his point was not understood, and went over like a ton of bricks. The translation was jarring where the original had been subtle. # This brings us to the inherent problem with Lu Chi's belief in a "calm transparency": the poetic act is mediated not only by the poet himself but by the very material of his art which is objective. My situation reading a poem in translation makes its fundament in the Chinese culture and language that much more obvious to me. I am especially conscious of the mediative aspect of language from the Chinese poet's thoughts written in Chinese translated into English which I must translate into my own thoughts.

I have had to struggle with the same problem with language in my study of Western Romantic poetry. Language reveals its own objectivity in Romantic Lyric poetry. The poet's most personal utterance must use one of the most objective mediums of any art form. I propose that the problems of language with which modern western Lyric criticism has to wrestle are the same for any language and do not become significantly greater when the mediation progresses through two languages rather than one. Any language has its own inherent problems common to all languages. Theodoro Adorno, writes that:

... language is itself something double. Through its configurations it assimilates itself completely into subjective impulses; one would almost think it had produced them. But at the same time language remains the medium of concepts, remains that which establishes an inescapable relationship to the universal and society....

He recognizes it as objective but then uses a Hegelian notion of concepts to show how it is language itself, because it operates in concepts which allows the poet to approach something universal. "The lyric work," says Adorno, "hopes to attain universality through unrestrained individuation.(p.38)" I am reminded of the story of Tao Ch'ien, a heroic figure in Chinese poetry because he left Government office to return to his home in nature. Tao Ch'ien chose to follow his personal taste. He was not widely read till 300 years after his death, when he quickly assumed his position as one of China's very best poets. Hsieh ling-yun, his contemporary, who was very popular in his time, is not so widely recognized today. It seems that Tao Ch'ien has struck a universal chord, through his pursuit of the personal. To which Adorno responds: ...even the resistance to social pressure is not something absolutely in individual; The artistic forces in that resistance, which operate in and through the individual and his spontaneity, are objective forces ...forces, that is, that are part of the constitution of the whole and not at all merely forces of a rigid individuality blindly opposing society.(p.43)

Those forces are equivalent to the objective force of language, which finally unifies the individual with universality.

The universality of the lyric's substance, however, is social in nature. Only one who hears the voice of humankind in the poem's solitude can understand what the poem is saying....(p.38)

In Lu Chi's essay the poet is a window from the universal nature, his inspiration comes undistorted from "the myriad living things" he observes. In Adorno the poet is a window to the universal. The perspective of the essay has changed from the poet to the reader. By reaching inside himself, the artist can actually tap into something which is universal in all of us. He reaches beyond the cultural conventions to what is human and the core of us all. It is this fundamental sameness which I find to be the most powerful ground for any kind of cultural criticism.

I have tried to describe the feelings I get from a Chinese approach to poetry, which I am not in a situation to fully understand. But the glimmer of a universality streaks through the layers of convention separate me from the poet's experience in nature. I am standing before Lu Chi's poem the way he describes a poet before nature. The poem contains the light of nature, or universality, but is sometimes impenetrable, and other times reveals a flash of illumination. My situation as critic is not so different as the one he describes for the poet:

Such moments when Mind and Matter hold perfect communion, And wide vistas open to regions hitherto entirely barred, will come with irresistible force,
And go, their departure none can hinder.

So I leave you, feeling that the wide vistas I hoped would open in this paper, have been small compared to the dark impenetrable mass of confusing prose I have tried to rearrange until a light would shine through.
