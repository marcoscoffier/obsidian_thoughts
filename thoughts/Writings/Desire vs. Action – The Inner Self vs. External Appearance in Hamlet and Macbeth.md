# Desire vs. Action: The Inner Self vs. External Appearance in Hamlet and Macbeth

> Desire vs. Action: The Inner Self vs. External Appearance in Hamlet  
> and Macbeth  

The discrepancy between the inner self, beliefs and desires, and the external, action and expression, is a key concern of Shakespeare. Everyone of the four great tragedies Hamlet, Macbeth, Othello and King Lear is concerned with this problem. Othello constantly has to deal with the fact that many people cannot look past his skin color, to see his magnanimous inner qualities. King Lear strips off all the outer garbs of royalty, and stands naked in a storm asking "Is man no more than this?" We have been told that some critics have stated that Macbeth is Hamlet told from Claudius's perspective, or, I suppose that, Hamlet is Macbeth told through Malcom's' eyes. Even if the regicide plots are similar in these two plays the characters are different which changes the stories. Of course, stories change when told from different perspectives as do the definitions of the characters. If you ask two enemies what they think of themselves and what they think of their enemy, the two descriptions are bound to be very different. This is the discrepancy between internal perception and the external. No one knows exactly how they come across to others, and no one knows exactly what someone else is thinking. Trying to make connections, a moment when two people do think or feel the same thing, is a continual part of my life, at least, I can't talk for everyone. The study of literature is about making these connections. Reading is about associating with an author. He can bring one into a world he has created and make one react to it. Discussion of literature is the discussion of these reactions. The connection between the inner world of the mind and its representation to the rest of the world is of utmost concern to Shakespeare and all writers; it is the art of expression. Shakespeare always had an audience whom he had to please, and had to be constantly aware of how he came across, especially dealing with the subtle criticisms of the social system of which his works are full. In Hamlet and Macbeth, the art of expression is compared to the connection between desire and action in the lives of these warrior kings. Macbeth wishes to hide his inner thoughts. His problems are not about whether his true self can be understood, but whether his true self will be discovered. Duncan says, "there's no art/ To find the mind's construction in the face [Macbeth, I, iv,12-13]." Lady Macbeth tries to get her husband to create a false external appearance to protect him from the danger of anyone discovering his inner thoughts:

Lady Macbeth: Your face, my Thane, is as a book where men
May read strange matters. To beguile the time,
Look like the time; bear welcome in your eye,
Your hand, your tongue: look like th' innocent flower,
But be the serpent under't. [Macbeth, I, v, 63-67]
She is asking him to act, to play a part. Macbeth agrees with her when he
says, "False face must hide what false heart doth know [I, iv, 12-13]."
Macbeth does not succeed. He is not accused of killing Duncan on stage,
but he is not treated as a king should be treated. At the end of the
play, it is obvious that Macbeth's inner workings have been discovered.
Angus one of the soldiers who are about to defeat Macbeth, makes a
metaphor, about how the Macbeth's image has shrunk. His soldiers are
no longer willing to fight for a false face.
Angus: Now does he feel
His secret murders sticking on his hands;
Now minutely revolts upbraid his faith-breach
Those he commands move only in command,
Nothing in love. Now does he feel his title
Hang loose about him, like a giant's robe
Upon a dwarfish thief. [Macbeth, V, ii, 17-22]

Clothes become a metaphor for external appearance in Macbeth. This is not surprising clothes have a lot to do with external appearance. In Hamlet, Polonius, giving his son, Laertes, (who was on his way to the city of high fashion, Paris) parting advice says, "apparel oft proclaim the man." Hamlet's clothes are also referred to in reference to his outward appearance:

Queen: Why seems it [clouds over the conscience] so particular with thee?
Hamlet: Seems Madam? Nay, it is. I know not "seems."
‘Tis not alone my inky cloak, good mother,
Nor customary suits of solemn black
Nor windy suspicion of forced breath,
No, nor the fruitful river in the eye,
Nor the dejected haviour of the visage
Together with all forms, moods, shapes of grief,
That can denote me truly. These indeed seem,
For they are but actions that a man might play,
But I have that within which passes show;
These but the trappings and the suits of woe. [Hamlet, I,i,75-86]
Hamlet is not trying to hide his inner most thoughts, but is incapable
of expressing them to the other people in his world--he does a fine
job expressing himself to the audience in the Globe just not to that in
Elsinore. He gives the Globe theater audience a wonderful description
of his inability in one of his famous soliloquies. He reacts to one of
the actors from a traveling company:
Hamlet: O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!
Is it not monstrous that this player here,
But in fiction, in a dream of passion,
Could force his soul so to his own conceit
That from her working all his visage waned,
Tears in his eyes, distraction in his aspect,
A broken voice, and his whole function suiting
With forms to his conceit?...
Yet I,
A dull and muddy mettled rascal, peak
Like a John-a-dreams, unpregnant of my cause
And can say nothing. No, not for a king,
Upon whose dear property and most dear life
A damned defeat was made. Am I a coward?...
Why what an ass am I! This is most brave,
That I, the son of a dear father murdered,
Prompted to my revenge by heaven and hell,
Must, like a whore, unpack my heart with words
And fall a-cursing like a very drab [prostitute] [Hamlet, II, ii, 560-599]
The ending lines of this soliloquy start to refer to the similarity
mentioned at the beginning of this paper between the internal and external
selves and the desire for action and the action itself. Hamlet is not
yet ready for action, but he is a step away from talking to himself.
He decides to stage a play in which the actor will act out the scene
which is known only to his inner self. They are going to act out what he
knows because he can't do it. They are going to express for him. It is
not until act four that he asks, "O, from this time forth,/My thoughts
be bloody, or be nothing worth! [IV, iv, 65-66]" He never completely
embraces action, until it is to late: he is going to die.

This inability for action is one aspect which separates these two plays. Macbeth kills Duncan too hastily. Macbeth and his Lady spend most of the play trying hide what they have done, and stop it from aggravating their consciences. Hamlet, though he is far more justified (in the morals of the day) to kill his king, cannot do it, he is incapable of action. He needs a Lady Macbeth, who is undeniably responsible for pushing Macbeth to murder.

Lady Macbeth: Art thou afeard
To be the same in thine own act and valor
As thou art in desire? Wouldst thou have that
Which thou esteem'st the ornament of life,
And live a coward in thine own esteem,
Letting "I dare not" wait upon "I would,"

like the poor cat i' th' adage? [Macbeth, I, vii, 39-44.] This is Hamlet's problem, "I dare not" has control over "I would." It is paradoxical that the character who is least capable of transforming his desire into action is most able on the other side of the similarity, at turning his inner thoughts into words. He cannot however use these words to get himself understood by anyone in his kingdom, but gives them to us, his audience. This is literature. We, the audience are connecting with the inner thoughts of this character. We see the rest of the play through his eyes. His thoughts affect the way everyone views the rest of the play. Hamlet is not playing Shakespeare, the author's role, but we are reacting intimately to the world Shakespeare has created which is dominated by Hamlet.
