# Pebbles on a Beach: Joyce's Conglomeration of Themes in Ulysses

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Sybille Reichert  
> English 129-13  
> April 19, 1993  
> Pebbles on a Beach: Joyce's Conglomeration of Themes in Ulysses  

"The jingle a tingle jaunted," "dreamy creamy," "blue," "white," "dark," "dark horse," "bold hand Marion," "high grade ha," "gold from afar, bronze from anear," "tap," "toolaroo," "haw haw horn"... are all phrases (or sounds really) which any reader of James Joyce's Ulysses recognizes and which through repetition, combination and reformulation create a resonance equivalent to that derived from recognition of a line of Shakespeare, or of a song, or of the Bible, or of any other of the daunting number of texts to which Joyce alludes. The repeated themes referring to moments in the narrative are more interesting to me. My enjoyment of Ulysses grew as my repertoire of these sound combinations expanded. Joyce has brought a microscope to everyday life. Moments in the text (an observation or occurrence or sensation) may seem trivial but since they are repetitively referred to and thus become one of the many minor themes of the book, they become important and can take a spot center stage with the major themes such as Bloom the cuckold, Bloom and Stephen, Jewishness, Irish nationalism etc.. These minor themes make up the essential fabric of this text. They refer to and reflect and affect the evolution of the major themes. Items as trivial as a lemon soap Bloom has bought early in the day, because it makes constant reappearances, and even has a speaking part in the hallucinogenic play in chapter fifteen, or the pin Martha Clifford uses to hold the pages of her letter to Bloom together, which becomes associated to a song and also reoccurs in different lines of thought, become key players in this text. Tracing all of these themes is impossible so I have chosen two, seemingly unrelated, except that they both involve the sound combination "Throwaway" which is bound to bring them together.# In the beginning of chapter eight, Bloom is handed a pamphlet: A somber Y.M.C.A. young man, watchful among the warm sweet fumes of Graham Lemon's, placed a throwaway in the hand of Mr. Bloom.... [Bloom's] feet walked him slowly riverward, reading. Are you saved? All are washed in the blood of the lamb. God wants blood victim, birth, hymen, martyr, war, foundation of the building, sacrifice, kidney burntoffering, druid's altars. Elijah is coming. Dr. John Alexander Dowie restorer of the church of Zion is coming.

Is coming! Is coming!! Is coming!!!

All heartily welcome. [8.5-]#

This short passage is a good example of the associations made in a stream of consciousness, and of how several minor themes get intertwinded together, refer to each other, and affect the reader's interpretation of each because they are placed together. Bloom reads about the blood of the lamb in the pamphlet, birth, hymen, martyr are the natural flow of associations to blood or Christian ideas. Foundation of the building makes sense because the art represented in this chapter is supposedly architecture. The kidney refers to Bloom's breakfast. None of these associations are conclusive, they more closely resemble automatic reactions. One feeds off of the last. Hymen has to follw birth which produced the thought of the womb and the vaginal opening. Kidney (burntoffering) follows sacrifice or it wouldn't fit. Once the reader gets into this mind-set of constant associations, the "throwaway" which is placed in Bloom's hand cannot help but echo the name of a horse who is going to compete in the Ascot Gold cup that afternoon, but let us continue following the first "throw away"

Bloom crumples up the pamphlet and throws it to the seagulls hoping one of them will try to eat it:

He threw down among them [the seagulls] a crumpled paper ball. Elijah thirty two feet per sec is com. Not a bit. The ball bobbed unheeded on the the wake of swells, floated under by the bridgepiers. Not such damn fools. [8.57-]

One would expect the ball never to be mentioned again. It is in the river thrown away. But it pops up several times. It does not fall out of the sharp focus of this narrative so soon. We read in chapter ten:

A skiff, a crumpled throwaway, Elijah is coming, rode lightly down the Liffey, under the Loopline bridge, sailing eastward past hulls and anchorchains, between the Custom house old dock and George's quay. [10.294-]

The throwaway's journey is described just the way any other character's would be. One cannot say that anyone is observing its progression. It becomes the prime focus of the narrator in these passages. The journey continues:

Northwall and sir John Rogerson's quay, with hulls and anchorchains, sailing westward, sailed by a skiff, a crumpled throwaway, rocked on the ferry wash, Elijah is coming. [10.753-]

As these combinations of words and sounds are repeated, they take on their own meanings and Joyce can become freer with the language, he no longer needs the same number of articles, prepositions or conjunctions. The associations are automatically made by the reader who recognizes the meanings and mental images created by these groups of sounds: Elijah, skiff, light crumpled throwaway, sailed eastward by flanks of ships and trawlers, amid an archipelago of corks, beyond new Wapping street past Benson's ferry, and by the three masted schooner Rosevean from Bridgewater with bricks. [10.1095-]

The paper itself comes to its final destination here, we don't hear about it again, though the phrase "Elijah is coming" lives on as do the seagulls. They both reappear in Bloom's stream of consciousness and as characters in chapter fifteen. The phrase, "Elijah is com" resonates with Christian and Jewish notions. The Jewish faith does not accept Jesus Christ as their savior-- God's son on earth. They await the second coming of Elijah. One can only wonder whether Joyce purposefully blends the two religions when he repeats, Elijah is com (rather than coming) allowing the statement to refer both to the future and the past, a combination of Elijah has come (the Christian notion) and Elijah is coming (the Jewish one).

One still has to wonder, however, why Joyce follows the trajectory of this piece of paper, which seems a dead end. The association to major themes in the book can be made without the paper itself, the message is recorded in Bloom's memory. Why does it matter how the movement of the paper synchronizes with already complex movements in chapter ten? One does not usually follow a throwaway through its travels, but rather finds it at the end of its journey. If someone found this paper floating with the corks, and had enough interest to pick it up and read it they would imagine its journey from up the river, from the bridge from which it was thrown. Seeing a car wrapped around a tree by the side of the road initiates a recreation of the accident by the viewer. Seeing any piece of junk, or graffiti initiates the questions: How did it get there? Or, who owned this junk? Who painted this wall? Why? Joyce has expanded the range of the narrator's perception beyond anything we are used to. When Bloom is on the beach after viewing Gerty MacDowell, he:

...stooped and turned over a piece of paper on the strand [sic.]. He brought it near his eyes and peered. Letter? No. Can't read. Better go. Better. I'm tired to move. Page of an old copy book. All those holes and pebbles. Who could count them? Never know what you find. Bottle with a story of a treasure in it, thrown from a wreck. Parcels post. Children always want to throw things in the sea. Trust? Bread cast on waters. What is this? Bit of a stick. [13.1250-]

Bloom is interested. A child would imagine the crumpled ball's journey, having faith that it would be found. This attention is utterly beautiful and realistic. Bloom has picked up a piece of paper and a stick, with the childish hope that they could be important in some way. Could the paper be the very one he threw off the bridge? Or the stick the very stick which clung to Gerty's dress, or to the dress of the young woman Stephen saw on the beach? It doesn't matter, the association has been made. In this case and in most cases, the association is no more than that, it does not come to anything. The stick and the paper are important simply becasue they have been noticed. Bloom thinks of counting all the pebbles, making each important. Everyone he sees or picks up would become more important than its unnoticed neighbor. Bloom thinks of counting them all. Bloom's attention seems to be similar to Joyce's: Joyce does not want any part of this day to slip by unnoticed. The further the reader gets in the text, the closer the stream of consciousness in the text resembles his own, because as the reader's repertoire of allusions grows, he starts to make the same associations that will be made (and often have been made) in the text. The recognition of an association of two unrelated incidents in the narrative, by the reader, which is then explicitly stated in the text creates one of the most potent excitements of reading this book.

The other incident which involves "throwaway" is completely unrelated to the pamphlet floating on the Liffey. Their only connection is the shared word. Bantam Lyons approaches Bloom, he wants the paper to find out about the Ascot Gold cup race which will occur at three o'clock that afternoon, on which practically every man in the story is betting, except Bloom:

--Hello, Bloom. What's the best news? Is that today's [paper]? Show us a minute.

After a couple lines of Bloom's stream of consciousness, Bantam continues:

--I want to see about that French horse that's running today... Where
the bugger is it?
[Bloom's stream] Better leave him the paper and get shut of him.
--You can keep it, Bloom said.
--Ascot Gold cup. Wait, Bantam Lyon's muttered, half a mo. Maximum the
second.
--I was just going to throw it away, Mr. Bloom said.
Bantam Lyons raised his eyes suddenly and leered weakly.
--What's that? his sharp voice said.
--I said you can keep it, Mr. Bloom answered. I was going to throw it
away that moment.
Bantam Lyons doubted an instant, leering: then thrust the outspread
sheets back on Mr. Bloom's arms.
--I'll risk it he said. Here thanks. [5.532-]

Bloom doesn't realize it, but Bantam thinks that he has just been given a tip about that afternoon's horse race. A horse named Throwaway is one of the contenders, at the worst odds. This tip is discussed throughout the story, without Bloom ever realizing that he gave it. Even Blazes Boylan bets on Throwaway because of this tip. Lenehan, who set the odds for the paper says:

--I met Bantam Lyons going to back that horse [Throwaway] only I put him off if and he told me Bloom gave him the tip. Bet you what you like he has a hundred shillings to five on. He's the only man in Dublin has it. A dark horse.

--He's a bloody dark horse himself, says Joe. [12.1554-]

Throwaway ends up winning the race, and so Bloom's prediction becomes the talk of many, though he is still oblivious.

Throwaway is frequently referred to as an outsider: When asked who won, Lenehan says, "Throwaway ... at twenty to one. A rank outsider. And the rest nowhere. [12.1221-] Bella in chapter fifteen says, "Just my infernal luck, curse it. And that Goddamned outsider Throwaway at twenty to one. [15.2936-] Bloom has been called an outsider, he's Jewish and his father was Hungarian, and more frequently he is called a dark horse, or just dark. I have just quoted Lenehan and Joe in chapter twelve. When Gerty MacDowell notices Bloom we read, "she could see at once by his dark eyes and his pale intellectual face that he was a foreigner." [13.415-] The Gifford Annotations mention at some point that foreigners were called outsiders in Ireland at that time, so the connection between Bloom and the underdog horse, Throwaway, who wins at twenty to one odds is rather clear. In chapter seventeen, as the associations become more rampant, Bloom's tip and the pamphlet are put side by side as answers to the question: "Where had previous imitations of the result, effected or projected, been received by him?" (which I don't pretend to fully understand).

...outside Graham Lemon's when a dark man had placed in his hand a throwaway (subsequently thrown away), advertising Elijah, restorer of the church of Zion: in Lincoln place outside the premises of F.W. Sweny and Co. (Limited), dispensing chemists, when, when Frederick M. (Bantam) Lyons had rapidly and successively requested, perused and restituted the copy of the current issue of Freeman's Journal and National Press which he had been about to throw away (subsequently thrown away).... [17.331-]

The inevitable connection between the two throwaways has been made. To make further connections, in chapter fifteen, another melting pot of associations, Bloom says at one point: "Thirtytwo head over heals per second. Press nightmare. Giddy Elijah. Fall from cliff. Sad end of government printer's clerk." [15.3375] We have to assume he is thinking of himself as the crumpled piece of paper--he has already been metaphorically associated to Elijah [12.1910-] as has the paper. He is the government printer's clerk, and the head over heals implies someone falling. The Nameless One of chapter fifteen emphasizes this combination of themes: "Arse over tip. Hundred shillings to five." [15.1149] Bloom, the horse and the crumpled paper are all throwaways.

Such comparisons make it very easy to make a statement like: Bloom is an outsider, a throw away, and Joyce chooses to make him the center of attention the same way he focuses on the crumpled piece of paper in the river and allows an unknown horse to win the race. Of course Joyce makes such a statement possible, but he makes a lot of other statements as possible. The individual statement is not nearly as interesting as the whole network of relating, compared and evolving associations. Joyce has purposely put hundreds of minor occurrences into the lime light with the major themes, and allows them all to interact in the same metaphorical ways, and thus affect how they are each understood. He resembles Bloom on the beach, he notices the sticks and bits of paper. He does not always, however come to a conclusion about their importance. Te asscoiation is enough. The associations and connections are recognized, and make up the body of the text. They also move the reader through the text, by guiding his stream of consciousness so that it gradually gets closer to resembling those represented in the text. The reader is exposed to a type of simulated reality, some what like being in someone's mind and sharing their thoughts, which I find to be the major achievement of this book.
