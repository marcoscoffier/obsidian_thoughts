# Reality in a Glass

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Lit. 120: Narrative Forms  
> Section-- Sandra Ferdman  
> Nov. 2, 1992  
> Reality in a Glass  

There are two main conflicting developed themes in Hoffman's "The Sandman". One, after which the story is named is that of the Sandman and how this image haunts the narrator and finally drives him through insanity to his death. The image, or theme of the Sandman whose power is drawn from the uncanny way in which it reoccurs throughout the narrator's life is studied in detail in Freud's essay, "The Uncanny". Freud uses Hoffman's essay as his principle model and example of the uncanny. He chooses to discuss the theme of the Sandman over that of the doll as his guide for the uncanny:

> ...I cannot think... that the theme of the doll Olympia, who is to all appearances a living being , is by any means the only element to be held responsible for the quite unparalleled atmosphere of the uncanniness which the story evokes; or, indeed, that it is the most important among them.#

He goes on to discuss the uncanny in how it pertains to this theme of the Sandman. Later in his argument, he even further separates the Sandman theme from that of the animated wooden doll, Olympia, when he says: The story teller... can select his world of representation so that it either coincides with realities we are familiar with or departs from them in what particulars he pleases. We accept his ruling in every case. In fairy tales, for instance, the animistic system of beliefs is frankly adopted. Wish-fulfilments, secret powers, omnipotence of thoughts, animation of lifeless objects, all the elements so common in fairy stories can exert no influence here; for, as we have learnt, that feeling cannot arise unless there is a conflict of judgment whether things which have been "surmounted" are regarded as incredible are not, after all, possible and this problem is excluded from the beginning by the setting of the story.... [As soon as the writer pretends to move into the world of common reality]...he accepts all the conditions operating to produce uncanny feelings in real life; and everything that would have an uncanny effect in reality has it in his story. #

He is essentially saying that any uncanny feeling that the story of the Sandman can produce in the reader is usurped if the reader does not believe he is reading realities. Set in a fairy tale none of the aspects of the Sandman would seem uncanny, or even exert any influence on the reader. One is forced to ask the question what kind of world does Hoffman create? The answer is not an easy one. On the one hand we have the psychological destruction of the subject, Nathanael, realistic and accurate enough to attract Freud's attention and for Freud to psychoanalyze-- not the subject of fairy tales. On the other, we are given scenes straight out of the "Nutcracker" ballet, Nathanael's hands and feet are unscrewed, the woman he falls in love with is an automated wooden doll. Where Freud chose to concentrate on the Sandman theme, I will choose that of these automatons-- a few short instances which glare out and essentially ruin what could be a reality, which would allow the reader to enjoy the uncanny moments of the Sandman theme. I will not attempt to prove that the Sandman theme is realistic, but for simplicity's sake assume that all the uncanny moments in the development of Nathanael's insanity could explained in some realistic way were it not for those few moments which I am going to discuss.

The text begins with a letter-- actually three letters. It will not take much argument to prove that a letter is the most realistic way a text could begin. The narrator is not telling us about the main character, that character is telling us about himself--this is a primary document. With such a beginning the reader is primed for the realistic. A fairy tale doesn't start with a letter. One can expect a text to be fantastic, but not a letter. A letter is a document which is circulated in real life to send information or news. A narrative deals with the world of fantasy; even at its most realistic, it is an escape from reality. The reader here is misdirected by the first letter (which is misdirected itself--sent to Klara instead of Lothar). The letter is form Nathanael to his friend, Lothar. He describes a childhood fear which has recently been rekindled--that of the Sandman who comes at night to steal children's eyes. The story runs smoothly. As Klara states in her letter (the next), everything that seems fantastic is fully accounted for: Naturally, your childish mind associated the dreadful Sandman of the nurse's tale with old Coppelius--who would have been a monster particularly threatening to children even if you had not believed in the Sandman.#

She claims his reaction is natural. One does not have to be Freud to agree with her. She even goes on to de-mystify the mysterious activities of his father and Coppelius which lead to an explosion, his father's death and the disappearance of Coppelius:

> The sinister business conducted at night with your father was probably nothing other than secret alchemical experiments, which displeased your mother because not only was a great deal of money being squandered, but... your father's mind was so imbued with illusory desire for higher knowledge that he may have become alienated form his family. ...Yesterday I asked our neighbor, an experienced chemist, whether experiments of this kind could possibly lead to such a sudden lethal explosion. "Absolutely," he replied....#

The first instance which Klara doesn't explain, and which arouses our suspicion, is when just as Nathanael, hiding in his father's study, has made the connection that "The Sandman, the horrible Sandman, was the old lawyer Coppelius... the most horrible figure could not have filled me with deeper horror than this very Coppelius."# His association is readily confirmed. Coppelius says "Give me eyes! Give me eyes!" This is too coincidental and extraordinary to still seem real. But, even this statement could be explained by a misunderstanding as the same statement is explained by Coppola later in the story. The addition of eyes to the associations between the image he has of the Sandman and Coppelius is a final blow. It prompts Nathanael to scream out in fear and reveal himself. "Now we have eyes, eyes a beautiful pair of children's eyes!" Coppelius whispers, and is about to sprinkle glowing grains from the fire into them when Nathanael's father cries,"Master! Master!...Let the child keep his eyes." "...but now," Coppelius shrieks, "we must carefully observe the mechanism of the hands and feet." and now I quote:

> He thereupon seized me so violently that my joints cracked, unscrewed my hands and feet, then put them back now this way then another. "There's something wrong here! It's better the way they were! The Old Man knew his business!" Coppelius hissed and muttered.#

There is no possible explanation, at least not from the realistic framework from which we have been expecting in this letter, for this unscrewing of the hands and feet. Unscrewing of the hands and feet is not an image associated with the Sandman, nor is it possible. It is highly inconsistent with what could be realism in the text. The possibility of an action would, of course, not be an issue had the reader not been lead to believe that he was dealing with a real situation. A partial explanation and recapturing of reality is achieved in Klara's letter. But one is still forced to ask what Hoffman intends for this text? Where is it trying to lie-- in the world of truth or that of fairy tales? The answer does not come easily.

After the opening letters the reader is surprised to find a narrative voice talking directly to him--another inconsistency. This voice tells us that Nathanael is his friend and that though no one asked him about the "history of young Nathanael", he is "most strongly compelled" to tell us "about Nathanael's disastrous life." These phrases seem to refer to a reality. He is going to tell us about his friend Nathanael's life-- not just tell us, he is going to give us a history of his life. One can assume that since Nathanael was his friend he is telling us at least some information from first hand knowledge of these events. This seems to imply that he experienced this story in real life, and that there can be no fantastic in it. I am, of course, putting real life in opposition to the fantastic, I am sure that unless any mystics are reading this, such a juxtaposition is valid. The reader has been misdirected again, the narrator immediately refutes this thought as it was refuted in the first letter by saying:

> The marvelous and extraordinary aspects of his life entirely captivated my soul; but precisely for this reason and because my dear reader, it was essential at the beginning to dispose you favorably to the fantastic--which is no mean matter-- I tormented myself to devise a way to devise a way to begin Nathanael's story....#

This sentence is pregnant with interesting points to which we will return, but for now let us note that the history of Nathanael has now become Nathanael's story. Not only that, but a history is not devised, and history does not include the fantastic, which our narrator says is essential. The narrator then suggests three beginnings: "Once upon a time..." he finds too prosaic, "In the small provincial town of S-----, there lived..." he finds better, and finally he suggests "‘Go to Hell!' the student Nathanael cried, his eyes wild with rage and terror..." but he finds that there is "something humorous in Nathanael's wild look-- but the story is not at all comic." He continues, and this is the most important part, "There were no words I could find which were appropriate to describe, even in the most feeble way, the most brilliant colors of my inner vision. I resolved not to begin at all."# Each of the beginnings he tries would be fit to begin a story, but not a history. Once upon a time is the beginning of a fairy tale, a history is set in time. He is right that in the small town of blank is better, but this lacks the impact he wants to get across. Even if the "Go to Hell," beginning were the beginning of a history, the narrator or the historian would have to be inventing part of the scene because neither of them were there. Nathanael's wild look would be an assumption. The narrator chooses not to create a beginning, not to begin at all. He chooses not to create the beginning of the story as he does the rest of the text, but to let it begin, as a history. He allows the real life, or the closest he has to a real reproduction of those scenes speak for itself. He does not interpret the beginning. Again we are led to believe that the story is set in a realistic tone, but are now led to a union of what we have been considering too opposites: As a good portrait painter, I may possibly succeed in making Nathanael recognizable even if the the original is unknown to you: and you may feel as if you had seen him with your own eyes on very many occasions. Possibly, also, you will come to believe that real life is more singular and more fantastic than anything else and that all a writer can do is present it as "in a glass, darkly."#

Let us return to discussing the possibility of the coexistence of the real world and the fantastic in light of the second instance of automated humanity-- the story of Olympia.

Nathanael gets caught between the world of reality and that of the fantastic. These are defined in reference to the world in which he is placed. There are fantastic occurrences in each. There are two places in the story where Nathanael slips into a world which I call the fantastic because it is not consistent with the world as everyone around him perceives it. The first time is when he goes home shortly after having seen Coppola the optician for the first time.

[That the barometer dealer Coppola had exercised a disastrous influence in his life] was evident to everyone for even in the first few days of his visit, Nathanael seemed completely changed.#

I is also said that:

> Nathanael had formerly possessed a notable talent for writing delightful and amusing stories... now, however, his tales were gloomy, unintelligible, and shapeless.#

His departure from everyone around him becomes more and more marked until at the end of his stay he finds himself in a duel with Lothar, his best friend who, "reproached him for his insane behavior." And now I quote, "Nathanael, incensed, answered in kind, ‘Crazy, conceited fool'...# They are each calling the other crazy. Their realities are inconsistent. Nathanael because of the intervention of Klara returns to the same reality as his loved ones.

Nathanael felt as if a heavy burden which had weighed him to the ground had been lifted, as if by resisting the dark powers that had gripped him he had saved his whole being from the threat of utter ruin.#

Before going on do discussing the Olympia sequence which I stated was to be the most important in this paper, there is one final point which has to be made, that is about Klara, the force which consistently brings Nathanael back to the world of reason. It is important to discuss Klara before getting to Olympia because they are opposites of each other in many ways. The narrator says:

> Dreamers and visionaries had bad luck with her [Klara]; for despite the fact that she said little... her clear glance and her rare ironical smile asked: "Dear friends, how can you suppose that I will accept these fleeting and shadowy images for true shapes which are alive and breathe?" For this reason many chided Klara for being cold, without feeling and unimaginative; but others, those whose conception of life was clearer and deeper,were singularly enamored of this tenderhearted, intelligent, and child-like girl....#

These two ways of thinking about Klara can be paralleled to the two worlds between which Nathanael is caught. At the height of his first trip to the fantastic which we were discussing earlier, before he gets into the duel, he writes a poem about the Sandman who comes and tears his lover's eyes out at the altar, and throws them at his chest. He reads it to Klara and collapses in a fit. "Klara pressed him tenderly to her bosom and said in a voice at once very slow and somber: ‘Nathanael, my darling Nathanael, throw that mad, insane, stupid tale into the fire." Nathanael cries out, "You damned, life-less automaton," and runs out. Klara is at her warmest. Nathanael thinks she is the coldest she is ever mentioned in this text-- a damned lifeless automaton.

After recovering and re-finding to his normal self, he returns to school. Klara is still with him: "...with Klara in his heart, he remained perfectly indifferent to the stiff and rigid Olympia."# He is even protected when Coppola comes to visit him again: "Remembering... what he had solemnly promised his sweetheart regarding the Sandman Coppelius, he felt ashamed of his childish fear of ghosts and forceably pulled himself together.... Coppola pulls out some eyeglasses for him to try, Nathanael starts to go crazy, but " as soon as the eyeglasses were gone Nathanael grew calm again, and focusing his thoughts on Klara, he clearly saw that this gruesome illusion had been solely the product of his own mind....# In a moment of near calmness, he buys a spyglass from Coppola, which, unfortunately, becomes the instrument which drags him back to the insanity which Klara has been able to ward off. Olympia becomes transformed through the spyglass:

> ...as he continued to look more and more intently through the glass, it seemed as though moist moonbeams were beginning to shine in Olympia's eyes. It seemed as if the power of vision were only now starting to be kindled; her glances were inflamed with ever increasing life.#

The same mechanism which turned Klara cold when he started slipping into his world of fantasy is know warming up Olympia-- that which turned Klara lifeless is giving life to her opposite. Nathanael gets hopelessly separated from the safety of Klara's love and fall hopelessly in love with Olympia. he has the same type of disagreement with a friend here about Olympia as he did with Lothar about Klara. A good friend of his Siegmund is worried about how the entire town is laughing at Nathanael's new love-- "that wax-faced wooden puppet across the way." he says: [Olympia] seems to us-- don't take this badly, my brother-- strangely stiff and soulless. ...if her eyes were not so completely devoid of life... she might be considered beautiful. Her step is particularly measured; all of her movements seem to stem form some kind of clockwork. her playing and her singing are unpleasantly perfect, being as lifeless as a music box.... She seems to us to be playing the part of a human being, and it's as though there were something hidden behind all of this.#

This is absolutely opposite from the description we were given of Klara, who was tenderhearted and child-like (there is nothing hidden about a child). Nathanael is angry at his friend: "Olympia may indeed seem weird to you cold and unimaginative mortals." He is calling his friends cold and unimaginative, the same way he called Lothar crazy, when in fact it is he who is cold and unimaginative and crazy.

This section about Olympia adds quite another problem in the question of finding where is the reality in this text. There has been a reversal, from Nathanael transforming a human, Klara, into an automaton, to his transformation of an automaton into a human. The first case is far less problematic because the automaton did not really exist. It was a figment of Nathanael's imagination. Now, however, Nathanael's imagination seems more realistic than what we have been taking to be world of reality. The fantastic is lying in our reality, and we are brought back to the problem of reconciling the two which the narrator suggested in the beginning. Klara saved Nathanael by saying:

> Yes, Nathanael, you are right; Coppelius is an evil and malignant principle. His effect can be no less diabolical than the very powers of hell if they assume living form, but only if you fail to banish him from your thoughts. He will exist and work on you only as long as you believe in him; it is only your belief which gives him power.#

In his own poem Nathanael has Klara save him from the horrible illusion he has of Coppelius tearing her eyes out. He hears her voice: "That which burned your breast were not my eyes. Those were fiery drops of blood from your own heart. Look at me I have still got my own eyes." Nathanael thought: "It is Klara: I am her's forever." Then it was as though this thought had grasped the fiery circle and forced it to stop turning....#

We have no Klara to reconcile the fantastic creation of our narrator, but we have a clue which she left us-- it is all in his mind. The narrator begins talking after the three letters by posing the reader a question. "Have you, gentle reader, ever experienced anything which totally possessed your heart, your thoughts, and your senses to the exclusion of all else?"# We might assume he is talking about how the Sandman possessed Nathanael, but he continues: "And wishing to describe the picture in your mind with all it's vivid colors, the light and the shade, you struggled vainly to find the words.... If like an audicious painter, you had initially sketched the outline of the picture within you in a few bold strokes, you would easily have been able to make the colors deeper and more intense until the multifarious crowd of living shapes swept your friends away and they saw themselves as you see yourself, in the midst of the scene that had issued from your soul."# It must be obvious now that he is not talking about Nathanael at all. Nathanael doesn't care what those around him think. Our narrator is talking about himself. He has a scene inside himself which is totally possessing his soul the way the image of the Sandman possessed Nathanael's soul. Could it be that Nathanael's story is forcing our narrator to the fantastic the same way in which the image of the Sandman forced Nathanael to the fantastic? I propose that is so. It is the only way of reconciling the fantastic and the realistic in this text. This text (and any text) is itself fantastic, and the product of someone's mind. This brings us back to the original problem in this paper, and a quote which I have already used: As a good portrait painter, I may possibly succeed in making Nathanael recognizable even if the the original is unknown to you: and you may feel as if you had seen him with your own eyes on very many occasions. Possibly, also, you will come to believe that real life is more singular and more fantastic than anything else and that all a writer can do is present it as "in a glass, darkly."#

We have never seen Nathanael's original, and that is a problem. Our narrator's portrait of Nathanael is going to be created from the image he has inside of him, and thus we know is going to be warped because this image has possessed his soul, as the Sandman had Nathanael's. The few instances were human beings become automatons are the only instances of the fantastic experiences in the text which are not easily explained as relating to the psychological consequences of Nathanael's obsession with the Sandman. The first two instances can be described being figments of Nathanael's insanity as they are both experienced and recounted by him. In the case of Olympia, the system is reversed. The human Olympia resides in Nathanael's insanity, the automaton in what has been the real world. An examination of this inconsistency with the rest of the reality leads us to the conclusion that what we have taken as the real world, and what our narrator calls real life in the aforementioned quote, cannot be. It has been created by another mind and thus is a representation of the real world seen "in a glass darkly". We can imagine the glass creating the fantastic by warping the real image inside it. The darkness exists because of the impossibility of saying exactly everything that one feels or perceives and thus the impossibility of allowing full light on the reality. Freud will back us in sying that darkness is fertile ground for the creation of the fantastic.
