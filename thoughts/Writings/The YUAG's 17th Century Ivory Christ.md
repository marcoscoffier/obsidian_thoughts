# The YUAG's 17th Century Ivory Christ

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof.s Hollander and Forge  
> Word and Image  
> March 21, 1994  
> The YUAG's 17th Century Ivory Christ  

This ivory figurine is obviously of Christ, but its arms have been broken off so the iconographic message is less powerful than it would have been. It is as though chance has picked out the most human part of what used to be an icon and underlined it by removing the most blatantly iconographic element, the spread arms. In my mind, the entropic passage of time has added to the power of this art work. We are reminded of the material from which it was made and its inevitable decay
The sculptor did a splendid job giving to this ivory the illusion of a living body. The stomach and rib cage of this figurine are the most memorable part. One can imagine a kind of life in these tiny bones. I think of a lab rat who has been splayed out on a dissecting table. Its softest side up. One can see the fleshy stomach verses the hard cartilagy ribs, and can imagine the vulnerability of a living form. One does not always think of the crucifix in such human terms, but in this case, the arms have been cracked and the message of life which the representation of a rid cage contains becomes more powerful than the religious message. I am reminded of the first changes made in the representation of this icon at the beginning of the Italian Renaissance, when artists like Cimabue began to humanize the Christ image, and ended up making a new set of rules for representing this icon. Christ's no longer looks out at the viewer, but his head drops in exhaustion, or in the clutches of the beginning of death. The body was modified too: rather than imitate the rigid lines of the cross, it swung to one side as the artists started to think about what it must have been like to hang from a cross. These artists even developed a new way of depicting Christ's stomach muscles and the rib cage.

In the same way that the breaks in the ivory, by removing some of the iconography, emphasize what the artist chose to represent as human in this object, they subvert its power as the representation of a human being--this is unmistakably a piece of ivory. I can see the breaks and grain of this tusk. An elephant used to have this piece stuck on its face. He used it to knock down trees. One day in the 17th century, this elephant was killed. Small sinuous men rushed around the collapsed mass of rough gray flesh and cut its tusks off. The fleshy carcass was left bleeding on the sand next to a bush, a huge breeding cavity for flies. The tusk was sent to Europe, where an artist picked it up off a worn wooden table to get a closer look of it in the light of the window--a long shiny tooth with a gentle curve. How could he resist making the body curve the way the tusk curved? a slow bend to one side. The artist attached arms to this piece, completed the legs and fixed it onto a wooden cross. It had hung in a dark cold church reflecting the warm candle glow, and the light off the upturned faces of the devotees. It stood in the slowly rising smoke of incense and prayers, witness to the solemn chants and movements of generations of services. Then, a war? a fire? had dislodged it from its post. It was lost or buried for years, until someone roaming through the country side, or the wreckage of an old church had seen a shiny piece of something, a rock? a pearl? and went to pick it up. When someone picks up a stone or a stick out of the millions of stones or sticks there are to pick up, the particular one they grab becomes one tiny theme in the fabric of their lives for a minute or an hour, or for as long as they leave it on their dresser. This individual was in for a surprise. After scraping some mud away the size of this thing became obvious. What was it?

Just a stick?
Let's take it to the fountain.
Look a rib cage--
What is this, some kind of petrified animal?
No, look there's a head
This looks like a Christ figure
How long has it been buried?
Gosh I don't know... years.

An icon is a kind of visual sign. The inherently repetitive message of an icon cannot help but lose its power and even its importance the same way a linguistic sign which at first may be a new, ingenious and more precise way of naming some concept looses its novelty and thus much of the precision of its meaning in its repetition. The term "barbecue", for example, comes from the French "barbe au cul" (beard to ass) which are the two points in the anatomy of an animal through which one has to pass a skewer to hang it above a fire. God only knows what meanings the sign "barbecue" and even "BBQ" have taken on today--probably something involving charcoal lighter and very far from involving two extremities of an animal carcass. The same generalizing process has sofened the meanings of many linguistic signs and the ever broadening concepts they represent. This discussion is quickly exceeding the boundaries of my limited knowledge of etymology and semantic change, suffice it to say that there is a similarity in the egregious repetition of a linguistic sign and that of a visual symbol--their meanings become more generalized. The crucifix falls prey to this broadening of meaning--to one person it means hope and the possibility of an after life, to another it is the symbol which is carried into battle high over the shoulders of his enemy. This symbol has become more than the representation of a man nailed onto a cross.

Artists in the renaissance chose to re-humanize their icons, but certain symbols had to remain. Time has removed some of these from this icon and has reaffirmed its effect for the modern viewer as a work of art, created by a man, out of a natural material.
