# Hopper and Covert in the Terms of the Progression of the Modernist Movement

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Jonathan Weinberg  
> 20th c. American Art  
> Michael Lobel --Section.  
> Hopper and Covert in the Terms of the Progression of the Modernist  
> Movement  

In his essay "Towards a Newer Laocoon," Clement Greenberg states, "My own experience of art has forced me to accept most of the standards of taste from which abstract art has derived, but I do not maintain that they are the only valid standards through eternity." My views are almost the opposite, I can understand and even enjoy abstract art, but the paintings which move me the most are figurative or representational. The two paintings I have chosen to discuss in terms of his essays are John Covert's "Will, Intellect, Sensation, Emotion," and Edward Hopper's "Rooms by the Sea", neither of which is abstract, but both of which have qualities similar to those he has described as being key to his view of the historical progression of avant-garde art. John Covert's painting operates mostly as a line drawing in paint. There are slight value and color variations but these serve only to emphasize the shapes defined by the lines which circumscribe them. In the "Newer Laocoon", Greenberg says, that in modernism, "Line, which is one of the most abstract elements in painting since it is never found in nature as the definition of contour, returns to oil painting..." Line itself may be abstract, but Covert's simple smooth curving lines create rather bulbous shapes which can be seen as three general receding planes of a prostrate person: the head, the shoulders and arms and the legs and hips. The shapes created are not so much representative of the human body as they are reminiscent of it, but they unmistakably describe a figure. Greenberg has a view on representation which is not as simple as one might think, he says in "Modernist Painting":

It is not in principle that Modernist painting... has abandoned the representation of recognizable objects. What it has abandoned in principle is the representation of the kind of space that recognizable, three-dimensional objects can inhabit. Abstractness, or the non-figurative, has in itself still not proved to be an altogether necessary moment in the self-criticism of pictorial art.... Three dimensionality is the province of sculpture, and for the sake of its own autonomy painting has had above all to divest itself of everything it might share with sculpture.

In the sense of this last paragraph, Covert's painting does not fit into Greenberg's scheme of development. It is quite sculptural. In a previous paper solely on this work, I described the feeling of gravity one gets from the way the figure's imaginary spinal column flops like a palm fond to the right of the picture. Though Greenberg does not describe it explicitly, gravity must be a quality which he would attribute to sculpture and not painting. Furthermore, except for some tiny floor lines, the space for this figure is not described. Unlike Greenberg, I find that this makes the shapes in this painting more sculptural; they exist on their own. A sculpture is a slave to the space in which it is placed. Covert has left the space empty, ideal way not to draw attention away form the sculptural forms. A completely representational painting creates the world around the object which is its subject.

Hopper's "Room's by the Sea" is in many ways the opposite of Covert's painting. Space is the object, and light, not gravity, is the external force which is depicted for the viewer. In prominence in the middle of the painting is the representation of a blank white wall upon which sunlight is cast through an open doorway. The overall composition, deprived of all its associations is large pale color shapes. A purple stripe down the right hand side of the painting, which we read as the shadow behind the door, followed by a pale green-blue sliver which we read as the door. next is a blue shape which is cut in half darker blue with thicker paint and whitish undulations on the bottom, which we read as waves and pale warm blue on top--the sky. One can continue mapping the shapes and what we take them to represent for the whole painting, and for any painting for that matter, any painting is really only made up of carefully arranged color shapes. Hopper's painting emphasizes this fact. In the middle of the canvas, larger than anything else are four shapes: one mostly triangular blue gray with a thin purple glaze; below it of much lighter value one which changes from almost pure white near the top, to pink and blue; below this also very light, a yellow shape; and below this, a darker greenish-ochre sliver which extends horizontally across almost the entire bottom of the picture. These shapes do create the illusion of sunlight a wall and the floor. Essentially they are large areas of color. In many ways, though he does not give up illusionism or a value system, Hopper partakes in the ideals which Greenberg ascribes to Barnett Newman, and Mark Rothko in "After Abtract-Expressionism". He says that:

...by renouncing tactility , and detail in drawing, Newman and Rothko achieve what I find a more positive openness and colour [than Clifford Still]. The rectilinear [which he has mentioned these two artists keep ambiguous] is kept open by definition: it calls the least attention to drawing and gets the least in the way of colour space. A thin paint surface likewise gets the least in the way of colour space, by excluding tactile associations."

The large majority of the shapes in "Rooms by the Sea" are larger than an inch. Detail is not the strong part of Hopper's art, one has only to look at the rather clumsy way he paints figures in other paintings. The figures do not interest him as much as the space they inhabit and the light which brings this space to life. "Rooms by the Sea" is one of the strongest examples of Hooper's concern with space. Though many of the shapes are on a diagonal, Hopper's painting is rectilinear and not ambiguously. Perhaps another painting of Hopper's such as "Early Sunday Morning" is a better example of the true rectilinearity which permeates his art. Also the paint surface is very thin. It is thickest in the lowest part of the water where the waves are depicted, but even this treatment is thin if one were to compare this handling of water with Childe Hassam's "The West Wind, Isles of Shoals" where the paint is so thick, that the highlights which give this surface the shimmery appearance of water are produced by the reflection of the museum lights off of the thickest parts. The surface of Hopper's painting is very smooth. Textures are not represented. The wall, the door frame, and even the couch and the water have nearly the same slick, cool texture. What is most carefully thought about are the different colors which each of the areas in this painting take on to give a beautiful representation of the quality of the light. The most stunning and obvious example of this attention is the difference in color of the two areas of most intense sunlight-- on the principle wall, and on the smaller wall in the tiny sliver of room to the left, which are a pinkish/yellow and blue and pure pale greenish-blue respectively. Because of our prior experience of looking, we the viewer know that this is discrepancy in color is caused by the fact that the second light is passing through a window. The fact that the color in this window-lit room is treated in a completely different way than that in the larger main room (combined with the fact that the ceiling line is shown in the smaller room and that it is so cramped) gives the viewer a greater feeling of openness in the front room, whose door is flung open to the elements. Greenberg says that: ...the ultimate effect sought [in Newman and Rothko's paintings] is one of more than chromatic intensity; it is one of an almost literal openness that embraces and absorbs colour in the act of being created by it.

Hopper's painting gives me a more tangible feeling of openness through the use of color than Rothko or Newman precisely because I can relate my own experiences to this image, which enhance the effect these color shapes would have on me.

I have only discussed a few of Greenberg's views, those which I felt most directly relate to the work I have chosen to discuss. The issue of the flatness of the picture plane is too involved to cover in such a restricted space, though I can note that Hopper's space is very shallow which accentuates those effects he achieves which are similar to those sought after by Newman and Rothko especially. The point I disagree most strongly with Greenberg, or modernism I suppose, is that any art form has to be "pure". One of the most wonderful aspects of painting is that it can give the illusion of three-dimensional space on a flat picture plane, and that an image can have many associations. These are qualities inherent to the art of painting, not just borrowed from sculpture and literature. Abstract art made advances in the world of "pure painting" which can be applied to non-abstract art. I am most moved when the ideals are returned to the world of representational painting.
