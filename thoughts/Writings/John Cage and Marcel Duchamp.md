# John Cage and Marcel Duchamp

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Katerina Clark  
> The Avant Garde  
> Dec 14, 1994  
> John Cage and Marcel Duchamp  

I propose that there are similarities in the work of Marcel Duchamp and John Cage but that due largely to the fact that they work in two different mediums and that though their careers overlap, they do not coincide( by the time Cage meets him, Duchamp has already made his greatest works) --it is extremely difficult to draw exact parallels between their works. Duchamp and Cage are similar in that they both have tremendously high artistic standards and a coherent aesthetic derived from specific choices about the nature of reality and art. There are times when it can be said that they were making the same moves in each of their respective mediums: art and music. But those are few and I will concentrate only on the issues surrounding Cage's indeterminacy and Duchamp's readymades. In both of those stages in their art they were concerned with removing the presence of the artist from the creation of the work of art, which could be seen as the culmination of the centuries old art historical tradition of realism. The real world has infiltrated the art object to such a point that there is no longer any distinction. William Wordsworth has certainly not gotten to that level of reality, he still believes the art object ( the poem in his case) to be at best a translation of the real world. Starting in this tradition of realism can give a little perspective on the issues Duchamp and Cage come to confront. In 1798 in the Preface to Lyrical Ballads Wordsworth says:

> The principle object, then, which I proposed to myself in these poems was to choose incidents and situation from common life, and to relate or describe them, throughout, as far as was possible, in a selection of language really used by men; and at the same time, to throw over them a certain colouring of the imagination, whereby ordinary things should be presented to the mind in an unusual way....#

This passage adumbrates Victor Shklovsky's technique of ‘defamiliarization' as the technique of art where art is the process of making one see the life that surrounds him but which he cannot see because he has become blinded by habit. Duchamp and Cage have gone one step further than Shklovsky's "defamiliarization" or Wordsworth's colouring of the description so that the presentation can be original. They do not bother to present the ordinary moment in an interesting way. For them it is enough simply to present. Each of them tries to get as little in the way of the transformation of the real world sound or object into a work of art. When Cage was asked what his next step would be, he answered:

> I think the work will resemble more and more, not the work of a person, but something that might have happened even if the person weren't there.#

No contextualization or frame can ever be completely escaped. In Duchamp's Readymades the frame, Shklovsky's "interesting presentation", is the gallery setting, or simply the fact that a man who calls himself an artist is presenting an object as a work of art. In Cage's, 4'33" the frame is the concert hall a stage with a piano, a pianist, and the division of a piece into movements. The pianist at the first presentation of this piece was David Tudor, who frequently played Cage's compositions for the Merce Cunningham Dance company. He sat at the piano and at the appropriate times to mark off the beginning and the three movements of the piece, he raised his arms as though about to play, but he never played. The sound of the piece was all the sounds the audience heard during that time--a neighbor coughing, a truck or fire engine passing by outside, etc.. The sounds were real life sounds, absolutely unaltered by the artist, simply framed by him. John Cage says:

art is not an attempt to bring order out of chaos... but simple a way of waking up to the very life we're living, which is so excellent once one gets one's mind and one's desire out of its way and let's it act of its own accord...

If everyone appreciated the world we do actually live in --we wouldn't need art.#

Duchamp echoes this argument when he says:

> I would have wanted to work, but deep down I'm enormously lazy. I like living, breathing, better than working. I don' think that the work I've done can have any social importance in the future. Therefore, if you wish, my art would be that of the living: each second, each breath is a work which is inscribed nowhere, which is neither visual nor cerebral. It's a sort of constant euphoria.#

One of Cage's most interesting developments is his idea of indeterminacy a concept which actually culminates in the infamous 4'33". The artist's release of control on the outcome of the piece of music produces indeterminacy. The beginning glimmerings of this in Cage's music is with the prepared piano works. Cage would put all sorts of objects between the piano wires and on the dampers to alter the sound the piano would make. Each of these alterations was carefully noted and kept in a score. When he actually went to the piano to write the piece he did not know what sound would be produced by which note. He created a system where the alterations to the instrument are known and music was written to tell the player which notes to hit, just like a regular piece of music, but the sound of the piece, the final effect was unpredictable. The prepared piano works however, are absolutely repeatable. The next step in the history of indeterminacy was a score which gave much less strict directions to the players. It would contain instructions such as between four and eight minutes into the performance, the percussionist should hit the symbol. In the piece Concerto for Piano and Orchestra, the pianist is given 84 different musical passages sketches really and asked to play any selection he desires in any order, and he doesn't have to play anything if he doesn't want to. In his later scores, Cage includes no music only prose descriptions of directions. It is obvious that no two performances of these pieces could ever be the same. Their outcome is in the hands of the performers. Even this is not pure indeterminacy. One could get a general idea of what one performance would sound like depending on the group of musicians which is chosen. Arthur Rubenstein would surely play something recognizably different from an untrained player. In 4'33'' no performance can be predicted or recognized as having been performed by a particular pianist. Cage said many years after he composed the piece that he could play it or any part of it in his mind any time. The piece thus has truly become all the world of sound.

When Duchamp drops a string to create his measuring system of three standard stoppages, he is using indeterminacy. He has no idea how the strings are going to fall. He used these stoppages as the building rudiments of sections of two of his most complete works: the mural, ‘Tu m'' (at the Yale University Art Gallery) and in the ‘Large Glass'. He tried to capture nature's indeterminacy when he varnished the accumulated dust onto his canvases. One could also draw a comparison between 4'33" and Duchamp's ‘Large Glass': The people walking through the museum and the ever changing light of day make this transparent painting look different every instant and it could theoretically contain the entire visual world in the same way that Cage's piece can own the entire world of sound.

John Cage always held Duchamp in such high esteem, that it is hard to gather anything from his comments on Duchamp. Anything Duchamp does is remarkable according to Cage. Reading the interview he had with Moria and William Roth one cannot help but be overcome by Cage's deep reverence for the older mysterious artist. In that interview he says:

> At a Dada exhibition in Dusseldorf, I was impressed that though Schwitters and Picabia and the others had become artist with the passing of time, Duchamp's works remained unacceptable as art. And, in fact, as you look from Duchamp to the light fixture, (pointing in the room) the first thought you have is, "Well that's a Duchamp." That's what I think and that doesn't lead me to any questions. It leads me to the enjoyment of my life. #

Of course one has to realize that the first thought which is described is actually John Cage's impression, not every one's. In fact because Duchamp was so specific about the number of Readymades he allowed and about the objects he picked, I don't believe many people have the reaction that Duchamp's readymades brought to their attention the fact that any mass produced item could be a Duchamp. Duchamp's careful choice of objects and even of their placement which is inevitable in their presentation-- the urinal especially was transformed when it was placed on its side--makes them quite susceptible to a type of idolization. Because the work of art is so carefully chosen( even though it is chosen for its plainness ) it regains the "aura" which Walter Benjamin claims is lost in the mass produced object.# It is true that it did not matter to Duchamp when Yale lost the readymade "In Advance of a broken arm.' A quick run to the neighborhood hardware store fixed that dilemma. The individual lost shovel did not have an aura which would make its loss tragic, but every snow shovel of that kind gets a Duchampian aura. Every copy of a few types of mass produced items-- a hat rack, a bottle stand, a urinal or a snow shovel-- gains a type of aura, but every mass produced object in the world certainly does not. Cage, we know, has conceived of his 4'33" encompassing all the sounds in the world and giving them the status of art, yet one cannot say that Duchamp had this idea, the scope of his readymades is comparatively quite restricted.

Duchamp does not hold Cage in as high esteem as the younger musician does him. He says in an interview:

Duchamp: I like happenings very much because they are something diametrically opposed to easel painting.

Cabanne: They adhere completely to your theory of the ‘onlooker.' Duchamp: Exactly. Happenings have introduced into art and element no one had put there: boredom. To do a thing in order to bore people is something I have never imagined! And that's too bad, because its a beautiful idea. Fundamentally, it's the same idea as John Cage's silence in music; no one had thought of that.

He has not understood Cage's piece well if he believes its goal is to bore people. I think that Cage would consider boredom an unfortunate side effect. It is interesting to note that in exactly the same way that Cage read Duchamp's works in a distinctly Cagean way, Duchamp views Cage in a Duchampian way. He understands the moment of silence as a move on the audience to bore them. He seems to think of the audience reaction a little more than Cage. He pays attention to it at least enough for it to amuse him. For example, he was delighted with the rejection of his ‘Urinal' from the independent's show:

Cabanne: You really would have been disappointed had the ‘Fountain' been welcomed...

Duchamp: Almost. As it was, I was enchanted. Because fundamentally, I didn't have the traditional attitude of the painter who presents his painting, hoping it will be accepted and then praised by the critics.#

In respect to the critics reaction Duchamp and Cage have fundamentally exactly the same response. Cage says:

Whenever I've found what I'm doing has become pleasing, even to one person, I have redoubled my efforts to find the next step. Though Cage may be a little more adamant about it, they both share the feeling that society or convention is the anti-thesis of the artist, that the search for acceptance and understanding can only lead to the curbing of the artistic impulse.

This abstract idea takes a different timbre when one thinks of the nature of Duchamp's readymades and Cage's indeterminacy. They try to separate both the artist and society from the work of art. The art object is doublely alienated. It is no wonder these works claim to have no meaning. It is rather disheartening to realize that if we in retrospect take a liking to any of these works, we are defeating its original function. In saying that the readymade's were a brilliant move or that the framing of everyday sound is really a beautiful idea, we are bastardizing the work. We are relegating it to the collection of relics of these most avant-garde artists which only have value because of the nostalgic association to a now vanished radicalness. This is no more complicated nor less artistic than any idolization of the past simply because it cannot return--like a baseball card, or some stamp. What could be a more commonly mass produced than those two staple collector's items?
