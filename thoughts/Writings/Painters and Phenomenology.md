# Painters and Phenomenology

The domain of the writer is the same as that of Merleau-Ponty's past experiences. Poetry's investigation of the world, can bring parts of it to our attention. It can not only evoke but make experienceable certain phenomena. The way we experience the real world is affected by the our internal nature which can be affected by the poetic image. Merleau-Ponty seems to support a hypertextual approach even when it does not put the natural world highest on the scale of experience. The painter seems to seek-out the exterior world. Merleau-Ponty talks of the best which can be brought out of a painting in an essay on Cézanne, entitled "Le doute de Cézanne:"

Nous voyons la profondeur, le velouté, la mollesse, la dureté des objets, --Cézanne disait même : leur odeur. Si le peintre veut exprimer le monde, il faut que l'arrangement des couleurs porte en lui ce Tout indivisible: autrement sa peinture sera une allusion aux choses et ne les donnera pas dans l'unité impérieuse, dans la présence, dans la plénitude insurpassable qui est pour nous tout la définition du réel.#

In L'Oeil et l'esprit Merleau-Ponty takes many examples from painters. He uses the way they look at the world and conceive of it to develop many aspects of his philosophy. He quotes Giacometti: «Ce qui m'intéresse dans toutes les peintures, c'est la ressemblance, c'est -à-dire ce qui pour moi est la ressemblance : ce qui me fait découvrir un peu le monde extérieur.» # And very close by quotes Cézanne:

«la nature est à l'intérieur », dit Cézanne. Qualité, lumière, couleur, profondeur, qui sont là-bas devant nous n'y sont que parce qu'elles éveillent un écho dans notre corps, parce qu'il leur fait accueil. Cet équivalence interne, cette formule charnelle de leur présence que les choses suscitent en moi, pourquoi à leur tour ne susciteraient -ils pas un tracé, visible encore, où tout autre regard retrouvera les motifs qui soutiennent son inspection du monde?

He has given painting or the image a power alongside real things. He does not go so far as to say that the image affects our vision of the world, the way Bachelard did of poetry, but he has given the image a particular type of presence in reality. He makes this point more explicit: Alors parait un visible à la deuxième puissance, essence charnel ou icône du premier.... Je serais bien en peine de dire où est le tableau que je regarde. Car je ne le regarde pas comme on regarde une chose, je ne le fixe pas en son lieu, mon regard erre en lui comme dans les nimbes de l'Être, je vois selon ou avec lui plutôt que je ne le vois.#

He further gives strength to the painter's act by saying: Il...faut bien avouer, ...que la vision est miroir ou concentration de l'univers, ...que la même chose est là-bas au coeur du monde et ici au coeur de la vision, la même ou , si l'on y tient, une chose semblable, mais selon une similitude efficace, qui est parente, genèse, métamorphose de l'être en sa vision. C'est la montagne elle-même qui, de là-bas se fait voir du peintre, c'est elle qu'il interroge du regard.#

We essentially return to a statement that man is in the world. Merleau-Ponty's discussion of painting is grounded in the body. He is attracted to painting because of its proximity to pure perception. The painter does not wish to analyze the world but to describe it. He starts the same chapter in L'Oeil et l'esprit which I have been analyzing and which contains most of his comments on painting, with : Le peintre «apporte son corps», dit Valéry. Et, en effet, on ne voit pas comment un Esprit pourrait peindre. C'est en prêtant son corps au monde que le peintre change le monde en peinture. Pour comprendre ces transsubstantiations, il faut retrouver le corps opérant et actuel, celui qui n'est pas un morceau d'espace, en faisceau de fonctions, qui est un entrelacs de visions et de mouvement.#

Visible phenomena, become a part of the body. They can be conceived of as part of the self. In drawing classes early on, I was taught or shown how to hold a pencil at arms length and close one eye to sort out the sizes and angles of the scene I was trying to draw. I realized recently that I have so internalized this system of measurement that I automatically scale my drawings to the size that the objects before me appear at arms length. A way of conceiving of my visual perceptions seemed to make perfect sense: I see my range of vision as comprising of the front two thirds or so of the sphere which I can touch by moving my extended arms in all directions around me. My feet are one thumb wide. The building out my window is much smaller. Any understanding of the space around me at any one instant comes down to the small distances of the body: the size of my hands, my height-- the distances I can know. Or, one could say that I have extended the most natural system of measurement, the distances of my own body, out on to the world# This system can appropriate the entire visual world, but only works for the instant. In a chapter entitled "Space" in Phénoménologie de la perception, Merleau-Ponty quotes Berkeley:

> L'argument de Berkeley, si on l'explicite tout à fait, est à peu près celui-ci. Ce que j'appelle profondeur est en realité une juxtaposition de point comparables à la largeur. simplement, je suis mal placé pour la voir. Je la verrais si j'étais à la place d'un spectateur latéral, qui peut embrasser du regard la série des objets disposés devant moi, tandis que pour moi ils se cachent l'un l'autre-- ou voir la distance de mon corps au premier objet, tandis que pour moi cette distance est ramasser en un point. Ce qui rend la profondeur invisible pour moi, c'est précisément ce qui la rend pour le spectateur visible sous l'aspect de la largeur: la juxtaposition des points simultanés sur une seule direction qui est celle de mon regard.#

This image is very different from the painter's vision, described earlier. There is inherently something unknowable about depth a major quality of space which calls immediately for different ways of conceiving of it. The interior does not perfectly match the exterior. Space is not vision.

I came to the realization about vision, which I mentioned earlier, this fall on the roof of a particular garage where I spent many hours painting. I made paintings in every direction from a single point where I had chosen to stand. I explored virtually every part of the visual situation around me. I was used to that particular familiarity one feels to a scene after having painted it, but had never before had a three-dimensional experience of this feeling. Every direction I looked, I had painted. I felt that I had somehow appropriated that spot. It had become a part of me. One day, I went to brunch at a friend's apartment on one of the highest floors of a near-by building from which I could see my garage. My spot was tiny on the other building. Suddenly I became very aware of the new perceptual sphere around me at that moment in my friends apartment. I was looking at the place where I had memorized my perceptual situation and was very far outside of it. There were two distinct points for me: the one I knew because I had spent so much time painting it and the one immediately around me. The one I can never leave. Between them was a path which I could never fully reconstruct, because it held no perceptual realities like the two spots I was contemplating. An understanding of space eluded me from both those spots. The sphere of perceptions which surrounds me at every instant but only for that instant can only know space to a certain extent--that which is real to me at any one instant. Space is much larger than anything I can take in at any one instant, than anything I can ever come to know. The closest I can get to this larger space is a conceptual approximation of it. We are incapable of ever knowing the horizon, or getting beyond it because once we arrive where we think it had been, there would be a new horizon. It is never knowable.

Merleau-Ponty finishes his chapter on painting L'Oeil et l'esprit with a strong appraisal of painting's strength which extends only to the instantaneous perception of space. The exterior world:

> L' «instant de monde » que Cézanne voulait peindre et qui est depuis longtemps passé, ses toiles continuent de nous le jeter, et sa montagne Sainte-Victoire se fait et se refait d'un bout à l'autre du monde, autrement, mais non moins énergiquement que dans la roche dur au-dessus d'Aix. Essence et existence, imaginaire et réel, visible et invisible, la peinture brouille toutes nos catégories en déployant son univers onirique d'essences charnelles, de ressemblances efficaces de significations muettes.#

Cézanne's appropriation of the mountain is only partial. From Aix, Cézanne can see the silhouette of this mountain, from other towns in the valley or from some other valley which gives view on another side of the mountain, that silhouette looks completely different. These are the maximum view's or conceptions which Cézanne's canvases can recreate anywhere in the world.

If I climb a mountain, my view of it is ever changing, sometimes just a path and the next highest point looming before me, then a glimpse of the summit, or the highest part I can see from where ever I find myself. When I arrive at the top of the mountain, my view of it radically changes. If I flew around the mountain it would again look quite different. From the plane I couldn't see the human sized events along the path which were such an essential part of my concept of the mountain when I climbed it.
