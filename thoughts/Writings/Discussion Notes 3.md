# Discussion Notes #3

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof. Stephen Anderson  
> Language and Mind  
> Jan. 31, 1995  
> Discussion notes #3  

I found Lasnik's elucidation of why the "wanna" sounds wrong in the sentence: "The student, you wanna solve this problem" to be a strong proof of that a language user is subconsciously aware of the ‘trace' of a word in these reorganized sentences, but I wonder how many more examples like this one there can be. There are not many contractions in English. In most cases the trace will not fall between two words which can form a contraction. Jackendoff's examples of the long distance dependencies make us all too aware of this trace and they too seem wrong: --What did General Washington eat kippers and ∆ for breakfast?

He responds by saying,"these examples seem to reveal ‘imperfections' in mental grammar, situations where it doesn't allow us to express our thoughts as freely as a completely general system might.(p.78)" I don't know when it was ever said that mental grammar might be a completely general system. I thought that a major aspect of mental grammar was that it was not general, that it was specific to one language, or language family. I find the fact that one can make sentences which seem like they should acceptable to a mental grammar, but are absolutely not to be evidence of the strength and pervasiveness of a specific mental grammar for each language, with governs every speaker of that language.

I find it interesting if not a little bit sad that a major question in Linguistic studies seems to be a problem of definition. Jackendoff gives us two phrases as examples: 1--the old man in the chair with the broken leg 2--Many people here have read two books.

Each sentence is ambiguous. Jackendoff states, " The question is if the sentence(2) like the phrase(1), has different syntactic structures for these different interpretations, or whether it has just a single syntactic structure that can be interpreted either way." (p.73) I don't see how the sentence(2) could have two syntactic structures. There is no way of diagraming the sentence(2) in a way which removes the ambiguity. One can diagram the phrase(1) in one of two ways to remove the ambiguity, either the prepositional phrase "with the broken leg" is subordinate to the man or to the chair. The ambiguity in the sentence requires information beyond that which can be extracted from the syntax in order to be resolved: "these two books," or "at least two books" clears the ambiguity right up. I'm not sure I understand the nature of the problem Jackendoff is trying to describe (of course he "can't begin to get into the complexities here"). The reason for there being ambiguity in these two syntactic groups is so different that I really don't understand how they could fall into the same problem of analysis. Can't ambiguities arise elsewhere than just from in the syntax. I have to say that I am not terribly impressed if this is a problem Linguists have been working over for thirty years. I rather hope that Jackendoff has simply done a very poor job of describing the problem, and that it is considerably more interesting.
