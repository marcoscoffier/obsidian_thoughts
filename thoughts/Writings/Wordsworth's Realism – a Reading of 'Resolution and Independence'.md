# Wordsworth's Realism: a Reading of 'Resolution and Independence'

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof. Roger Blood  
> Lit 410: The Interpretation of the Lyric  
> Dec. 13, 1994  
> Wordsworth's Realism: a Reading of ‘Resolution and Independence'  

Wordsworth's poem Resolution and Independence poses problems he seems to have struggled with throughout his poetry. It is impossible to discuss these problems adequately within the confines of that single poem so I will have to bring in examples from others and from some of Wordsworth's own critical writings in a combination which I hope my reader will not find too confusing in such a short paper. Wordsworth is often most interested in representing a real experience in poetry. Even in a poem like ‘Michael' where he relates a story --possibly fictional--he first brings the reader to a very specific place which he knows well in the real world and builds Michael's story from there. I want to discuss the problems Wordsworth encountered trying to imitate or describe his real experience in poetry. Many of his problems in representation arise from the nature of language itself. Resolution and Independence presents an example of most of the important devices Wordsworth uses to translate experience using words from the real world into poetry.

In a letter to Sara Hutchinson, Wordsworth describes the moment which inspired the writing of Resolution and Independence and which he wished to convey somehow to his reader:

> I consider the manner in which I was rescued from my dejection and despair almost as an interposition of Providence.... It is in the character of the old man to tell his story in a manner which the impatient reader must necessarily feel as tedious. But good God! such a figure, in such a place, a pious self-respecting, miserably infirm old man telling such a tale!#

To the best of our knowledge Wordsworth did have this encounter with a leech gatherer. In the poem, he is trying to recreate the effect of this event. He fully realizes, however, that, as he says in the Preface to Lyrical Ballads:

> it is impossible for the Poet to produce upon all occasions language as exquisitely fitted for the passion as that which the real passion itself suggests, it is proper that he should consider himself as in the situation of a translator, who deems himself justified when he substitutes excellences of another kind for those which are attainable by him; and endeavors occasionally to surpass his original, in order to make some amends for the general inferiority to which he feels that he must submit. #

That the Poet must translate experience into poetry makes evident a separation between the world of the poem and the real world. Each operates under its own terms and mechanisms.

Wordsworth's discussion with the old man, which he so adamantly described to Sara Hutchinson, as it is presented in the poem is in fact quite strange. He approaches him with an opaque equivalent of "nice day isn't it" at the end of verse XII. The man responds with a "gentle answer" which isn't even described. Then Wordsworth asks him "what occupation do you here pursue?" and, rather than quote the man's tale, proceeds to describe the man's "flash of mild surprise," and "vivid sable eyes". The "feeble words" like his "feeble chest," which are " above the reach/ Of ordinary men" are never recited, only paraphrased:

> He told that to these waters he had come
> To gather leeches, being old and poor:
> Employment hazardous and wearisome!
> He had many hardships to endure:
> From pond to pond he roamed from moor to moor;
> Housing, with God's good help, by choice or chance;
> And in this way he gained an honest maintenance.

The man's tale-- "such a tale"--which could easily have been quoted remains absent from this poem. Even with the poetic language Wordsworth chooses to use, one is certainly left at a loss why he felt he gained so much from this discussion. I don't think anyone could understand the epiphany he seems to have had with the Leech-gatherer on the lonely plain. We can at best accept it as fact and try to sympathize by remembering moments of our own when the simplest unexplainable circumstances resulted in some kind of revelation. But regardless of its emotional effect, or lack thereof, the fact that Wordsworth felt he could not quote the Leech gatherer's tale in his poem leaves some interesting questions for poetry. To emphasize this still further I wish to quote him again from the Preface to Lyrical Ballads:

> there cannot be any doubt but that the [Poet's] language... must in liveliness and truth, fall far short of that which is uttered by men in real life, under the actual pressure of those passions, certain shadows of which the Poet thus produces, or feels to be produced in himself. However exalted a notion we would wish to cherish of the character of a Poet, it is obvious, that, while he describes and imitates passions, his situation is altogether slavish and mechanical, compared with the freedom and power of real substantial action and suffering.#

Wordsworth makes a further step to split language into two categories. He says that the words in real life are powerful because of their contextualization, the passions to which they are attached are real and present. Unlike objects or scenery, words can be easily brought into a poem or a piece of text, one needs only two little quotation marks, yet Wordsworth does not want to bring a relic from his conversation with the leech gatherer into his poem. Though he tries his best to describe it, Wordsworth cannot bring the context of this meeting to a point where it is strong enough to support the words of this leech gatherer, or which could create an environment where the reader would understand the power he had felt from them. So rather than place them in the poem where they could only be ridiculed, he leaves them out. Their absence becomes much more powerful. It allows the reader's imagination to do the work to fill in the gaps. Realizing that he cannot adequately describe the scene he chose to use absence as one of his devices. The very conversation which motivated is conspicuously left out of the poem. The absence comes to represent language's inability to recreate the real world, but the hole in the language allows the reader to slip from the words into the reality of his own thoughts and thus nearer to real life objects and situations.

Wordsworth's choice in "Resolution and Independence" to use poet's talk rather than the man's real life words is an interesting move because it brings to light a fundamental division in Wordsworth's conception: that poetry and the real world are two separate places. Wordsworth made a similar division in a general comment prefacing the Intimations Ode or rather the Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood:

Many times while going to school have I grasped at a wall or a tree to recall myself from this abyss of idealism to the reality. At that time I was afraid of such processes. In later periods of life I have deplored, as we all have reason to do, a subjugation of an opposite character, and have rejoices over the remembrances.... To that dreamlike vividness and splendor which invest objects of sight in childhood, everyone, I believe, if he would look back could bear testimony....

The way I have presented this passage could be misleading, but I did it on purpose to underline a common misaprehension: poetry is often equated to the inner idealism from which Wordsworth has to force himself by grasping onto the tree. The two worlds of this passage, however, are not poetry and the real world, but rather two forms of real life experience. The poetic world is yet a third place, bridging the external reality of objects and the internal idealism of emotions but in its own space with its own rules which Wordsworth uses to try to imitate or describe both his worlds of experience. I wish to begin this study with moments when Wordsworth tries to bring the external reality of objects into the poems--specifically two examples where objects in the real world which Wordsworth has seen, become symbols of a story he recounts in the poem. Like ‘Resolution and Independence', Wordsworth claims to have had a particular mission in writing ‘The Thorn':

> Arose out of my observing, on the ridge of Quantock Hill, on a stormy day, a thorn which I had often past, in calm and bright weather, without noticing. I said to myself, ‘Cannot I by some invention do as much to make this Thorn permanently an impressive object as the storm made it to my eyes at this moment?#

He deliberately chooses to transform the Thorn into a symbol. It comes
to symbolize with the small pond and the mound covered in crimson moss,
the story surrounding a mythical murdered infant. The story is couched
between the two repeated descriptions of the Thorn at the beginning and
the end of the poem. By the time the reader arrives at the second, the
Thorn has become a signpost to locate the area where the woman sat and
wept; its height is reminiscent of a child's size and the crimson moss
comes to represent the child's blood or the mother's cloak. The process
of bringing the Thorn to the reader's attention and then transforming
it into a personal symbol is very similar to that Wordsworth uses in
the longer lyric "Michael". The opening again leads us to the framing
device which is here a heap of stones:
If from the public way you turn your steps
Up the tumultuous brook of Green-head Ghyll,
You will suppose that with an upright path
Your feet must struggle; in such a bold ascent
The pastoral mountains front you, face to face.
But, courage! for around that boisterous brook
The mountains have all opened out themselves,
And made a hidden valley of their own.
No habitation can be seen; but they
Who journey thither find themselves alone
With a few sheep, with rocks and stones, and kites
That overhead are sailing in the sky.
It is in truth an utter solitude;
Nor should I have made mention of this Dell
But for one object which you might pass by,
Might see and notice not. Beside the brook
Appears a heap of unhewn stones!

By the end of the poem the reader is told that these stones are the remains of a broken covenant between Michael and his son Luke, that they were to build a sheep-fold which was never finished and that Luke laid down the first block at his father's request, promising to return from the city with money to save the land for his family. The reader realizes by the end of the poem that the heap of stones moves beyond being a symbol like the Thorn, and takes on the signifying property of an index.# The actual stones would not be near Green-head Ghyll had Michael not wished to make a pact with his son. They would not be in a "straggling heap" if the pact had not been left unfinished. Whether the wanderer near Green-head Ghyll knows it or not, that pile of rocks is the remains of the unfinished pact between Michael and his son (A bullet hole in a door was caused by a bullet whether an interpretant is there to connect a bullet with the hole, or smoke on the horizon has an indexical relationship with a fire. Whether you can see the fire or not ). The process the reader undertakes from the first presentation of the stones to the realization of their indexical relationship to Michael and his son is quite powerful. Together with the description and careful location of the stones in an existing place, a place Wordsworth had seen, the indexical relationship makes the story very real--like the unfound bullet to the hole, or the unseen fire to its smoke, the story of Michael and his son acquires an undeniable reality.

In each of these two poems Wordsworth started with objects in the real world, simple common objects, which he wished to bring to the ‘view' of the reader's poetic eye. He is making the reader notice what he would otherwise have missed. Wordsworth has chosen to bring out the beauty of the simple and common. He has made a point about the commonness he wishes to use as subject matter in the Preface to Lyrical Ballads:

> The principle object, then, which I proposed to myself in these poems was to choose incidents and situation from common life, and to relate or describe them, throughout, as far as was possible, in a selection of language really used by men; and at the same time, to throw over them a certain colouring of the imagination, whereby ordinary things should be presented to the mind in an unusual way....#

Those simplest subjects are often the ones which are right below ones nose but simply aren't noticed. Victor Shklovsky describes this bringing of attention to what would otherwise go unnoticed as the process of art in his essay "Art as Technique":

Habituation devours works clothes ,furniture one's wife, and the fear of war.... And art exists that one may recover the sensation of life; it exists to make one feel things, to make the stone stony. The purpose of art is to impart things as they are perceived and not as they are known. The technique of art is to make objects "unfamiliar" to make forms difficult, to increase the difficulty and length of perception because the process of perception is an aesthetic end in itself and must be prolonged. Art is a way of experiencing the artfulness of an object the object is not important.

This can certainly be read as a description of the process we have just described in ‘Michael' and ‘The Thorn', but Shklovsky means it to refer to words themselves. He spends his entire essay giving examples of how Tolstoy makes metaphors which make us realize something new about the object which is described. Wordsworth too uses new or even strange juxtapositions of language to describe events or objects in his poetry. The description of the leech gatherer is much stranger and more powerful than the paraphrased tale. He creates a metaphor which takes up an entire stanza:

> As a huge stone is sometimes seen to lie
> Couched on the bald top of an eminence;

Wonder to all who do the same espy,
By what means it could thither come, and whence;

So that it seems a thing endured with sense:

> Like a sea beast crawled forth, that on a shelf
> Of rock or sand reposeth, there to sun itself;

Such seemed this Man...

A rock, since well before Christ founded the church on the shoulders of his apostle Peter, has been a metaphor for stillness or immobility. Yet this rock is on top of an "eminence" and gives quite a different message. Despite its rock-like stillness, it is forever prepared to fall. The stone's dual message makes it alive in the poet's eyes, The life comes from the dissonance of these contradicting images that fight each other out of becoming a labeled convention (like either a still rock or an object which is perpetually moving like a pendulum). We have represented for us the perpetually still moment before movement. Both stillness and mobility are always present. Wordsworth compares this rock to the leech gatherer, a strange metaphor, but it gives this man an incredible power which he would otherwise not have: Such seemed this Man, not all alive nor dead,
Nor all asleep--in his extreme old age:

> His body was bent double, feet and head
> Coming together in life's pilgrimage;

As if some dire constraint of pain, or rage
Of sickness felt by him in times long past,
A more than human weight upon his frame had cast.

Wordsworth is at his strongest when he doesn't allow his descriptions of nature to fall into a conventional association-- when he mixes opposing feelings in a single image. Anytime Wordsworth picks either joy or dejection for an image, that situation is classified, or labeled--it becomes one or the other. In Tintern Abbey, Wordsworth uses many oxymorons in the descriptions of his childish experience with nature: the coarser pleasures of his boyish days "--That time is past,/ And all its aching joys are now no more,/ And all its dizzy raptures...." (Italics are mine.) The contradictions continue in the description of his regained experience:

For I have learned

To look on nature, not as in the hour
Of thoughtless youth: but hearing oftentimes
The still, sad music of humanity,
Not harsh or grating, though of ample power
To chasten and subdue. And I have felt
A presence that disturbs me with the joy
Of elevated thoughts; a sense sublime
Of something far more deeply interfused,
Whose dwelling is the light of setting suns,
And the round ocean and the living air,
And the blue sky, and the mind of the man:
A motion and a spirit that impels
All thinking things, all objects of all thought,
And rolls through all things.

Tintern Abbey takes two steps away from the the signification process
of ‘Michael' and ‘The Thorn'. The abbey isn't present (like the heap
of stones) in the poem at all, it is merely a place to which Wordsworth
returns which unleashes:
--feelings too
Of unremembered pleasure: such, perhaps,
As have no slight or trivial influence
On that best portion of a good man's life,
His little nameless, unremembered acts
Of kindness and of love.

The subject of this poem is his experience his own inner reality rather than being the stories he relates in the other two poems. Much of Wordsworth's best lyrics aspire to describe an inner sensation. He usually manages to connect it to some object in external reality but as he said about the Intimations Ode, he has trouble staying in the reality of the physical world. I will let Hegel take the first step in validating Wordsworth's position:

> ... since the inner subjective life is the proper source of lyric, the poet must retain the right to limit himself to expressing purely inner moods, reflections, etc., without spreading himself into portraying a concrete situation in its external features as well.#

‘Resolution and Independence' is worth mentioning when we are moving to look at poems where he is concerned with describing the inner experience, more and more detached from any external influence. In it he switches from one world to the other. After the man has told him his occupation, he says:

The old Man still stood talking by my side;

But now his voice to me was like a stream
Scarce heard; nor word from word could I divide;

And the whole body of the man did seem
Like one whom I had met with in a dream;

Or like a man from some far region sent,
To give me strength, by apt admonishment.

When he sinks back into this inner world all the fears and perturbations
from which the Man had saved him return:
My former thoughts returned, the fear that kills;
And hope that is unwilling to be fed;
Cold pain and labour and all the fleshly ills;
And mighty poets in their misery dead.
--Perplexed, and longing to be comforted,
My question eagerly did I renew,
"How is it that you live, and what is it you do?"

The Man repeats what he has already said in the poem but to lesser effect. The Poet's mind seems incapable of ceasing its wanderings. This is not the first time that he has repeated a passage in this poem. In the first two stanzas of Resolution and Independence he describes a scene: "There was a roaring.../" "But now... /" "All the things that love the sun are out of doors.../ The hare is running races in her mirth.../" In the third stanza he places himself in this world and repeats the images: I was a Traveler then upon the moor;

I saw the hare that raced about with joy;

I heard the woods and distant rivers roar;...

He repeats the description of nature at the beginning of Resolution and Independence with out changing it, and then repeats the leech-gatherer's tale. This mechanism is used rather often in his poetry. Repetition is a similar device to the absence I discussed at the beginning of this paper. It makes one aware of the distance in meaning between each repetition. One can recall how our concept of the Thorn or of Michael's heap of stones changes as we learn its meaning as a symbol. One is made aware that the single reading of an individual statement cannot contain all the meanings it is capable of evoking. Wordsworth comments on the device of repetition in Note to ‘The Thorn':

> There is a numerous class of readers who imagine that the same words cannot be repeated without tautology: this is a great error: virtual tautology is much oftener produced by using different words when the meaning is the exactly the same. #

He continues:

There are also various other reasons why repetition and apparent tautology are frequently beauties of the highest kind. Among the chief of these reasons is the interest which the mind attaches to words, not only as symbols of the passion, but as things active and efficient, which are themselves part of the passion. And further, from a spirit of fondness, exaltation, and gratitude, the mind luxuriates in the repetition of words which appear successfully to communicate its feelings.

He is in effect describing a process similar to what he did with the heap of stones and the Thorn: he turns words into personal symbols.# It is interesting to note that that this use of language is directly opposite to the one Shklovsky describes. Rather than try to defamiliarize us with words which have become common, he repeats them, turns them into his own convention which brings out a particular other strength of language. He writes in "The Prelude: Book V":

> Twice five years
> Or less I might have seen, when first my mind
> With conscious pleasure opened up to the charm
> Of words in tuneful order, found them sweet
> For their own sakes, a passion, and a power;

And phrases pleased me chosen for delight,
For pomp, or love.

He manages to rein in conventional words and transform them into his own personal symbols to which the reader of his poetry can become accustomed. This is the ultimate power of the poet. He makes language his own. The repeated word or passage makes one realize how many meanings the individual word or passage can have depending on the changing contestualising state of the reader's own experience. The repetition emphasizes a lacuna of meaning between each successive use. I believe that the only understanding of Wordsworth's epiphany one can come to in Resolution and Independence is to realize that the importance of the external situation he describes has everything to do with his internal experience at that time. Like the different meaning that a word can have dependent upon its context, different situations in the external world can have different meanings dependent upon the situation of the ever changing internal experience. The fact that language can point to a meaning or a concept precisely when it loses control, when it stops to have a specific sign signifier relationship, as exemplified by the device of repetition, is quite a strength. Language still manages to operate at the dull murky level of shifting signification and undescribable modulations of meaning. Wordsworth says, also in Book V of the Prelude:

Visionary power

Attends the motions of the viewless winds,
Embodied in the mystery of words:
There the darkness makes abode, and all the host
Of shadowy things work endless changes, --there,
As in a mansion like their proper home,
Even forms and substances are circumfused
By that transparent veil with light divine,
And, through the turnings intricate of verse,
Present themselves as objects recognized,
In flashes, and with glory not their own.
