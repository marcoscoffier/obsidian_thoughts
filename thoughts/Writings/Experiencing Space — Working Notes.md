# Experiencing Space — Working Notes

A powerful tension is built in the courtroom scene of L'Etranger. The trial makes perfect sense. Anyone would be shocked if the verdict had come out any other way, given how the information sounds to the jury. The reader realises that any wish for Mersault to be set free, is a complete usurpation of the judicial system--of society as we know it. This is a very powerful effect for an author to pull off. The tension arisses for the reader, because Camus is a much better at writing than Mersault is at presenting his case. When Mersault tries to say that it was the sun which, had made him shoot, it sounds absolutely rediculous to the courtroom, but the reader, remembers the weight of its light, shattering on the water, the sweat in the eyes, the glint of the Arab's knife. Our omniscient narrator, has a firmer grasp on his protagonist's life, than the character himself.

It is interesting to note how many disciplines and situations use the vocabulary of space to define thier realm: social space, literary space, Euclidean space, personal space, public space the list is inexhaustable. All these conceptions use space or the language of space as a metaphor for that which they are discussing. The space which I want to discuss comes before all these, it is not a metaphor for any thing. It is the real actual experience, but I am looking at literature. Literature cannot deliver us space, or an experience of space any more than science or mathematics can. But it can deliver a description of this experience.

In describing phenomenology, Merleau-Ponty says:

> Il s'agit de décrire, et non pas d'expliquer ni d'analyser.# ... Tout ce que je sais du monde, même par la science, je sais à partir d'une vue mienne ou d'une expérience du monde sans laquelle les symboles de la science ne voudraient rien dire. Tout l'univers de la science est construit sur le monde vécu et si nous voulons penser la science elle-même avec rigueur , en apprécier exactement le sens et la portée, il nous faut réveiller d'abord cette expérience du monde dont elle est l'expression seconde. La science n'a pas est n'aura jamais le même sens d'être que le monde perçu pour la simple raison qu'elle en est une détermination ou une explication.#

Painting also produces a second reallity, but it is closest to Merleau-Ponty conception of the phenomenological world. Bachelard discusses the extent of literature's power to represent, or describe an experience in a chapter entitled, "La chute imaginaire" in L'air et les songes:

Bien que les images de la chute soient nombreuses, elles sont loin d'être aussi riches en impressions dynamiques qu'on ne penserait à premier examen. La chute «pure» est rare. Le plus souvent, les images de la chute ont une richesse d'adjonction: le poète leur adjoint des circonstances entièrement externes.... Par example, il ne sert de rien, pour émouvoir notre imagination dynamique, de nous dire, comme Milton dans son Paradis perdu , que Lucifer, précipité de ciel tomba pendant neuf jours. cette chute des neuf jours ne nous fais pas sentir le vent de la chute, et l'immensité de parcours ne fait pas grandir notre effroi. On nous aurait dit que le démon tomba pendant un siècle que nous n'aurions pas vu le gouffre plus profond.

He is looking for a phenomenological description of falling: Combien serron plus actives les impressons où le poète sait nous communiquer la differentielle de la chute vivante, c'est à dire le changement même de la substance qui tombe et qui en tombant, dans l'instant même de sa chute deviens plu pesante, plus lourde, plus fautive...Toute image doit s'enrichir de métaphors pour mettre l'imagination en vie.#

The poet cannot deliver this experience except through metaphor. The painter too, operates on metaphor.

Now at my desk in my own apartment I find it quite difficult to conjure the reality of either of those scenes. I can see the outside world through the window which back-lights my monitor. I have the shade pulled down just low enough to keep the sun out of my eyes, though it still manages to explode out from underneath. My window is open rather wide so that the cold air from outside can mix with the heat from the radiator which always warms my legs. The breeze constantly, and I think pleasantly, changes the temperature. It would be impossible to continue to describe my entire perceptual and mental situation, but I only wanted to highlight how this instant is the only perception I can know. I can picture where the garage I was discussing is and where my friends apartment is, but the map I define in my head like the connection I tried to make at brunch this fall is purely conceptual. This is the difference I would like to make between perceptual and conceptual space. The first is epitomized by the space one can perceive at any one instant, for the second one must construct a string of perceptual spaces or resort to a map, a representation of space.

Opposite to this is the world of only perceptions and actions and no internal imagination. Camus describes this man in the myth of Sysiphus. The line Sysiphus is happy has choked even the heartiest Camus fan. Perhaps one can understand it or redefine it to mean flat-line, but I think that any one except the devout Bhuddist will agree with me that it is impossible to imagine Sysiphus as genuinely happy at least the happy we are accustomed to experiencing. He says: in the essay "Le desert" in Noces: Quelle raison d'être ému pour qui n'attend pas le lendemain? Cette impassibilité et cette grandeur de l'homme sans espoir, cet éternel présent, c'est cela précisément que des théologiens avisés ont appelé l'enfer."# Is having no hope the same as having no imagination? Fabien the protagonist of Vol de nuit is incapable of communicating with the town's people to whom he delivers mail, Saint Exupéry writes in the opening passages of Vol de nuit:

> En descendant moteur au raltenti sur San Julian, Fabien se sentit las. Tout ce qui fait douce la vie des hommes grandissait vers lui: leurs maisons, leurs petits cafés, les arbres de leur promenade.... Fabien êut désiré vivre ici longtemps, prendre sa part ici d'éternité, car les petites villes, où il vivait une heure, et les jardins clos de vieux murs qu'il traversait, lui semblaient éternels de durer en dehors de lui.... Et le village coulait déja au ras des ailes, étalant le mystère de ses jardins fermés que leurs murs ne protégeaient plus. Mais Fabien, ayant atteri, sut qu'il n'avait rien vu, sinon le mouvement lent de quelques hommes parmi leurs pierres. Ce village défendait, par sa seule immmobilité, le secret de ses passions, ce village refusait sa douceur: il eût fallu renoncer à l'action pour la conquérir.#

The change in his perception of the town at the "mais" from when he is in the air to when he touches the ground is remarkable. The world from an airplane is seen from a privileged position. Fabien's wing passes over the villager's splayed secrets, but at the same time that the plane's position is privileged, it is removed. He has to pay in distance what he has gained in perspective. When he lands, he finds that the walls are just as high as they ever where. This world of the village is not and cannot be his. His propensity for action keeps keeps him from being able to know the secrets hidden by the villager's sheer immobility .

In order to learn their secrets, he must give up on action. Camus tells us that the majority say no to the existential question, and that the yes decision is the same as the artist's to make a great work. Janine cannot know the nomads because she is not a part of their action. Fabien's yes to action isolates him just like Janine.

Saint-Exupéry makes observation about communication in Terre des hommes:

> Un songe de jeune fille l'isole de moi, comment l'y joindre? Que connaître d'une jeune fille qui rentre chez elle à pas lents, les yeux baissés et se souriant à elle même, et déja pleine d'inventions et de mensonges adorables? Elle a pu, des pensées, de la voix et des silences d'un amant, se former un Royaume, et dès lors il n'est plus pour elle, en dehors de lui, que des barbares. Mieux que dans une autre planète, je la sens enfermée dans son decret, dans ses coutumes, dans les échos chantants de sa mémoire. Née heir de volcans, de pelouses ou de la saumure des mers, la voici déjà à demi divine.#

This girl is not an actual person he sees before him, "Un songe de jeune fille," is not " le songe d'une jeune fille." Later in the paragraph, she takes on mythic possibilities--she is born of a volcano etc.. Yet he describes details about this young girl who does not exist, which allow us feel for her or immediately identify with her. It is important to notice that this is an identification. Far more often than I notice some one shuffling along with his or her eyes lowered, lost in thoughts which make them smile to themselves, I am there myself. I think that people are too self-absorbed to notice those lonely details about someone else, and are all that much more aware of when one is feeling them oneself. Proof of this lies in the simple fact that no one ever notices those details when you are at the heart of your loneliness and you would most want someone to. Also, no one can save you from the deepest loneliness--it is most pleasant just to thrive in this solipsism.

When Descartes came up with his coordinate system, it must have been marvelous to think that with only the simple placement of three perpendicular axes, any object in space could be placed in an exact relationship with any other object. Suddenly all of space was accounted for, Descartes' grid could extend as far as the mind could concieve of it, but this system did not take into account the real presence and placement of real objects. It merely laid a plan for the conceptual exercise of mathematical theories. When navigators needed to place themselves in real space, when physical manifestations like the curvature of the earth could not be simply pierced by a conceptual grid but had to be accounted for, they had to turn to other systems of measuring space and other conceptions of space.

He also describes his first flight in Terre des hommes. He is afraid of the possibility of engine failure which was quite common in those days:

Quand je sortis de ce bureau, j'éprouvai un orgueil puéril. J'allais être à mon tour, des l'aube, responsable d'une charge de passagers, responsable du courrier d'Afrique. Mais j'éprouvais aussi une grande humilité. Je me sentais mal préparé. L'Espagne était pauvres en refuges; je craignais, en face de la panne menaçante, de ne pas savoir où chercher accueil d'un champ de secours. Je m'étais penché sans y découvrir les enseignements dont j'avais besoin, sur l'aridité des cartes ; aussi, le coeur plein de ce mélange de timidité et d'orgueil, je m'en fus passer cette veillée d'armes chez mon camarade Guillaumet.

...

Mais quelle étrange leçon de géographie je reçus là! Guilaumet ne m'enseignait pas l'Espagne ; il me faisait de l'Espagne une amie. Il ne me parlait ni d'hydrographie, ni de populations, ni de cheptel. Il me parlait pas de Guadix, mais des trois orangers qui, près de Guadix, bordent un champ : «Méfie-toi d'eux, marque les sur ta carte…» Et les trois orangers y tenatient plus de place que la Sierra Nevada. Il ne me parlait pas de Lorca, mais d'une simple ferme près de Lorca. D'une ferme vivante. Et de son fermier. Et de sa fermière. Et ce couple prenait, perdu dans l'espace, a quinze cent kilomètres de nous, une importance démesurée.

Guillaumet continues to describe havens for him and to warn him of hidden dangers such as a stream in the middle of a field which would otherwise be perfect for a emergency landing:

> Et, peu à peu, l'Espagne de ma carte devenait, sous la lampe, un pays de contes de fées. Je balisais d'une croix les refuges et les pièges. Je balisais ce fermier, ces trente moutons, ce ruisseau. Je portais, à sa place exacte, cette bergère qu'avaient négligée les géographes.#

There is a definite difference established between the geography of the map and the one his friend describes for him which is far more elaborate. The map world is a world of symbols, whereas the new one is made up of experiences, or at least imagined experiences. The map of Spain with his flight plan penciled across it, is being highlighted with known moments of safety. He no longer sees Spain as a large colored blob on a piece of paper with position markings grided in. It is becoming a network of these safety havens: the farmer with the three orange trees--the field which has the hidden and dangerous stream running down the middle.

I was in an airplane once crossing the Atlantic which had small television monitors posted all around the cabin and showed the passengers a map with a little airplane on it which would move one pixel at a time as we made progress across the ocean: I felt as though I was watching the minute hand of a clock. Even with this map though, making the leap from my immediate unchanging surroundings of the cabin to the distant landscape below me was quite impossible.
