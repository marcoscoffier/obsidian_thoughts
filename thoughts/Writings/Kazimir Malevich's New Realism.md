# Kazimir Malevich's New Realism

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Katerina Clark  
> Lit. 410: The Avant Garde  
> Yale University  
> Nov. 4, 1994  
> Kazimir Malevich's New Realism  

I know it is not a good idea to start an essay for a class by saying that the person whom I am going to discuss makes me angry, but an avant garde artist poses a special problem. Anger is a key element in his agenda. I have come to realize that the angry viewer gains the most from the avant-garde when he picks apart his anger to see what caused this uncontrollable and thus genuine emotion. He can then reconstruct a more specific idea of what it was he loved about art in the first place. The challenge forces him to reevaluate what he had come to take for granted. The anger is a sign post for the viewer--something precious to him has been insulted and violated. The "that's not art!" reaction of the conservative causes the question, "what then is art?" The avant-garde artist becomes a tool for the viewer's greater understanding of himself.

The viewer's anger, however, marks him as being separate from these avant-garde artists. This process of "épatage"-- forcing understanding by shocking--especially when the avant-garde artist is not even concerned with being understood becomes very alienating for the audience. The avant garde becomes the smallest elite, almost no one can be a part of this cutting edge crowd. This movement was constantly dividing ranks, splitting and fighting among themselves. The Surrealists broke off from the Dada artists; the Supremacists broke from the Futurists or the Cubists: Such is the repertoire of the history of this period--it resembles the power upheavals and turmoil following the demise of any powerful monarch. But, this new elitism of the avant-garde is the largest factor in their own downfall. It is the way in which they most imitate the academy they are so vehement about over throwing. This eliticism is particularly pronounced in the Supremacists writings of Kazimir Malevich. Like an adolescent, the avant garde artist makes blanket statements to undermine and destroy the academy--completely overlooking the academies strengths. What the avant garde artist fails to realize is that the fundamental forces that uphold the institutions he is targeting are identical to those which he is trying to invoke to destroy those same structures. Today's academic must stand in a parental position with respect to this artist. He cannot become one of his cronies, and must study him at a distance to glean what he can from him. The academy eventually realizes the life--the art?-- which the avant-garde artists flaunt and change to slowly incorporate them into the core of their disciplines, at the same rate that a parent releases responsibility to a child. When the avant garde artist takes an adolescent swing at the "academy", the cruelest retaliation the academy could undertake would be simply not to notice. The lightweight, however is unconsciously aiming his blows at the heart of the academy--at the fundamental reason for its existence--it must react. The academy is necessary for the avant garde artist to exist. If it weren't for the academy, no one would care about any of their acts, which are for the most part, acts of retaliation. Without something to rile against, there would be no avant garde. Similarly, without the life force of the avant-garde, the academy would become the dead petrified institution which the avant-garde have chosen as their target. It is almost a ridiculous situation to be studying these artists ( a term which they are very conscious of having redefined) in a class at Yale University, an elite University--the off-spring of the academy which the avant garde so roared against. But this academy's situation is certainly not the same one which characterized the academies in Malevich's time. The Academy in general is stereotyped by two roles: the first is a conservative, self-propagating elite who honor people who are similar to them and share similar ideals--for which the academy was notorious at the turn of the century. Such a structure for an institution makes it quite unresponsive to change, especially the type of radical change required to emulate the radical change in lifestyle brought about by technology at the turn of the century. This same Academy, however, is the only institution in the world which is purely interested in humankind's greatest achievements, and which is interested in the different human actions which can be labeled as artistic. In many respect the conservative tendencies of the Academy are to preserve what is most dear to humanity. I don't wish to define art here--such a definition was the demise of the Supremacists and the old academy before them. I only want to say that the academies and the museums--the institutions who are the butt of the nihilism characteristic of many avant garde movements-- are the only institutions maintained to preserve this nebulous quality which we call art.

My anger towards Malevich is two fold. The first is that he fails to recognize the human achievements represented by the institutions he seeks to destroy. Secondly the theory he puts forth does not coincide with the actual art he produces. Any artist who makes theories is on a double agenda which takes away from the work of art produced. Art cannot be made purely from any kind of technical agenda. Malevich's most important role was in getting the academy to realize its own weaknesses, not in adding art to the list of man's greatest creations. One cannot work on two agenda's like that. The motives of the avant garde exterior to art making which include the épatage anger process are ulterior to making art and not conducive to making art. The avant garde in general is more successful at making changes in the academy than at producing works of art. Because of the fractured nature of his text, it is quite impossible to approach Malevich in any systematic way. Let us attempt, however, to follow his idea of the history of art. "The savage" he says, "was the first to establish the principle of naturalism" and "laid the basis for the conscious imitation of nature's forms." He continues:

> The distortion of reality in his depictions is the result of weak technique.#

He implies with this last statement that strong technique would result in a non-distorted depiction of reality.# He has gotten the process of art making completely backwards. It seems to me that technique implies a repetition, a method for making art. Throughout history it has been the breakers of traditional technique, including the avant-gardists, who have created art. Malevich himself says later in the essay that: Between the art of creating and the art of repeating there is a great difference.

To create means to live, forever and forever creating newer and newer things. #

It is those artists who come up with a striking new way of looking at the world, perhaps making a new technique, who actually make art. Giotto, for example, created a system of visual symbols to add several layers of impact to the Bible stories he was relating. He managed to create a more human representation than had been possible using the techniques of artists who came before him. Importance was given to characters by the massiveness of their garments, levels of understanding were added to these images by the movement from one narrative frame to the next and a reflection of the story in the forms of the background landscape. These techniques were then repeated by centuries of Florentine painters. Malevich goes on to claim:

Their [the masters of Rome and Greece] knowledge was taken away from nature into closed studios, where pictures were manufactured for many centuries.

That is why their art stopped short.

They closed the doors behind them, thereby destroying their contact with nature.

And the moment when they were gripped by the idealization of form should be considered the collapse of real art.#

These moments of idealization of form can also be seen as the moments when painterly aspects took over mimetic concerns in the history of art (one of the main precepts Malevich puts forth for Suprematism). The closing of the studio doors implies an overriding concern with the painterly aspects-- with what the painting needs to be successful as a painting rather than how it merely imitates the world. One can hardly say that Malevich's studio doors were flung wide open. This idealization of form for which Malevich chastises previous artists seems to be the first step towards making a painterly art. The subject becomes a symbol, and the artist, free from having to copy nature, becomes more concerned with how his painting works as a painting--the composition, balancing the tonal values and complementary colors etc. One has only to look at some of the greatest artists chose to paint religious figures--idealized forms--to see evidence of purely painterly creation. There is such an incredible moment in how Duccio painted his Madonnas. The artist of that time was faced with quite a problem in painting the Virgin Mary because for religious reasons he did not want to bring attention to her body, yet he was forced to paint it or the image would not be complete. Duccio solved this problem by allowing her dress to remain an almost formless blue shape, no wrinkles or revealing folds, and indicating that this shape was actually her body with the least corporeal of the painterly elements, a tiny gold line. This hem line transforms the blue shape to reading as a dress, and successfully wards off any lascivious thoughts his viewer might have had.

It seems that Malevich's major complaint with the idealization of form would be that in painting ideal forms the artist is in fact repeating himself or previous painters, thus not creating new forms.# I am not going to counter this argument. Copying any type of pattern in art is not art--creation--it is repetition. Victor Shklovsky makes a brilliant move when he calls defamiliarization the technique of art because defamiliarization cannot be copied. Thus technique takes on a new meaning for him, it can only be done a first time and then it becomes familiar. The ideal forms of the Supremacists are not free from the problems of the idealized forms Malevich chastises in previous art. I find that the forms he chooses for Supremacist art hardly offer room for much invention. He chooses forms created from intuitive reason, not found in nature but created entirely by humans:

The forms of Suprematism, the new painterly realism, already testify to the construction of forms out of nothing, discovered by intuitive reason.# He goes on to say:

There is no longer love of the little nooks, there is no longer love for which the truth of art was betrayed.

The square is not a subconscious form. It is the creation of intuitive reason.

The face of the new art.

The square is a living regal infant.

The first step of pure creation in art. Before it there were naive distortions and copies of nature.

. . . .

In the art of Suprematism, forms will live, like all living forms of nature.#

He sets up the love of little nooks as a repetition--"little cows" and "moon-lit landscapes"-- and posits true artistic creation in the creation of intuitive forms. He further limits the intuitive forms which the Supremacist will use to the square. This poses several problems for me. First is whether in fact the square is something new or created. The Greeks, and many mathematicians since have, thought that geometry exposed transcendental truths--these are not truths which were created by men, but only discovered by them. Secondly his choice of the square tends to run counter to the argument he gave against the idealization of forms when he said:

> art should not advance toward abbreviation or simplification, but towards complexity.#

Yet, he has limited the subject matter of Suprematism to such an extent that it becomes quite impossible to arrive at any level of complexity. The square is so simple it is very easy for it to become a convention. It is in fact debatable that the square has been a convention for the history of human kind. Malevich's travesty is that he is so absolutely unconscious of the complexity and variety of life which is present in the art of the generations who came before him that he can allow himself the most painful reductions of subject matter and terrible simplifications of art and life. He blatantly violates his own statement that art should strive towards complexity even within his own essay. He goes on to state more explicitly the the abstract basis of his art and presents the second major painterly element.

Painters should abandon subject matter and objects if they wish to be pure painters.

The demand to achieve the dynamics of painterly plasticity points to the impulse of painterly masses to emerge from the object and arrive at color as an end in itself, at the domination of purely painterly forms as ends in themselves over content and things, at nonobjective Suprematism--at the new painterly realism, at absolute creation.#

Like the square, the interaction of color is a questionable creation. Malevich insists that his pictures be pure because anything beyond the pure interaction of color would be borrowing a subject from nature. If the painting gives a feeling say of pain or peace then it must represent something in nature. Any association of color and or shapes to an emotion is a reference to nature. If someone says that a Rothko reminds them of looking into a landscape(sunset or seascape are usual reactions) the painting is conjuring a visual memory which is blatantly referential. But even if one says the painting is peaceful, he is referencing nature. When you ask a group of people to draw a "peaceful" or "restful" line a very high percentage will make it horizontal. Psychologists have stated that this is because all humans sleep lying down and thus associate a prostrate position in sleep, a horizontal, with the peacefulness of sleep--a cow probably doesn't think a horizontal is peaceful. Similarly, a "V" shape or a diagonal gives the impression of motion because we are all habituated with gravity and immediately associate the bottom of a picture plane with a floor plane and a tilted shape on this plane as being imbalanced: wanting to fall towards the bottom edge. Malevich's attempts not to reference nature begin to seem quite impossible.

Let us, however, consider Malevich a success in making a pure creation for a moment. He has created the purely painterly work of art and a new realism. Since he has eliminated all subject matter, the life in his images comes from the creation of a shape from intuition and from the movement between colors on canvas. We certainly have to ask what has he succeeded in doing. He has compared the creation of intuitive forms directly to creation of nature:

Here is the Divine ordering crystals to assume another form of existence. Here is the miracle. . . .

There should be a miracle in the creation of art, as well.# How, in fact, does he compare to nature? The pure, painterly work of art makes the human power of creation look rather pathetic next to nature's awesome variety. He says that his art is the first step and one has to wonder where the next step can be with all the restrictions he has imposed on himself, especially the limited subject matter he has chosen to use. Would he allow the passionate intensity of the American Abstract Expressionists as a development of his ideas?#

Malevich has not ended art or even paintings with subject matter and meaning exterior to the relationships of colors and shapes on the canvas. Thankfully, there is the academy and people who get angry at such destructive plans undertaken with almost religious ferocity. The avant garde's blind nihilist attack (a phrase which is redundant I'm sure) and the retaliation it demands is precisely similar to the cause of every religious war: where rather short-sighted fanatics, who care about their beliefs or ideas to the extreme that it makes them blind (and anyone but the leader of the religion absolutely unoriginal) butt heads into a stalemate. Though this process seems to be the rule for many major religions in the world, it is thankfully not a formula for art. The situation we have been concerned with has hardly been a stalemate. The avant garde artists have found their solid place in the museum's ranks and into the course catalogs of the most elite universities--proof that these institutions are hardly the archaic structures which the avant garde sought to destroy. The avant garde has been forced to compromise itself as well: their grandiose theories of destruction and elimination of previous art forms with subjects matter and/or meaning has certainly not taken place. The ideas of Malevich and Albers and the rest of the purists who wanted to use only painterly elements to make paintings now make up the first couple of chapters in any introductory art book. The resulting elucidation of the perceptual forces at work in painting have been expanded upon by psychologists and have been used to reread the entire canon of world art, and shown how very conscious artists through the ages have been of these forces even though they weren't so explicitly stated or subsequently researched. It is interesting to note that these books rarely use Supremacist paintings as examples, but rather use the Supremacist's ideas among others to refer to old master paintings, bringing those back to life. The agenda which was always exterior to the making of Supremacist art has been more successful than their works of art themselves.
