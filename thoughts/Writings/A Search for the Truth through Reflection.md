# A Search for the Truth through Reflection

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Lit. 120a-- Narrative Forms  
> Section-- Sandra Ferdman  
> Sept. 30, 1992  
> A Search for the Truth through Reflection  

The narration of the story and the reflection by the looking-glass in Virginia Wolfe's "A Lady in the Looking-Glass- a reflection," are acts which effectively mirror each other. That is to say they each play the same role to different interpreters-that of reflection or reinterpretation. The looking-glass takes a piece of the world outside and sends it towards the narrator, whereas the narrator takes a piece of the world that can be seen from the depths of the couch, (which includes that piece already reflected by the looking-glass), and reinterprets it for us, the reader. For most of the story these two frames are used to try to represent an elusive reality. Each seems to better represent an aspect of the cold hard truth which changes as the limitations of the powers of these reflecting devices are uncovered. The attention Wolfe brings to the limitations of these reflecting devices forces the story to its overpowering conclusion- that truth or reality can not be written, represented or explained: A blank page would be a better representation. The looking-glass is used as a metaphor for the process the narrator is undergoing in trying to recreate or reflect the world it is seeing, and therefore a metaphor for a type of reality. There is a striking double meaning of "-a reflection" in the title. It either stands for the thoughts that are being generated to form this narration, or in its more literal meaning, which in this case comes secondary, it is the image of the lady which the looking-glass reproduces. These two acts are constantly paralleled or, to use a word which is now getting to be excessive in this text, mirrored throughout the story.

The inadequacy of the reflection of the looking-glass vis-a-vis the real world starts to become evident when at the end of the first paragraph Wolfe writes:

> ... reflected in the Italian glass... one could see a long grass path leading between banks of tall flowers until, slicing off at an angle, the gold rim cut it off.#

The glass shows only a truncated piece of the reality outside, and then slices off the rest. The image of reality the glass reproduces can at best be partial. We know intuitively that the glass can only give a two dimensional representation of a three dimensional world, so in this respect, also, it is inherently deficient at reproducing the real world exactly. The inadequacy of the narrator's reflection is not so readily evident.

The text seems to begin as an attempt to reproduce the reality, the feeling, the moment in this house to such an extent that even the presence of a narrator would add an artificiality to the scene. Information about the narrator is kept as far from the reader as possible. One, the most removed pronoun, is used for the entire story. It implies neither gender nor even a subject at all--one could be anyone or even no one. The narrator is forced to occupy a space in this reality, but this space is as diminished and hidden as possible. "From the depths of the sofa one could see..." the story goes. The narrator is peering out from the depths, from darkness--hidden. This point is enforced when Virginia Woolf writes:

The house was empty, and one felt, since one was the only person in the drawing room, like one of those naturalists who, covered with grass and leaves, lie watching the shyest animals--badgers, otters, kingfishers--moving about freely, themselves unseen.

Not only is the presence of the narrator camouflaged, but Woolf says, "the house was empty..."-- not the house was empty except for me, but "the house was empty...." The animals are "unseen." The complete absence of a narrator is attempted.

The first human presence in the story is the room itself: ...the room had its passions and rages and envies and sorrows coming over it and clouding it, like a human being. ...since all the doors and windows were open in the heat, there was the perpetual sighing and ceasing sound, the voice of the transient and the perishing, it seemed, coming and going like human breath...#

The room is described as being human and having human breath. It has passions and rages and sorrows. The absence of a narrator and the personification of the room leaves one to wonder about the possibility of the room communicating to us what is there-- the ultimate reality, without the intrusive presence of a narrator whose presence itself changes the scene. Could the room tell us about, "...things that never happen, so it seems, if someone is looking"? or, the forest tell us about the tree that fell when no one was there to hear it? or, the refrigerator tell us what it is like inside when the light goes out? The possibility of these inanimate objects communicating to us is of course mere conjecture. Creating and even experiencing the moment or the sound of a tree falling in the forest when no one is there to hear or see it is impossible because someone would have to hear or see it in order to create it and experience it. In order for these objects to take on human qualities, a human must imagine these traits in them, whether it is the narrator to herself or on to us. There must be a narrator. If reality exists where there is no narrator, or even preceptor, then reality doesn't exist in this story.

The reader, however, is told about these "things that never seem to happen if someone is looking"-- " shy creatures, lights and shadows, curtains blowing, petals falling.... The room was... full of such nocturnal creatures." The narrator says, "they came pirouetting across the floor, stepping delicately with high-lifted feet and spread tails and pecking allusive beaks as if they had been cranes or flocks of elegant flamingos whose pink was faded or peacocks whose trains were veiled with silver." These lines cannot be described as anything more than imagination. Assuming that the reader is knowledgeable enough to at least know of any creature-- insect or rodent-- that could be in that room, and that there aren't many flocks of flamingos in country rooms, it is impossible for us to figure out what these fanciful creatures-- with high-lifted feet and allusive beaks-- could possibly be. The narrator could be exagerrating the traits of some bug or any combination of explanations, but these images, and even the exaggeration itself, like the personification of the room, are a flight of fancy on the narrator's part. She (if one can accept this feminine pronoun for what has been a genderless narrator) is allowing her thoughts, her perceptions, to cloud the representation of reality.

The mirror now seems to offer an alternative: "But, outside, the looking-glass reflected the hall table the sunflowers, the garden path so accurately and so fixedly that they seemed held there in their reality unescapably."# The narrator's imagination has not had time to work over the scene in the looking-glass and cloud it. But, there is an object in the looking-glass that is mysterious enough that it leaves ample room for exagerration-- Isabella. The first description of Isabella develops a metaphor which exactly mirrors the discrepancies between reality and reflected or imagined reality which we have been discussing. "[Isabella] suggested the fantastic and the tremulous convolvulous rather than the upright aster... The comparison showed how very little, after all these years, one knew about her.... Such comparisons are worse than idle and superficial-- they are cruel even, for they come like the convolvulous itself trembling between one's eyes and the truth. There must be truth; there must be a wall."# These comparisons, she says, these-- what are essentially other flights of fancy-- come between one's eyes and the truth; they cloud our vision. The convolvulous "that twine round ugly walls and burst here and there into white and violet blossoms," hide that wall-- hide the ugly truth.

The narrator tries to state facts about Isabella-- "she was a spinster;...she was rich; ..she had bought this house and collected [its furniture] with her own hands.... Isabella had known many people, had had many friends." Beyond this everything which is said about Isabella is invented by the narrator. The reader has to be careful to see through a network of "almost's" and "if's" and expressions like "judging" and "it seemed", to realize that everything said about Isabella is just the narrator's opinion. For example, she says: "In each of these cabinets were many little drawers, and each almost certainly held letters"--almost certainly she says--not certainly. She continues, "if one had the audacity to open a drawer and read her letters, one would find the traces of many agitations"--if. She has not actually opened a drawer to see these letters but only says if she had, she would find. Finally, she says,"judging from the mask-like indifference of her face, she had gone through twenty times more of passion and experience than those whose loves are trumpeted forth for all to hear."# Many things can be judged from a "mask-like indifference", twenty times more passion is surely only one, and a rather far fetched one at that.

The narrator admits that her imagination is changing her perception of things when she says, "under the stress of thinking about Isabella, her room became more shadowy and symbolic; the corners seemed darker, the legs of the chairs and the tables more spindly and hieroglyphic."# The room is being transformed--more spindly, darker, hieroglyphic. Yet the narrator says, "it was her profounder state of being that one wanted to catch and turn to words, the state that is to the mind what breathing is to the body, what one calls happiness or unhappiness."# In a final attempt to capture this state, she suggests,"one must prize her open with the first tool that came to hand--the imagination."# The imagination, a tool which so far has been used only for clouding reality or for creating beauty--the convolvulous-- around an ugly wall of truth, is now being suggested as the device for prying open the hard clam shell of truth and revealing its essence. This of course is impossible.

Isabella steps out of the looking-glass which has been the frame for all the conjecture the narrator has been imposing on her and steps into the front hall, into the parallel world of that in the glass-- that of the room. "At last there she was, in the hall. She stopped dead.... She stood perfectly still."# She is not pirouetting across the floor, like the narrator's fancy she is still, dead still. This stillness is reminiscent of a former stillness, in the looking glass, when it seemed the realistic alternative to the flocks of flamingos. Isabella is stepping into a more realistic light. "At once the looking glass seemed to pour over her a light that seemed to fix her; that seemed like some acid to bite off the unessential and the superficial and to leave only the truth." This light is the same that had seemed more realistic earlier. The number of "seeming's" is important here too. Everything that Isabella had seemed to be, now seems to be leaving. What seemed to be, now seems to leave, leaving hopefully only the truth. The narrator continues, now with no "seems": "Everything dropped form her--clouds, dress, basket, diamond--all that one had called the creeper and the convolvulous. Here was the hard wall beneath. Here was the woman herself. She stood naked in the pitiless light. And there was nothing. Isabella was perfectly empty."# This of course could be more "seeming", but if one chooses to believe that the truth really was revealed to the narrator, it brings us to an interesting conclusion.

The convolvulous was ripped away from the wall. The blind was removed and the truth was revealed. The reflecting devices, the mirror and the narrator have proved completely ineffective. More than that, the truth was not a wall but was nothing. Isabella the oyster had revealed nothing, " Isabella was perfectly empty." This entire text has failed to create and explain the truth, because the truth is nothing. How can one describe nothing? It would be like multiplying by zero; you get nothing. This idea has been experimented with at other times. Here is one quick example from Leonard Shlain's book Art and Physics about two other art-forms which try to recreate or understand reality. Shlain says:

Ad Reinhardt, a New York artist of the 1960's and the bête noire of modern art, found in the all-black painting the perfect metaphor for the ultimate statement about reality.... Reinhardt described his progression toward black squares as a search for an image like that of the Buddha, which he once called "breathless, timeless, styleless, lifeless, deathless, endless...." Like black holes, Reinhardt's paintings contained everything -space, time, energy, mass, light-yet they contained nothing.# This raises an interesting question about all art-forms. If an art-form is a search for reality and this reality has proved to be nothing, is the ultimate art-form a black canvas, or in Virginia Woolf's case the empty page? Or, on the other hand is all the convolvulous, all the clouding imagination, the mirroring, reflecting, reinterpreting worlds, frivolous because they are not the cold hard reality? I think not. This struggle on Woolf's part to find the limitations of her narrator's and ultimately her own creative powers, through a metaphor of the looking-glass and an investigation of herself, has created a flower-- a trip through her train of thought, her imagination, which leaves us with far more than an empty page, or a black canvas.
