# Mirrors in Merrill and Ashbery (Revision)

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof. Hollander  
> Word and Image  

Both James Merril and John Ashbery have written important poems about mirrrors. I want to investigate these poems perhaps treating them like ekphrasis or a mirror instead of a painting. Merrill's poem "Mirror" is spoken by a mirror who starts:

> I grow old under and intensity
> Of questioning looks.

One will see the results of this aging as the poem advances. The mirror begins its role as a metaphor for a work of art. But, one quickly sees that this does not necessarily mean a work of visual art:

Nonsense,

I try to say, I cannot teach you children
How to live.

Hegel has called poetry, the instructress of the peoples. The echoes of
his comment makes one realize that Merrill's mirror begins as a metaphor
for poetry, But the poem continues:
-If not you, who will?
Cries one of them aloud grasping my gilded
Frame till the world sways. If not you who will?

And it quickly becomes a metaphor for painting. "The world sways" must refer to the image inside the mirror's gilded frame. The mirror as a metaphor for painting is emphasized as the poem continues: Between their visits the table, its arrangement
Of Bible, fern and Paisley, all past change,
Does very nicely.

The mirror is part of a still life which underlines its role as a metaphor for painting. The then mirror takes on a classical role for a work of art: as a mirror for nature. The mirror compares itself to the real world by addressing the window:

> Across the parlor you provide examples,
> Wide open, sunny, of everything I am
> Not. You embrace a whole world with out once caring
> To set it in order.

A mirror can only show a piece of the whole world. Its frame, which the children grasp, sections off a particular fragment. Because it shows pieces of the world rather than the overwhelming whole, one can say that the mirror sets the world in order. It, like the art which it represents, breaks the world up and allows its parts to be placed into categories, where they can be more easily understood, and effectively ordered. The poem continues:

> Years later now, two of the grown grandchildren
> Sit with novels face-down on the sill,
> Content to muse upon your tall transparence,
> Your clouds your brown fields, persimmon far
> And cypress near. One speaks. How superficial
> Appearances are!

One could say that the children have reached the second stage of Narcissism, when Narcissus realizes that the face that he has fallen in love with is his own reflection, and does not exist, and thus that the individual he loved does not exist outside of his reflection. The grown children's exclamation seems also to echo a tenant of modern art. One does not have to stretch the meaning of "appearance", a pretense, to arrive at, "illusions." The children might be crying out "how superficial illusions are": thus subverting painting's traditional role by exclaiming that modernist notion that a painting is a flat surface and shouldn't be considered as an illusion of depth. In any case the children cause the mirror to realize its own one-sidedness and paranoia ensues: Since then as if a fish
Had broken the perfect silver of my reflectiveness,
I have lapses. I suspect
Looks from behind, where nothing is, cool gazes
Through the blind flaws of my mind.

Freud says that the nature of paranoia is that we don't have eyes in the back of our head. The mirror's "behind" is not a place which can ever be seen. It always remains behind. If one were to turn around to try to look at it, it would turn to remain "behind." The mirror is a pathetic fallacy. A point which may escape the reader too accustomed to pathetic fallacy is that this mirror is always talking about us. We too are one sided to ourselves. We cannot see the back of our own heads. We are ourselves the only object in the world for which there are absolutely no conditions which would allow us to see the backs of our own bodies excluding some kind of reflection. This point will become important later in the paper when a self portrait is considered. It is a special situation when a one sided person looks into a one sided mirror. Looking at oneself in a mirror is really about the confrontation of one sided objects. I believe that the peculiar fascination with ones own reflection may have more substantial reasons than the simple Narcissism, or love of oneself. This discussion must be put off until later in this essay

At first the "behind" in Merrill's poem is an area "where nothing
is," but the mirror says:
...As days,

And decades lengthen, this vision
Spreads and blackens. I do not know whose it is,
But I think it watches for my last silver
To blister, flake, float leaf by life each milling-
Downward dumb conceit, to a standstill
From which not even you strike any brilliant
Chord in me, and to a faceless will,
Echo of mine, I am amenable.

The world behind the mirror cannot be called "nothing." As the poem progresses and, possibly as the mirror's paranoia increases, this world becomes more and more of a presence. But the mirror's paranoia transforms into acceptance. When the mirror has finally lost all its reflective backing, it calls out to what it once claimed was "nothing", "I am amenable" Stephen Yenser has commented that "amenable", is defined ,not only "tractable"or "submissive", but also, "open or liable to testing, criticism or judgment."# The mirror is opening itself up to this unknown. Its final statement can be described as little less than an act of faith.

It is well known that Merrill is a fervent believer in the Ouija board as a method of communicating with an other world of spirits. The board in this parlor game becomes the gateway to this other world. Its surface, while remaining flat and impenetrable, nevertheless reveals a various and complex double world, but it is a double world of words. The spirits of the Ouija board are contacted through language. They are not seen. When all of the mirror's reflective flakes have come off, it loses its power to reproduce images. It becomes a window to the world that was behind and previously unobservable. This world has now overtaken the frame, it is as present as the blank wall behind the mirror which is now exposed to the parlor. By describing the mirror's faith in and submission to this unknown world, Merrill coaxes us to communicate with it. The mirror glass in its now clear state, like the Ouija board, is the only separation between these two worlds. If one is to believe that this other world is like the one which Merrill contacts through his Ouija board, and we are not given any other options, one has to say that this is a world of words. Merrill's poem we realize is not about the visual experience of this mirror but about its metaphoric potential. The reflected double world of the mirror becomes a window to a world of words and visually to a blank wall. Merrill's communication to the world on the other side of the Ouija board is through language. The mirror's subjugation to a "faceless will" which has emerged from the "blind flaws of [its] mind" are verbal clues that this poem represents a subjugation of the visual world. The last line and a half of the poem run: "...and to a faceless will/Echo of mine, I am amenable." Echo is the female verbal equivalent to Narcissus. Where as Narcissus can only see his own image, Echo can only repeat other's words. Thus, this invocation of Echo in the last line is a final clue by Merrill that the world on the other side is not a mirror image of the one in the parlor, but an echo of it. We are in the realm of words. Merrill's poetry is about subtle language decorations as demonstrated by the nearly unnoticeable rhyme scheme in "Mirror" where the last syllable of one line is rhymed with penultimate syllable of line preceding it. This scheme is exposed at climactic moments such as when the "fish" who threatens to break the mirror's calm reflecting surface, becomes a part of "superficial". Steven Yenser begins his book on James Merrill, The Consuming Myth by recalling a photograph of Merrill published in Life magazine in an article entitled, "Eleven American Poets." The image is reversed, which Yenser claims, is "NO ACCIDENT". Merrill, he says, "has been a writer uncannily alert to reversals and doublings. The duplicate and the didymous, the obverse and the inverse, the geminate and the specular are part and parcel of his art."# Merrill's language itself he claims, is about mirroring. One cannot call ornament what is one of the most important aspects of a poet's work. However, if Merrill's poetry is more about language than about the visual experience he conjures in this poem, I must stop putting words in his mouth, and quit trying to stretch meaning out of an image he has conjured in me.

John Ashbery is a much more visual poet than Merrill. Ashbery proves to be an excellent mediator between the worlds of poetry and painting. He worked as an art critic from 1957-1966 for the Paris Herald Tribune, was the executive Editor of Art News from 1966-1972, and his poems often grow directly out of a concrete visual experience or problem. Ashbery is a hard poet to understand. Many of his complexities, however, are not, like Merrill's, doublings and redoublings of language but rather of the ideas behind the language. Once the ideas have been elucidated, the language proves to be quite simple for the ideas it represents. Ashbery seeks something which is beyond the words. He yearns to arrive at a meaning or understanding which is not somehow conditioned by language. He explores the territory between normal routes of communication, and often hits a hidden well spring of new meaning. He says in "And Ut Pictura Poesis is Her Name":

The extreme austerity of an almost empty mind
Colliding with the lush, Rousseau-like foliage of its desire to communicate
Something between breaths, if only for the sake
Of others and their desire to understand you and desert you For other centers of communication, so that understanding May begin, and in doing so be undone.

His attempts at communication through his poems and discussion of communication inside them is a major theme in his work. I want to look at moments in his work when a visual experience informs this communication between poet and reader.

Ashbery's poem, "Self-Portrait in a Convex Mirror", grows directly out of a visual experience(Appendix). It is an example of actual ekphrasis. He is describing a painting by the Italian Mannerist, Francesco Parmagianino. The well liked and quite wonderful beginning:

["Self-portrait in a Convex Mirror...]

As Parmigianino did it, the right hand
Bigger than the head, thrust at the viewer
And swerving easily away, as though to protect
What it advertises. A few leaded panes, old beams,
Fur, pleated muslin, a coral ring run together
in a movement supporting the face,which swims
Toward and away like the hand
Except that it is in repose.

The toward and away movement in this opening has been commented on by many critics. It sets up a rhythm for the entire poem. Ashbery is regularly switching, from a kind of Narcissistic involvement with this work of art, when it becomes a real space and mirror of his own world, to the realization that it is simply a painted object. These shifts are generally marked by breaks of the text, but there are many more partial changes of reaction within each of the six sections of this poem. This poem does not under go one transformation, like Merrill's "Mirror", rather it follows the cork of Ashbery's thoughts floating in the tide. Several ideas (sometimes quite contradictory) are touched upon repetitively. None is given precedence.

The true visual experience in this poem comes not so much from the fact that Ashbery is studying a painting, but that this painting poses a visual conundrum and this conundrum is explored. This is a special painting. Ashbery quotes Vasari: Francesco one day set himself

To take his own portrait, looking at himself for that purpose In a convex mirror, such as is used by barbers...

He accordingly caused a ball of wood to be made
By a turner, and having divided it in half and
Brought it to the size of the mirror, he set himself
With great art to copy all that he saw in the glass,"

This direct prose quote of a translation of Vasari, is broken into verses. That it does not stand out markedly from the rest of Ashbery's text emphasizes the fact that his language in many ways is prose which has put into verse. It is not a special language of poetry like Merrill's. In any case, Ashbery is writing this poem in New York, where we must assume he does not have Parmagianino's original to contemplate. He has seen the original which is in Venice he tells us. The fact that he must be looking at a reproduction is particularly important with this painting because the reproduction must be flat, and the original as we are told by Vasari is convex. This fact does not keep Ashbery from meditating about the shape of this painting as though it were in front of him.

The special shape of this painting complicates his willingness to let the painting become merely a depth-less surface, and increases the painting's ability to communicate to Ashbery in a way characteristic of pre-modern poet's communication with portraits. Ashbery, an art critic well versed in the modernist flat picture plane, would find it much harder to view any pre-modern painting as the illusion it was meant to produce. It takes a special painting to key off the intimate discussion he undertakes. Shortly into the discussion he says: The soul establishes itself.

But how far can it swim out through the eyes
And still return safely to its nest? The surface
Of the mirror being convex, the distance increases
Significantly; that is, enough to make the point
That the soul is captive, treated humanely, kept
In suspension, unable to advance much further
Than your look as it intercepts the picture.

This painting is not flat like most every other painting. Since it is convex, it defines a space inside its surface. The shape of the painting, its convexity, Ashbery says, allows the soul of the depicted painter more room to roam about inside the space enclosed by the surface. The shape of this painting has lead him believe in an illusion of depth. The three dimensionality of the painting creates a space in which the soul of the painter can come to life. This is the first of many moments where he tries to find meaning or some kind of communication with the artist in this painting. He is trapped in a dialogue with this art object, not as an art object but as though it were a miniature world.

Let us look for an instant at Escher's print "Hand Holding Sphere" (Appendix). It is a two dimensional representation of the artist looking at himself in a spherical mirror. The change from Merrill's flat mirror to a spherical one is quite significant. There is no "behind" in a spherical mirror. It is the same from every side. More so, a spherical mirror is not stationary like the one in Merrill's poem whose position, like the still life which is placed before it, is past change. A spherical mirror cannot be stationary. It is a ball. It rolls around. One can pick it up to look at it. It actually reflects more than one hundred and eighty degrees. The further away one holds it from one's eye the greater part of the world it can encompass. It is mobile enough to get put in all the nooks and "blind flaws" from where the "looks" come in Merrill's poem. One can see the whole world in its reflection. A spherical mirror seems to solve Merrill's mirror's problem of paranoia. But, one can only look at one side of the sphere at a time, because we remain one sided. The world of our visual perceptions is inherently hemispherical because, as was evoked by Merrill's mirror, one can only see the front half of the world one occupies. The fact that as Vasari tells us, Parmagianino halved the sphere on which he planned to execute his painting is very significant.

Also significant is the fact that originally Parmigianino began with a sphere. One cannot look at a spherical mirror and not have one's eye in the exact middle of the ball. One's face can no more slide to the outside of the ball than one could crawl out of one's own body to look at one's own perceptual universe from a point other than one smack at the center which we are doomed to occupy. Ashbery says, much later in the poem: Today has no margins, flush the event arrives

Flush with its edges, is of the same substance
Indistinguishable#

One can, however, slide one's reflected face to the edge of a halved sphere because it has an edge; thus Parmagianino's painting does no absolutely imitate the real life situation. He could somehow escape his sphere, yet he decided to place his head directly in the center of this mirror and thus maintains the illusion that he is looking at himself in a spherical mirror like Escher. But this illusion can be ruptured. Ashbery says:

The whole is stable within

Instability, a globe like ours, resting
On a pedestal of vacuum, a ping-pong ball
Secure on its jet of water.

This ping-pong ball on its jet of water is a visually inspired metaphor. It implies an equilibrium for Parmagianino's head in the middle of the sphere. Such an equilibrium can be easily upset. The head, or "self-important ship" riding above the large hand, becomes capable of flying off its jet of water. Earlier in the poem Ashbery mentions that:

The soul has to stay where it is,

Even tough restless, hearing raindrops at the pane,
The sighing of autumn leaves trashed by the wind,
Longing to be free, outside, but it must stay
Posing in this place.
When the spherical section of Parmigianino's mirror becomes a metaphor for
the world of sense perceptions in which we are trapped it becomes obvious
that were Parmagianino a living person, his soul could not really fly,
about inside this globe. We cannot leave the dead center of our own
sense perceptions. Thus, it becomes obvious that the ping-pong ball
metaphor refers only to the work of art. Ashbery says:
But life is englobed.
One would like to stick one's hand
Out of the globe, but its dimension,
What carries it, will not allow it.
The sphere in which we are trapped is too large for us ever to reach
the surface. Not to mention that like Merrill's mirror's "behind" it
moves about with us where ever we go. Any step to approach the surface
moves it one step further away.

Let us look for again for an instant at the M.C.Escher print "Hand with Reflecting Sphere"(see Appendix). Escher is holding the spherical ball. His fingers actually touch the reflecting surface. It is this surface which must be recognized as surface, not an illusion of depth, in order for Parmigianino's illusion to fail. Because the flat picture plane pushed into three dimensions, this painting can con- even a well versed modernist into a dialogue with his person in this image. The effect of Escher's hand on the surface is quite different from that in Parmagianino's Self Portrait. Ashbery says:

> Francesco, your hand is big enough
> To wreak the sphere,...

One could never imagine Escher's hand piercing that surface. The points where the representation of the reflected hand meet the representation of the real hand define the hard shell which contains the reflection. Both hands do actually touch the surface. Only at those points are the two hands the same size. But, Escher's mirrored hand is pulled completely out of proportion. One could never imagine one's own hand distorted to that degree. And then the reflected hand has only just reached the surface. Where is this limpid warped thing going to get the strength to break through the surface? This surface has many complicated physical and metaphoric qualities. Ashbery continues:

And just as there are no words for the surface, that is, No words to say what it really is, that is not
Superficial but a visible core, then there is
No way out of the problem of pathos vs. experience.

Here Ashbery's ebb and flow is described. He is caught in the contradiction of accepting the illusion of this work of art or seeing it for what it is: just the flat surface. He is caught between pathos-- that quality in a work of art which arouses emotion or feeling--and experience. We arrive at the first transition, when experience wrenches him away from a relationship with this art object:

> The balloon pops, the attention
> Turns dully away. Clouds
> In the puddle stir up into saw toothed fragments.

I think of friends
Who came to see me, of what yesterday
Was like.

This is precisely the surface disturbance one would have expected. The reflecting puddle explodes into fragments. Immediately after this disturbance of his meditation, which draws him away from Parmigianino's self-representation, Ashbery mentions:

A peculiar slant

Of memory intrudes on the dreaming model
In the silence of the studio as he considers
Lifting the pencil to the self-portrait.
How many people came and stayed a certain time,
Uttered light and dark speech that became part of you
Like light behind the windblown fog and sand,
Filtered and influenced by it, until no part
Remains that is surely you.
His first impulse after getting shaken from his work is to liken his
disturbance to what must have happened to Parmagianino. Still there
are moments when Ashbery is completely removed from any of the pathos
he has felt in this object:

I see only the chaos

Of your round mirror which organizes everything
Around the polestar of your eyes which are empty,
Know nothing, dream but reveal nothing.

Art's pre-modern mission is failing in this passage. The mirror which, like Merrill's should organize the world to define its position as a work of art becomes chaos. The image fails, and the communication of a feeling or something which we know is important to Ashbery's art, also falls flat. The eyes know nothing. The eyes have been an important second metaphor of the reflecting sphere. The painter's eyes transmit information to the poet. They become a third microcosm. To understand the overriding visual conception of this poem, one has to imagine a series of sphere's inside one another: the eye of the painter, his spherical reflection and the world of perceptions around the poet. They are not concentric but they are each connected by gaze. Ashbery describes the largest sphere, the one in which he is stuck. It mimics Parmagianino's: I feel the carousel starting slowly
And going faster: desk papers, books,
Photographs of friends, the window and the trees
Merging in one neutral band that surrounds
Me on all sides, everywhere I look.

The poet's own world has flattened like the painter's. Their situations are now exactly similar. Each is surrounded, trapped in a globe; the poet's world has flattened into one neutral band, which is lining the inside of the globe from which he cannot even budge from the center. There are several moments like this one in the poem; when the poet and the painter's situation become identical. Harold Bloom mentions moments where:

> Times and places come together in the attention that makes the painter's and the poet's room into one chamber.#

The situation is similar to the end of Merrill's "Mirror" where what had at some points in the poem been a surface has become no more than a window between two worlds. These moments of similarity are when Ashbery is most caught in the pathos of this art object and he sees the most of himself in this representation of a painter. It is a Narcissistic situation. Whenever he sees himself in the work of art, he risks getting caught in a self-reflexive cycle with it. Such moments are brought to a culmination towards the end of this long poem. Ashbery begins by talking very specifically about the portrait in the manner of an art historian, quoting ideas from Sidney Freedman:

> The surprise, the tension are in the concept
> Rather than its realization.

The consonance of the high Renaissance
Is present, though distorted by the mirror.

What is novel is the extreme care in rendering
The vellities of the rounded reflecting surface
(It is the first mirror portrait),
So that you could be fooled for a moment
Before you realize the reflection
Isn't yours. You feel then like one of those
Hoffmann characters who have been deprived
Of a reflection, except that the whole of me
Is seen to be supplanted by the strict
Otherness of the painter in his
Other room. We have surprised him
At work, but no, he has surprised us
As he works.

As the passage progresses, Ashbery slips out of Freedman's voice, to a dialogue with "you" until he arrives at a resonant "me." He expects to see his own reflection in the sphere. He has completely identified with this art object. Anita Sokolsky makes what will be a final comment on "Self-portrait in a Convex Mirror:

> The portrait's convexity imitates the shape of any eye, making it difficult to locate who is beholding and who beholden. We become the image, the painting the perceiver, as we gaze; the self-portrait in a convex mirror traps us in a closed perceptual system in which the signifier and signified circulate endlessly.#

This defines Narcissism. Ashbery, however, is not caught endlessly in this cycle. A rhythm is set in this poem by his falling into and out of this closed perceptual system. He can somehow extract himself from this system; he has found a key out of the cycle which brings Narcissus to his death. I wish to carry this discussion of Narcissism and the notion that the reflecting sphere is a metaphor for an eye into a discussion about another poem of Ashbery's, "Wet Casements."

Ashbery's "Wet Casements" begins with the same visual conundrum we have just left. Ashbery was in disbelief that his own face does not appear in the reflection of Parmagianino's ball. "Wet Casements" starts:

> The concept is interesting: to see, as though reflected
> In streaming windowpanes, the look of others through
> Their own eyes. A digest of their correct impressions of
> Their self-analytical attitudes overlaid by your
> Ghostly transparent face.

There are no ends to the hypotheses of transformations one can imagine Ashbery is trying to describe. I believe that this concept can be taken literally, not as some dream mutation. He must be talking, simply, about viewing a self-portrait. The only way of seeing through someone's own eyes, is by somehow stepping into their body. The only way of seeing the look of someone though their own eyes is by stepping into their body when it is placed before a mirror. When viewing a self-portrait one does just that: one steps into the place the painter occupied in front of the mirror: By stepping into the space vacated by the painting's subject, one becomes the object of the painter's reflected gaze . The phrase: "A digest of their correct impressions of/ Their self-analytical attitudes..." can only refer to a painter's painting of himself. We have now arrived at the final problem of this literal reading: The only way that "your/ Ghostly transparent face" could overlay the digest of self-analytical attitudes, is if the painting's eyes were real, so that the viewer's face could be reflected on the painter's wet reflective cornea. Ashbery's metaphor is lapsing into the confusion between art and reality that we have experienced in "Self-Portrait..."

The title of this poem "Wet Casements" comes to mean, eyes. A casement window is one which opens outward. The eyelids are the shutters which can be flung apart to allow in all the light from the outside world. The window panes or corneas must be wet in order to be reflective.

After one of the most powerful metaphors for the randomness and ultimate impossibility of communication between souls who don't necessarily live in the same generation, this increasingly complicated poem embarks on a revision of the Narcissus myth:

I shall use my anger to build a bridge like that
Of Avignon, on which people may dance for the feeling
Of dancing on a bridge. I shall at last see my complete face Reflected not in the water but in the warm stone floor of my bridge.

Here Ashbery is playing the role of Narcissus. Rather than catch his reflection in a pool, he builds his own reflector. It is innately imperfect when compared to water which it is placed above, and thus it becomes a metaphor for a work of art. Only in Dante's Purgatorio could one see one's own reflection clearly in polished marble or stone. This passage makes an interesting reference to the relation between critics and artists. Art is a bridge built for the critics to dance upon and it is they who polish it to the point where the artist can see himself. It seems that as much as the art is built for critics to dance upon, the artist needs those critics to come and dance to polish it for him.

A paper about the representations of mirrors in painting and poetry must be aware of the story of Narcissus . It is found, in Ovid's Metamorphoses, where it is coupled with that of Echo; the domains of images and words are thus similarly coupled. An echo is a verbal mirroring, or reflection. The fact that poetry has appropriated so many terms from the visual world,such as" mirror," "speculate," and "reflect" is further proof of these two art form's inherent and deep connections. The art of poetry has however also appropriated the Narcissus myth applying it to autobiographical writing, when it already has the myth of Echo. The coupling of Echo and Narcissus is of course not symmetrical. Narcissus' story does not mirror Echo's.

Narcissus sees no one. He does not fall in love with anyone until he sees(/falls in love with) his own reflection. Echo can speak to no one, but can only repeat another's words. Ashbery's poem "Wet Casements" seeks to question what would happen if the restrictions on these stories were switched but the domain of reflected images remained Narcissus' and of repeated words Echo's. No matter who speaks, the reflection of this sound is Echo's own voice. So, if Narcissus were to change stories with Echo, the reflection of his image, his face, would have to somehow become everyone's.

This first situation is described in the first stanza. It occurs when somebody is viewing a self-portrait and like the last passage that was quoted from "Self-Portrait in a Convex Mirror" is surprised not to see one's own reflection there. Narcissus' representation of himself becomes the same image everyone sees when they step up to what they imagine is a mirror. Narcissus becomes everyone's reflection. The viewer takes Narcissus' place above the smooth lake, but still sees Narcissus' reflection. The self-reflective cycle is broken.

Echo in order to adopt Narcissus' story would have to somehow repeat her own words and fall into a pattern similar to that inextricable self-reflection that leads Narcissus to his death, but Echo's reflection would be verbal. The two last lines of "Wet Casements" have always seemed very puzzling to me. They stand out, utterly exposed, and are too blatantly self-referential for Ashbery:

> I shall keep to myself.
> I shall not repeat others' comments about me.

They complete the complicated chiasmus. The only way that Echo can speak about herself is if she repeats others' comments about her. If she adopts Narcissus' curse then this self-reflexive cycle will lead to her death when she comes to know herself and realize that the voice which she has fallen in love with is her own. The last lines of "Wet Casements", however, are spoken by Ashbery, not Echo. By saying, "I shall not repeat others comments about me," he once again breaks the endless circle of the Narcissus myth, even when it is taken on by the figure of Echo.

Ashbery, the poet, has often been often accused of Narcissistic verse. He makes the point in a convoluted manner that Narcissus really refers to the world of images. And that there are problems in directly appropriating a myth about one field of perception. Poets must look to Narcissus' verbal partner for warnings about poetry, but this is highly problematic. He has already commented that a self-portrait is not Narcissism because a viewer steps into the self-reflective cycle, and halts the otherwise endlessly reflective cycle. He then rewrites the myth once again by building his own reflector over Narcissus' water. Now in the last lines of this poem, he makes a claim about the inapplicability of this myth to his art: Auto biography is not Narcissism.

There is something very particular about looking at one's face in a flat wall mirror. I mentioned much earlier that it is the only time when a one sided object, yourself, observes another one sided object, the mirror. This one sided-ness is not a flaw in our make up it is a comment upon how we see the world. The one sided mirror can only show us a fragment, as we can only see a fragment. This becomes an age-old parable: that art orders the world. The spherical mirror poses a different problem. It is not one sided. It is true that only our sense of sight is one sided. By using all our means of perception perhaps we can arrive at more complete view of the world. The spherical mirror adds its own parable to this discussion: one cannot look at a spherical mirror without seeing oneself in it--at the dead center of it. Looking at oneself in a spherical mirror is a very different situation than one's own contemplation before a flat one. The spherical mirror presents us with a miniature universe, of which we ourselves are at the epicenter. These visuals inform us about our own situation. A work of art about looking at one's self is not the same as the self-reflexive trap of an individual confronting himself in the mirror. When a viewer or a reader comes into confrontation with it, the self-reflection becomes about communication. When the individual looks at his own image. He is confronted with the only soul which he can ever know. Perhaps the soul can rise further out of its deep encasement, when it is confronted with its own self and not an unfriendly, possibly incompatible viewer. One soon realizes that all art is somehow self-reflexive and at the same time it is about communication. This is Ashbery's strongest message. These are the depths of communication which Ashbery has tried to sound. Poetry as Merrill claimed is not a mirror to the world, but a window to the artist's soul. Painting is not a window, claims Ashbery after a long meditation on Parmigianino trapped in his sphere, but a cage or the flat band of one's own perceptions which creates the sphere around us which we cannot escape. Merrill's poem remains on the surface, it is largely about the language. Ashbery's ruptured surface gives more clues to the mind which lies behind the verses.

The bottle imp is a personification of a soul like Parmigianino's which was allowed, by Ashbery, extra room to roam with in his surface because unlike a conventional painting, it is convex. This bottle imp is the first part of a metaphor for the attempted connections between artists or scholars over generations. Though this poem begins as a visual metaphor, it transforms into a more general one. The role of the bottle imp becomes a name mentioned and lost. The wallet is a (more personal) analogy for a museum or library whose most precious items are gathered from personal collections. The public functions of a library or a museum have to accept what they can get--the name picked up not by the person addressed. The most wanted bit and the person who wants are not united. The bit carefully stored in a collection which it was not meant to inhabit is neglected for years and then deteriorates.
