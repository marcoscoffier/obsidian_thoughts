# The Image of Sex and the Imagination in Don Quixote

> The Image of Sex and the Imagination in Don Quixote  

It is safe to say that there is a recognized social code in seventeenth century Spain, described and I believe supported by Cervantes in his Don Quixote which is closely linked to ideas of purity and morality derived from Christianity. This code places restraints, especially on women, which are utterly idealistic and unrealistic. The Catholic Church to this day has the habit of placing its subjects in unnatural, uncomfortable and mostly impossible circumstances. The Pope, for example, who has never had sex in his life is making decisions about birth control for women in underdeveloped, overpopulated countries. Though no character opposes the ideals created by the church in quotable forms, the impetus of every story is the transgression of one of these rigid social boundaries. These transgressions interest me. They represent aspects of human nature which cannot be suppressed. Cervantes himself is, interestingly enough, cannot be called a transgressor. Don Quixote takes after chivalric romances, but as he says in the preface, he seeks to overthrow "the ill based fabric of these books of chivalry, abhorred by so many yet praised by many more..." The most memorable aspect of Orlando Furioso by Ariosto, one of these chivalric romances which I happen to have read, is an uncontrollable, unrelenting, teaming sexuality which drives though the course of the book. A brief quote is enough to characterize this driving force:

> "A mettlesome charger will often suffer himself to be reined in from a full gallop, however gentle the hand on the rein. Seldom, however, will the bridle of Reason check rabid Lust once it scents its quarry. It is like a bear: there is no distracting him from the honey once he has sniffed at it or tasted a drop left in the jar."#

This rabid lust is present in Don Quixote but is subdued. Cervantes is drawing from this tradition, but incorporating it into the framework which more closely resembles society in his time. There is a deliberate confusion about when Don Quixote takes place. The complex origin of this narrative, from an original, incomplete Moorish text by Cide Hamete Benengeli is elaborately described, yet undermined by the presence of Cervantes' other books and in the second part, of the first part of this book. This discrepancy defines the dual epoques which are incorporated in this text: the time of knights and dragon's, of King Arthur and Orlando, and Spain at the dawn of the seventeenth century.

Women in Cervantes' time, ideally, were to be completely cloistered until marriage, and utterly faithful after. The reason for this severe cloistering seems to be suggested in the narrative by the dire consequences when a woman reveals herself. Women, merely by being seen, drive men to commit acts forbidden by the social strictures, and by denying them the love which they desire, drive them crazy and even to death. There are two key transgressions which will give meat to my argument, by Marcela and Dorotea, two peripheral characters whose stories are recounted in the First Part. A goatherder tells Sancho Panza and the knight from La Mancha, the story of Marcela early in their adventures. The girl, who was renowned for her beauty, suddenly came out of her house dressed as a shepherdess and went to the countryside. The goatherder says:

> And now that she came out in public and her beauty was there for all to see, I could not tell you how many rich youths, gentry and peasantry, have dressed up like Chrysostom and roam through the country side courting her. #

She has created mass havoc; men are crying and lamenting everywhere. When she made it evident to Chrysostom, who was particularly infatuated by her, that she would never love him, he kills himself. Suicide is considered a sin in strict Catholic doctrine, so Marcela's revelation of herself has not only leads the men out of social boundaries, she has prompted one to sin. Furthermore, Chrysostom asked to be buried in the fields ("...like a Moor," the goatherder adds) because that is the first place he ever saw her. The goatherder says that the whole thing "sounds paganish" to him. This may sound trivial in our times, but she has driven him away from religion. She is referred to as the "she-devil"or the "mortal enemy of the human race" throughout the story.

Dorotea, the second transgressor-ess, is not so conscious about revealing herself. She is finally raped by Don Fernando whose passions have driven him into her bedroom. She relates her own story:

> ...I spent my life in such seclusion that I might have been a cloistered nun, unseen, as I thought by anyone except the servants of the house, for when I went to Mass it was so early in the morning and always accompanied by my mother and our maid servants and I myself was so heavily veiled and guarded that my eyes saw no more ground than the space before where I set my feet. Nevertheless, the eyes of love, or rather of idleness, sharper than those of a lynx, discovered that I had attracted the interest of Don Fernando.... no sooner, then, had Don Fernando seen me than he was smitten with a violent passion, as his behavior soon showed.#

The catalyst for each of these situations is the same, the woman has been seen. The teleological mechanism in each of these situations is taken for granted. It is the mettlesome charger described by Ariosto. Women in Ariosto are not, and are not expected, to be cloistered. Thus they are chased and hunted far more vigourously. Angelica, a character who can easily be compared to Marcela, is chased by every knight in the book, almost raped on several occasions, offered to a huge phallus, a log coiling sea monster, to hopefully satiate and subdue his appetite for young maidens, but never wants to give herself to any man. Her name, like Marcela's, is carved on every tree in a forest. Except, Angelica's carvings mark the ultimate rejection of her chastity. Cupid decides that she is too haughty and makes her fall in love with a country boy (well below her status). They carve their names together, in a heart, on every tree near which they have had intercourse. She spends the remainder of the book having sex in the forest. When one of her former infatuees, the title character, Orlando Furioso, sees these carvings and realizes what they mean, he takes after his namesake and goes crazy, terrorizing the countryside, ripping up trees and villages. Though Marcela has also driven a man to his end her personal outcome is quite different. She does not succumb to the mettlesome charger. Her desires she says, "are bounded by these mountains and if they soar beyond, it is to contemplate the beauty of the sky, steps by which the soul journeys to its first abode." Marcela frees herself of the guilt imposed on her by the crowds at the funereal. The Goatherder has said, "she is so watchful of her own honor that not one of her many suitors has boasted nor has the right to boast, that she has ever given him the slightest hope of obtaining his desire." Marcela speaks in her own defense, "...beauty in a modest woman is like distant fire or a sharp sword; the one does not burn, the other does not cut, the one who does not go near it... If in spite of all his discouragement, [Chrysostom] chose to defy hope and sail against the wind, why wonder that he is drowned in the gulf of his infatuation?" Marcela can extract herself from this situation easily. Her interests are "bounded by the mountains"; most women's are not so simple. Marcela is the ideal. She does not succumb nor is even blemished by the sexual force she has so unfortunately ignited.

Dorotea's case is not nearly so cut and dry. Her story closely resembles a typical modern rape dilemma. She is so shocked upon seeing Don Fernando in her room that she is unable to scream, she says, "I was so confused, I repeat, that I had not the strength to defend myself." Yet does say, "I confess I felt flattered by the attentions of a gentleman of his high rank," and eventually after she has gotten him to promise to marry her, though " he was like a man who was so eager to clinch a bargain that he brushes aside all the troublesome conditions he is bound to meet," she offers herself to him. She could have promised him to sleep with him on the next day, or as soon as they were married, but "finally his graciousness and handsome appearance began to incline me toward that course of action that... was to end in my disaster." I really don't want to get caught on the issue of what constitutes rape, I realize this is a very touchy issue, but I only want to say that Dorotea's situation is less cut and dry than Marcela's. Her more complicated ambitions and goals, to possibly marry a man who will raise her social status, and whom she admitted to be flattered by and attracted tonature of her situation is characterized by what she believes God will think and see, thus when she tries to superimpose her situation, onto a religious model which contains the ideal woman whom she cannot be. At first she says, "even though you clasp me in your arms, I hold my soul safe and secure in the purity and innocence of my thoughts..." I assume that her soul being safe means that if she is raped, God will be able to recognize that she was forced, that she is really pure and thus can go to heaven. Later to justify her sleeping with Fernando, she says, "as I am doing nothing which has not been done before, I should be wise to accept this honor that fortune offers me, for even if his desire only lasts until he has had his way, I shall be his wife in the eyes of God." What exactly does God see? One has to ask. In the first case he sees beyond the action to the inner thoughts of her soul, in the second he sees only the action, and must assume them in love and married. The religious framework cannot be adopted to fit a situation with a woman, who is a real person, with aspirations and desires. I have not mentioned Don Quixote himself, he too is a transgressor and he too falls short of his equivalent in Ariosto (Dorotea can be compared to Alcina, she finally gets her man. Alcina was a sorceress who trapped men with her illusion of beauty, and then, when she got bored of them she would turn them into trees and bushes, trap them forever. Dorotea gets her man, but she only gets one and her bounds to him are far more tenuous.) One of the goatherders is questioning Don Quixote about the customs of a knight-errant. He says that whenever he has read about two knights meeting, they call upon their women, before the joust , and that the joust is so quick that:

I don't see how the dead man could have had the time to commend himself to God in the course of such rapid action. It would have been better if the words spent on commending himself to his lady during the charge had been reserved to his duties and obligations as a Christian.

To this the knight of the rueful countenance replies:

> I say it is impossible that there could be a knight errant without a lady, because it is as proper and natural for them to be in love as for the sky to have stars.#

These notions of proper and natural are carried over from the books of chivalry whose values Don Quixote has internalized. Obviously, Don Quixote falls short of embodying these values. He has never seen Dulcinea of El Tobaso his lady, yet he claims to be in love with her. The mettlesome charger we have been discussing was always created as a reaction to the image of the woman who becomes the desired quarry. Remove the image, and the drive loses its impetus. As is demonstrated by the scene with Maritornes, the young prostitute at the first inn where Sancho and the Don spend the night. Maritornes enters the loft where they all are sleeping to meet a courier, but bumps into Don Quixote: ...he transformed her in his fantasy into the likeness of that princess of whom he had read in his books, who came thus adorned to see the grievously wounded knight, being overcome with love for him.#

He has already mentioned all of the fine garments she is wearing and, "thought he held in his arms the goddess of beauty herself," yet he will not have sex with her. He sites his wounds and more importantly his devotion to his lady as reasons against their intercourse. Any knight in Orlando Furioso presented with the woman whom Don Quixote thinks he has before him would have slept with her. I agree this is an odd statement, but epic hero's of whom Quixote is a mockery have not had any restrictions on their sex drive: Odysseus sleeps with Calypso for seven years while his wife is a model of virtue, and the knights in Ariosto have even less constraints.

I read an article in this month's Playboy, Sex in the Age of Illusion, by Pete Hamill which I can not resist including this paper. It helps bring this discussion to our day. It is about Madonna, one of the leading stretchers of the bubble of sexual restrictions today. He says that Madonna though she "appears to be a fearless sexual adventurer," has a "wink" in her act. She is fully aware that AIDS has made sexual freedom a "ghastly joke" he continues, and that, "the only completely safe sex is the sex you can imagine--that is, an illusion." And I quote:

If you can't have something you desire with every atom of your flesh and blood, you must be content with a gorgeous counterfeit. That insight became the shrewd armature of her work. And she elaborated on it with a shrewd understanding of sexual psychology: The most reliable erogenous zone is the human mind and the libido feeds on images not ideas.

Madonna is utilizing the mechanism in which the kight-errant from La Mancha is trapped. She has fully understood what the image of a woman can do, but further than that she has understood what the image of sex can do; she sells herself just as much to a female audience. Pete Hamill goes so far as to call her "the triumphant mistress of her medium: the sexual imagination."

Sexual restrictions in America today are being enforced by the fear of a world wide epidemic, in seventeenth century Spain they were upheld by the church. Cervantes and Madonna have dealt with incorporating unsuppressible sex drive into an unaccommodating situation. They have pushed it into the imagination. Ariosto's world in Cervantes' novel lives in Don Quixote's mind. Ariosto wrote Orlando Furioso one hundred years before Don Quixote. The societal restrictions at that time must have been very similar to those Cervantes describes, which means Orlando Furioso must have been received much the way Madonna is received today: some see her as a welcome force for human sexuality, others as a lascivious sinner who sells sex. Cervantes realizes that the unsuppressible human sex drive exists, which Madonna has never doubted, but, like Peter Hamill's Madonna, makes it live only in the imagination.
