# Richard Diebenkorn: Painting against the Grain (Second Draft)

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Weinberg  
> 20th c. American Art  
> Michael Lobel--section  
> Dec. 6, 1993  
> Richard Diebenkorn: Painting against the Grain  

One of the most interesting polarities in art is between representation, at one end of the stick and, abstraction, at the other end, and I've found myself all over that stick.--Richard Diebenkorn#

Richard Diebenkorn, a California artist, has set himself apart from the reigning styles and trends of his days to pursue his art. Michael Kimmelman, chief art critic of the New York Times has said, "when the New York crowd zigged [Diebenkorn] zagged."# But, Diebenkorn has always sold very well and had a remarkable career financially especially considering that he could not be associated with the major art trends of his generation (he came after the first generation abstract expressionists). He has always bred comments such as Dore Ashton's: He appears to me to be a born painter.#

and Hilton Kramer's:

> Painting seems, in [Diebenkorn's] case, not an option but the mode of thought he was born into. (About how many American painters can that be said I wonder?)#

I want to call Diebenkorn a "violent personalist" a statement coined by Harold Bloom referring to himself and by which he means that he is not concerned with poets whose motives are sociopolitical unless they are first and foremost concerned with making an excellent poem which is deeply meaningful to the individual.I have always found Diebenkorn's work to be very moving and beautiful. His pictures strike some kind of resonance in me. There is an inexplicable something which I find very satisfying in his work. The inexplicability is inherent to this reaction so I am not going to spend an entire paper trying to convince you of its existence. Instead I wish to look at a period in Diebenkorn's art where he switched from abstraction to figuration. Diebenkorn developed a name for himself as an abstract expressionist in the forties. He was one of a few young San Franciscan hot shots who taught at the California Institute of Fine Arts with Clyfford Still with the frequent visits of Mark Rothko. Diebenkorn never had a good relationship with Still and toward the end of 1955 switched to figuration the time when abstract expressionism was at its high point in the New York art world. Diebenkorn was grouped along with two friends David Park and Elmer Bishoff, by Paul Mills, director of the Oakland Art Museum at that time, into a school or style of Bay Area Figuration. Their style was described as "applying an abstract expressionist use of paint to realistic subject matter."# John Gruen who had some of the most wonderful interviews with Diebenkorn said that:

> Individuals and institutions with an investment in abstract expressionism tended to feel that the return to figuration was a defection from the mainstream of contemporary work. Those who had always questioned abstraction or expressive abstraction felt a validation in the defection of serious artists and particularly in that of a fast rising figure such as Richard Diebenkorn.#

After a little more than a decade of figurative painting Diebenkorn switched back to abstraction in 1968 as decisively as he when he made the first transition. This second transition dated and destroyed many of the arguments made supporting his switch to figuration as a battle won in the war against the domination of abstraction in the art world of the fifties and sixties. Diebenkorn didn't make either of these switches to bring attention to himself, or to raise the value of his art work or even to make any kind of statement about art (actions which seem to consume the bulk of the efforts of most of the New York Art world and much of art in America today). He has said, in one of his most adamant statements, "Of course, I don't go into the studio with the idea of ‘saying' something-- that's ludicrous."# Each of his transitions was made because he felt he needed to make a change, at a particular part of his career, for himself. Herschel Chipp says that:

> Richard Diebenkorn was one of a small group of young San Franciscans who...saw the personal feeling of the artist as the sole motivation [for revolting against the prevailing influences of Synthetic Cubism and a lethargic adaption of the Mexican muralist's style].#

Herschel Chipp is right that Diebenkorn's art is personally motivated but his statement is a bit misleading, because Diebenkorn hates to have himself associated with a school. John Gruen recounts a statement of Diebenkorn's:

> We didn't know we were forming a school,not until Paul Mills came around and told us we were a new movement--the California Figurative Painters! Well, I just hated this thing of getting people together and forming a school. I remember feeling wildly threatened by that.#

Diebenkorn is annoyed because he sees his art as his personal enterprise and being labeled as part of a school throws him into the midst of the "style" wars which rack the art world. Though he is such a solitary figure, his could not help but rouse reaction in the art world.

Diebenkorn made a statement about the reception of his early figurative pictures, which describes something very much like the reaction one would expect from an avant-garde work:

> ...during the ‘50s abstract painting was a religion , and it didn't admit to any backsliding to representational imagery. I mean when the three of us-- Park , Bishoff and I -- had exhibitions of our representational stuff in New York, we were attacked you wouldn't believe the incredible things people said to me!#

I believe it is this reaction which made critics want to make a movement out of his return to figuration. Anger has been the initial reaction to many an art movement. It is produced when someone's values are impinged. Abstract Expressionism had stolen the art market, dominated the sentiments of modern art lovers, and had even risen the validity of the U.S. as an art power ready to challenge Europe. It is understandable how Diebenkorn's rejection of these values could create such anger. Similarly vehement reactions had been expressed by the ignorant about avant-garde art. It is interesting that Diebenkorn's movement created anger among the connoisseurs. If one sees art as a battle of styles then the step to make a style out of a different way of painting is only logical. Sidney Tillim, a figurative painter who, starting in 1961, wrote a series of articles supporting a "new realism"counters:

> Representation is an impulse not a style. Yet the tendency today is to judge it in terms of received ideas about modernism and, therefore style. A straightforward approach to representation, to art which quotes the Renaissance rather than comic books, is dismissed as academic, while the possibilities of a new narrative art are not even imagined. Instead sociology is substituted for literature, trends for history and topicality for quality. The past is denied a role in the vision of contemporary experience.#

Diebenkorn does not quote the Renaissance, but has definite ties to the great Modern masters-- Matisse and Cezanne especially. He has always admitted to being deeply affected by these artists. He was sent to Russia by the state department in the sixties where he saw some very famous Matisse's. He made two paintings "Recollections of a Visit to Leningrad (p.138)" and "Large Still Life (p.139)" upon his return which are very deeply influenced by these. I do not wish to trace the influence of Matisse or Cezanne throughout this artist's work, but wish to propose that his his return to figuration may have something to do with his deep feelings for these artists. Matisse and Picasso never continued absolute abstraction but maintained a kind of figuration. Diebenkorn saw Matisse's "The Studio- Quai St. Michel (p.18)" at the Phillips in Washington D.C. very early in his art career, when he was serving time as a Marine in Quantico, Virginia. It is an example of what he was trying to do in his first figurative paintings, but I will return to this later.

I find that even in his early abstractions, Diebenkorn was leading towards representation if not outright figuration. These paintings were often referred to as Abstract Landscapes a term he initially resented because he would constantly try to eliminate any form of representation which would creep into these paintings. He eventually gave up ruining pictures to eliminate the accidental representational element and instead tried to emphasize these aspects when they were present. Many of these works are even referred to by the images people tend to see in them, "The Green Huntsman(p.53)" "The Archer(p.56)," "Motorcycle Wreak(p.42)" and "The Disintegrating Pig(p.39)" are four famous examples. Diebenkorn, has described his motivations for the change from abstraction, which are riddled with ideas and vocabulary of Clement Greenberg the most powerful advocate of Abstract Expressionism:

> I came to mistrust my desire to explode the picture. At one time the common device of using the super-emotional to get ‘in gear' with a painting used to serve me for access to painting too, but I mistrust that now. Something was missing in the process-- I sensed an emptiness as though I were a performer. I felt the need for an art that was more contemplative and possibly even in the nature of problem solving. It was almost as though I could do too much too easily. There was nothing hard to come up against. And suddenly the figure paintings furnished a lot of this.#

In performing he feels an emptiness-- a definite reference to the apostles of Clement Greenberg, the action painters. He has mastered the elimination of the resistance of the medium but finds that art making is now too easy. He felt that he needed some of that resistance. He read Greenberg's writings closely and I believe that Diebenkorn's art makes a lot of sense in respect to these arguments. Greenberg says in "Towards a Newer Laocoon":

> It suffices to say that there is nothing in the nature of abstract art which compels it to be so. The imperative comes from history, from the age in conjunction with a particular moment reached in a particular tradition of art. This conjunction holds the artist in a vise from which at the present moment he can escape only by surrendering his ambition and returning to a stale past. This is the difficulty for those who are dissatisfied with abstract art.... Abstract art cannot be disposed of by simple minded evasion. Or by negation. We can only dispose of abstract art by assimilating it and fighting our way through it.#

Diebenkorn seems to be trying to go beyond abstraction by going through it to figuration--to use what he has learned in abstraction and apply it to a new kind of figuration. Herschel B. Chipp wrote an article "Diebenkorn Paints a Picture" where the creation of "Woman by the Ocean" is documented. The process of reworking described in the making of this figure painting is the same as in his abstractions. Diebenkorn paints constantly rearranging the figure and the space around it waiting for the chance element which makes the painting work to come out of the painting itself. He then tries to capitalize on that element the way he would the chance representations which seemed to work in his abstractions. The figure does not seem to fit into this mode of painting. It is a form which must be completed. That essential chance element can be lost in the completion.

He was very interested in how the presence of a figure which will automatically draw much of the attention of the viewer would affect the mood of his his abstract spaces. This is an issue which comes directly out of Matisse's "The Studio- Quai St. Michel." I find that Diebenkorn's figures in these type of paintings often seem to rupture the two dimensional painting space. "Girl with Three Coffee Cups(p.91)," in Yale's collection "Girl on a Terrace(p.115)" are paintings very similar to "Woman by the Ocean" Most explicitly is "Woman in Profile (p.123)" were the figure is separated from the space by a window. She cannot blend in with the shapes because she is separated from the two dimensional picture space (landscape) outside the window. There can be no all over feeling of paint handling like there is in the abstractions because the figure is separated from the space. Diebenkorn has also said about his switch:

> You see I was trying to demonstrate something to myself-- that I wasn't going to get stuck in any dumb rut. I felt I could move on to something else. I got a kind of a thrill out of doing that . I said, "I can leave all of this behind."#

This is the bold kind of statement one would expect from a great artist. The greatest artists have remained inquisitive and curious until the end of their lives: Michelangelo developed the half finished sculptures into his eighties, and Goya (despite the fact that he seems to have gone insane) created some of his most original work on the walls of his home; the same can be said for the modern masters Matisse and Picasso whose respective deathbed paper cutouts and uncanny knack at consistently foreseeing artistic movements before younger generations have proven their spot in the history of art. Finding a mode of art making which works and simply repeating it is hardly an artistic endeavor. Diebenkorn created a different kind of figure painting, less reminiscent of Matisse which works better than the aforementioned examples. The shapes of the figure integrate with those of the background. In "Seated Woman, Green Interior(p.130)" Girl Smoking (p.131)" and "Woman at a Table in a Strong Light (p.126)" the shapes of the figures and their surroundings work together much more coherently. The figure is not set in opposition to the space but is treated in the same way.

I find the most effective and beautiful use of the figure to be in his drawings which are taken directly from observation, unlike the paintings which he developed the same way he would an abstract canvas. When Diebenkorn paints or draws directly from the figure, something else goes on. He cannot move the figure around as willingly as in the other paintings nor can he change the sex and placement etc. The shapes must be organized in his eye. He cannot react to what is going on in his painting but must react to what he sees. The dialogue is not between him and the painting but between him and his visual experience of the model. Images like "Seated Woman 1967(p.125)," and "Reclining Woman, 1964 (p.125)" seem much more complete and beautiful to me than any of the others I have mentioned. The shapes which describe the figure come to have the power of his earlier abstractions. I find the way he organizes the shapes of these figures, even cropping them so that they completely fill the page to be utterly beautiful. It seems the model has become his painting space. The space around the model is much more shallow. I find Diebenkorn's use of filling the space with the model to be similar to Matisse's "Blue Nude" series though Diebenkorn chooses to crop the model rather than warp her the way Matisse does. (There is a wonderful book by John Elderfield, The Drawings of Richard Diebenkorn which is on order by the Yale Library system which is full of many more such examples) Diebenkorn seems to move much more fluidly from abstraction to landscape and still life than he did to figure painting. As I have mentioned his abstractions have always been referred to as abstract landscapes. A painting like "Seawall (p.103) or "Chabot Valley(p.87)" have the completed unchangeable feel of his abstractions. He does not have the option of moving the shapes around at will--the dark green triangle in the middle of "Seawall" wouldn't make sense anywhere else it represents some kind of shadow off the wall, but one wouldn't want to move it. This is exactly what he looks for in an image:

> All I want is for a set of configurations to stay put. I want them to look as though they were meant to be there. That's my idea of ‘meaning.' When that finally happens well then the picture is done.#

I feel that I could move Diebenkorn's early figures around the way he did. They are not settled in their spaces. Yet, the drawings are fixed. The shapes and composition come to have the same meaning as his landscapes. For some reason he does not have to fight to do this in landscapes. He can find the shapes in nature. His still lives are the same way, he can set them up to create the composition he desires in the painting and then uses his talent to complete the painting. A striking similarity can be drawn between "Table Top/ Cane Chair(p.136)" and "Berkeley No.42, 1955 (p.78)"the smaller shapes to the right in the lower white half of the abstract painting become the white letters and envelopes in the representational one. Diebenkorn's rhetoric remains consistent throughout his career. His best figurative paintings echo the most aspects of his earlier abstractions.

Stanley Tillim, made a powerful general comment about Diebenkorn:

> ...the case of Richard Diebenkorn, a crucial personality in the history of new "realism,' is very instructive. Diebenkorn received his rhetoric entirely from Abstract Expressionism while his sense of subject, basically intimiste, resisted the moral elevation implicit in Abstract Expressionism's ‘sweeping' painterly grandeur. I suspect that his return recently to abstraction was compelled by the fact that he could not fulfill a rhetoric to which his subject matter was essentially antagonistic. Diebenkorn's rhetorical style elevated his subject matter to a plane of significance greater than anything accomplished by Pop rhetoric, but his painterly instinct apparently insisted it was not enough. His reversion to abstraction then, is, I think, more a matter of uncertainty rather than a conviction and is not actually contradictory.#

I find that he did manage to make paintings where the subject was less antagonistic to the rhetoric of the abstractions, but Diebenkorn's best figures (which he calls drawings) are the least connected to the painterly grandeur-- working from life is a great restriction on the essentials of abstract art as described by Clement Greenberg: The purely plastic or abstract qualities of the work of art are the only ones that count. Emphasize the medium and its difficulties, and at once the purely plastic, the proper, values of art come to the fore. Over power the medium to the point where all sense of resistance disappears and the adventitious uses of art become more important.#

Diebenkorn decided to switch back to abstraction in 1968 when he started his Ocean Park Series which he continued until he died. These paintings have lost the recognizable elements of the prefiguration abstractions--perhaps he had purged the latent need for subject in his twelve or so years as a representational painter. The Ocean Park abstractions still refer to a tangible experience of his visual world. People will always claim to see the California light and colors in them, or even claim that the compositions-- the groups of parallel lines parallel to the edges of his paintings which are so common to this series--are reminiscent of the architecture of his California neighborhoods.

I would like to contrast a second statement to Sidney Tillim's though it is much less clear. I cannot say that Hilton Kramer is an advocate of abstraction, but he is definitely looking for something more complex and subtle than simply figuration in art. He says:

> Diebenkorn's sensibility seems about equally divided between something peculiarly American--a sense of preference for the emphatic, at the expense of fine discriminations, that is given overt expression in the surface rhetoric of his pictures--and something that looks like an allegiance to the great tradition, which is the custodian of the language of fine discriminations. This sensitivity is animated by high ambition, but the ambition is compromised (increasingly, I think) by the division. Traditionally the leverage that American artists have enjoyed in confronting tradition--which is European tradition--has been existential.... They brought an experience to art that was employed to deal with it. What Diebenkorn has done --and in this he may be taken as representative of a generation-- is to trade this existential leverage (or rather identify with it) an aesthetic idea, namely Abstract Expressionism, which was itself deeply in debt to the artistic tradition it is called upon to confront. He has thus sealed off a source of power that even a talent of his power cannot do without.#

The "existential leverage" he mentions is simply the virtue of our being American. An American doesn't have to identify with a school or tradition of European Art. This gives the American artist a kind of freedom from tradition which allowed a painter like Jackson Pollock to happen and thrilled the likes of Matisse and Duchamp when they visited the U.S. (The more avant-garde of the two being almost its definition, decided to stay.) This leverage raised the American Avant Garde into a position where it could rival the European art world. Diebenkorn, according to Hilton Kramer is not in touch with this "American" quality: Beneath the fresh, hip, energetic surface of his paintings is an artist dreaming of the masters#

Only in modern time could such a statement be taken as a kind of an insult. I have mentioned that perhaps it is Diebenkorn's dreams of the masters which caused him to turn to figuration, Matisse and Picasso both backed off complete abstraction to a type of figuration. But Diebenkorn's move towards figuration is also the action of his which is the closest some kind of avant garde movement-- return to figuration when abstraction dominates the art world. Diebenkorn did not care what the art world was doing, and made his switch which prompted the fuss over a new style, the Bay Area School etc.because he had a personal quandary to work out, but I cannot help but see his return to the regular Ocean Park series as a weak move in comparison. I do find the paintings in the series to be some of his best, the most refined and sometimes the most moving, but I miss the inquiry and questioning about art, his art, which went into his figurative years.

Still, I am strongly drawn to Diebenkorn's paintings of all three periods. Paul Mills the man who originally declared the Bay Area Figurative's School made a remark which may at first seem very condescending but it brings to light a fundamental aspect of art:

There are two kinds of significant painters in the world--the talented and the untalented. among the untalented a few have the strength of character, the vision, and sometimes just the stubbornness to create some germinal form of new art structure. Cezanne, for instance, was one of the ‘untalented.' In comparison to the figure drawings of his contemporaries, his are obviously rather inept. Certainly Jackson Pollock for all his immense effect on modern painting, was ‘untalented.' Somehow this often seems true of those who initiate the most monumental changes in direction in painting.

Diebenkorn, on the other hand, belongs to the world of the talented: those who can draw beautifully, who have an unfailing sense of color and brushstroke, who have--pardon the expression--taste....I am a person who likes a bit of talent in a painter. whatever the theoretical significance of a work, it gets a bit academic without the sensuous, less describable satisfactions of looking at superb painting.#

Critics and artists can be drawn to Diebenkorn by his talent, in the same way that others can be turned off by his lack of innovation. Mills goes on to say that:

> "talent can... be as much of a problem as an asset. For every artist with something to say but the inability to say it well there are two who could say something well if they just had something to say...[those] who first dazzle one with their brilliance but later cloy with their repetition of successful formulas."#

I'm afraid that I feel this repetition in the Ocean Park series. I wish I could see as much variety and search there as in the figurative work. But I will always be drawn to and moved by his paintings because they are in touch with a sensitivity and care for the personal which I often find to be lacking in American art today
