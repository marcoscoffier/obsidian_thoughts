# Giovanni da Milano's Joachim and Anna Series in the Rinuccini Chapel

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof. Gilbert  
> Pioneers of the Renaissanceq  
> April 23, 1993  
> Giovanni da Milano's Joachim and Anna Series in the Rinuccini Chapel  

To talk about Giovanni da Milano's fresco cycle, the stories of Joachim and Anna, in the Rinuccini chapel in Santa Croce without mentioning Giotto and Taddeo Gaddi would be impossible. Many of the devices Giovanni uses successfully are easily attributable to a breakthrough made by one of his two more illustrious predecessors. These three artists all worked in Florence on chapels in Santa Croce and each painted a fresco cycle which included the story of Joachim and Anna at some time in there respective careers. Taddeo Gaddi's Joachim and Anna is in the Baroncelli Chapel of S. Croce. He was a student of Giotto's and probably assisted his master in painting the Arena Chapel Frescoes in Padua which are the first pictoral representation in history of these stories. Examining these three fresco cycles together allows for a more complete understanding of Giovanni's work. To say that Giovanni just synthesizes the advances of his two fellow Florentines would be to short change an artist who has a remarkable touch and sensitivity of his own. Unfortunately, not all of Giovanni's differences from these two artists are for the better, but he does utilize three dimensional space in a more complex manner, and can represent very subtle expressions which add emotion to these scenes.

The story of Joachim and Anna is told in Jacobus de Voragine's The Golden Legend which is the same text which would have informed each of these artists of these stories. It goes like this, Joachim and Anna have been married for twenty years, and have had no offspring:

And once, when Joachim and his kinsmen went up to Jerusalem at the feast of the Dedication, he approached the altar with them, in order to offer his sacrifice. A priest saw him, and angrily drove him away, upbraiding him for daring to draw near the altar of God, and calling it unseemly that one who lay under the curse of the Law should offer sacrifice to the Lord of the Law, or that a childless man who gave no increase to the people of God should stand among men who bore sons. At this Joachim was covered with confusion, and was ashamed to return to his home, lest he have to bear the contempt of his kindred, who had heard all.

He went off to dwell with his shepherds. One day when he was alone the text continues, an angel came to him and told him he was going to have a daughter and that he was to name her Mary and that her son would be Jesus. After all this good news the angel tells Joachim:

> And this will be a sign to thee: when thou shalt come to the golden gate of Jerusalem, Anna thy wife will meet thee there, who now grieves at thy tarrying, and then will rejoice to see thee.#

After this meeting the Virgin is born. This is as far as the Giovanni da Milano cycle goes because he died in the process of painting it and another artist took over the Presentation of Mary at the Temple and the subsequent marriage the Virgin.

Giotto was the first to represent this story, and neither of the other two make any changes to the story itself, they just choose to highlight certain aspects because neither has the expansive space Giotto was allotted by the Scrovegnis. How the movement in each scene corresponds to the overall movement of the narrative is very different in each of these works. In Giotto it is very simple, you read from left to right like any comic strip and the motion in every scene (which has motion) is from left to right. Joachim is being pushed out of the temple towards the right. He arrives at the shepherds from the left. He arrives to meet Mary at the gate from the left. Even the woman bringing a gift at the nativity of the Virgin which is an otherwise still scene, enters from the left. Taddeo has no set pattern, he actually makes his figures move in a direction opposite that of the sequence of the scenes. Taddeo's top panel splits the scenes of the expulsion from the temple and the dream with the shepherds. Instead of having Joachim expelled to the right towards the shepherds, he is sent off to the left which helps to separate the two scenes (the motion is put back to back). Giovanni's movements are more complex; he utilizes all three dimensions. In his expulsion, Joachim comes towards us: he is in front of the structural support which seems to be the furthest forward in the picture plane which has the same affect as Taddeo Gaddi's last supper in the dining room of the convent of the Fransicans; Joachim is pushed into our viewing space. The actions in the temple which show what Joachim could be doing (the same way the two figures in Giotto's temple did) are behind the priest. A priest figure who is recieveing a lamb from two eager men to the left has his back to the priest. He faces the opposite direction than that towards which Joachim is being pushed. A long, heavy, Giottesque back, kneeling towards the temple, further closes the scene inwards. Being inside the temple and out is not separated by right and left here as it was in Giotto, but as front and back, inside the painting and out. The next scene splits Joachim's dream with the meeting at the golden gate, but the motion is a kind of three dimensional "L" shape. The earliest event, the annunciation, occurs behind the other images. The angel is tiny and distant in the middle. As one's eye comes down from the angel along the sharp edge of the mountain side which separates the two scenes, the gaze of a shepherd who is cut in half by the frame moves us to the right, the same direction the traveler (who is in the center of the image) faces, and we end with the meeting in which Joachim arrives from the left. This composition places the traveler in the center of the scene. Giotto showed that his traveler was in motion by not having him completely in the scene; he was cut in half by the frame, which allows Joachim and Anna's meeting to be the center of attention. He also was the first to dress the shepherds in short tunics so that their bodies wouldn't be as massive as the main character's. Taddeo does not cut his traveler in half like Giotto but has him off to the left; the traveler balances Anna's entourage and creates the movement because Joachim and Anna's coming together balances itself out. Giovanni has placed his traveler in the dead center of the painting: Anna's entourage is squashed into a tiny gateway. It is obvious that if you want to stay with the same format that Giotto had created for these scenes and put them both on the same panel, the traveler does risk being in the center of the image. Giovanni, one can assume, tried to minimize the importance of the traveler by placing his head in a valley. The mountain behind the dreaming Joachim and the city walls behind the meeting of Joachim and Anna, in the language developed by Giotto, let us know that those two scenes are of weight, that they are important. But, one cannot deny the fact that compositionally the traveler is in the middle, he turns his head to catch our glance. One has to wonder if he could be an allegory for Joachim's travels and return home and therefore deserve to be in the center because he is the link between these two images. In any case his placement there is the cause of a combination of two scenes developed by Giotto, some new innovation or composition would be necessary to achieve a scene in which the importance of the subject matter is balanced the way Giotto's were, but no attempt is made to do that, nor is any attempt made to make a new statement such as, the traveler is the important one: he is what this painting is about. The statement can be made, but it is not similar to a statement which can be made about any other of Giovanni's works I have seen so it remains the particular result of combining these two scenes. The nativity has no three dimensional movement. The scene takes place in one room which lacks the depth of the previous scenes, but depth in those scenes was a result of the movement. A movement through space (in the expulsion) and in time (the scene with the traveler) defined the space and created depth into and out of those paintings. The nativity scene is not a scene about movement and is thus presented in a relatively two dimentional space. Even the movements to the right and left, the arrival of the gift from the left and the woman who reaches from the right for the baby Virgin, counterbalance each other. An analysis of the way the key gestures are handled in these cycles can bring us to new conclusions. Giotto's expulsion is built from the heaviness of the figures, the priest's weighty, thick arm builds up to its climax: the small piece of Joachim's robe which he has grabbed turning Joachim's whole body. Joachim's reticence is shown by the fact that he is half turned, he does not want to go. He looks back, hurt, gently cradling the lamb. We empathize with him. The visual image of his situation is so clear: he is being pushed away; he does not want to go; he is a kind man who cradles a lamb; we can empathize with him. Taddeo relies on the fact that Joachim is leaning over to the left. The priests gesture is hidden by Joachim's body. I find the expression on Taddeo's Joachim to be ineffective. Making sense of Giovanni's expulsion relies upon the composition. Joachim blocks the priest's gesture and does not lean. If he did not stand in front of a column which pushes him literally out of the painting, there would be few other clues to inform us of what was happening. Joachim's hurt resentful expression can be as wonderfully subtle as it is because, like in the Giotto, the expression does not have to fill in parts of the story which have been left questionable by the compostion. Giovanni has achieved in generating the same level of emotion as Giotto using tactics which we can attribute to an innovation of Taddeo Gaddi's, allowing the subject to come out in front of the picture plane Giotto's representation of the kiss when Joachim and Anna meet at the gate is one of the most powerful moments I have been exposed to in the painting of this time. The grace and tenderness with which they hold each other (especially with which Anna holds him) could not better express the angel's prophecy to Joachim that Anna will "rejoice at thy seeing". Taddeo's representation is not bad though it looks somewhat like a dance, they both come together almost as a mirror image of each other. Giovanni's meeting needs a closer look. Joachim moves towards Anna. She is still. They both lean in carefully the way an old couple would (not like Taddeo's youthful dance). Anna's robes create a solid, still mass to the floor, whereas Joachim's break up the mass of his body, and are folded over. He has been moving; she has been still. (This is the language of Giotto). His outstretched arms, sharply differentiated, bright yellow and red, grasp her elbow. He looks at her lovingly, excitedly. He wants to tell her the good news he has learned. She, however, does not meet his glance, but looks down in a stern, religious and basically blank expression. Anna's expression is the same as every other woman in these three paintings of Giovanni.

Every woman in Giovanni's nativity scene has tightly pursed lips and an almost stony expression. Except for possibly one woman on a foot stool who reaches for the baby their faces show no more expression than Giovanni's rigid line of women in the temple from which Joachim is rejected. Only the appearance of a baby which should soften even the most devout expression, can crack these stern countenances. Taddeo even though he has drawing problems in rendering facial features shows much more feeling in the only part of his nativity which survives. Both women look adoringly on as one gives the baby her fingers to hold which must be a characteristic of playing with babies which goes back not just six hundred, but two thousand (and surely more) years. It must hurt anyone in our age to see the radically different way in which Giovanni paints his women. Not only because it is painful to think of what the religious ideal for women was at that time, but also because the art viewer has been denied expressions as tender and subtle as we know Giovanni da Milano can produce in his men.

As a final example, I wish to mention a fresco on the opposite wall of the chapel, also by Giovanni, which is not in the Joachim and Anna series but in the Life of Christ, the Raising of Lazarus. Giovanni has chosen to make Lazarus alive, he looks up to Christ in what I am forced to read as resentment. He may just be waking up, but Christ looks apologetic. This interaction is beautiful. Happily I would like to note that the two Marys who look on have loosened their expressions to smiling wonder.

Giovanni da Milano is deeply indebted to his two predecessors but has a beautiful way of expressing the emotion of the male and sometimes female characters in the narratives he paints.
