# Male Feminist Critic (Draft in Need of Fixing)

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof. Holquist  
> Lit 300  
> Section: Leif Dahlberg  
> Final paper  
> Male Feminist Critic  

Being a male, I tread dangerous ground when talking about feminism. Such an act on my part can be seen as condescending by the female sex. Women's situation (singular) is in many ways similar to that faced by minority groups who seek power in a white majority. Males still control this society and there remain many further steps to be taken to shift this power structure to a more equal situation. If a male chooses to talk about feminism, it does not necessarily strengthen the female position. I can be seen simply as a friendly adversary, who feels sympathetic to a weaker neighbor. I have a strong reaction against discussing the categories of sex and gender on such universal levels. It is in the universals that binary oppositions can be so simple as to remain binary. In the universal the individual is abstracted till he or she becomes culture. This abstraction becomes a tradition and when the tradition hampers the individual, is when it should be changed. I realize that there is a problem inherent in the task I set before myself. I need a willingness from the reader to see me as an individual, not as a representative of a larger cultural construct in order for this essay to succeed at all. Toril Moi wrote in her essay "Feminist, Female, Feminine" that:

> Under patriarchy men will always speak from a different position than women, and their political strategies must take this into account. In practice, therefore, the would-be male feminist critic ought to ask himself whether he as a male is really doing feminism a service in our present situation by muscling in on the one cultural and intellectual space women have created for themselves within "his" male dominated discipline.(p.122)

This is a very specific warning. It is aimed at men, like me, who wish to step into the world of discourse about feminism. Moi's warning treats all men as a unified whole who can do nothing but muscle into arguments or territory where they do not belong. She is setting up an adversarial situation. Similar to one used by the white man to get himself where he is. Criticism is "his" (presumably my) male dominated discipline, and feminist criticism is the discipline created by women for themselves to counter this discipline. Moi refuses to allow man the freedom not to subscribe to this patriarchal ideology; she qualifies the whole statement with the immoble opening, "under patriarchy...."

Toril Moi spends much of her essay differentiating between the female (biological), feminine (cultural) and feminist (political). Yet, no differentiation is even implied between male, masculine, and sexist (the male equivalent to radical feminism). These three terms become synonymous. Especially in Moi's essay and for a large part in Hélène Cixous' manifesto, "The Laugh of the Medusa," all men are lumped into the universal structure of domination. Cixous in her charming way does, however, mention that:

> As a woman, I've been clouded over by the great shadow of the scepter and been told: idolize it, that which you cannot brandish. But at the same time, man has been handed that grotesque and scarcely enviable destiny (just imagine) of being reduced to a single idol with clay balls. (p.288) She realizes that often in these feminist discourses, man, because of the retaliatory manner in which these texts are written, is left very little room to be a person. He is reduced to a similar small position which women have had to inhabit. Judith Butler quotes such a women's situation in the first chapter of her book Gender Trouble : ...the universal person and the masculine gender are conflated, thereby defining women in terms of their sex and extolling men as the bearers of a body transcendent universal person hood. (p.9)

So far, all the quotations I have chosen have treated the sexes as opposites. What man is; woman isn't. What man has; woman doesn't. This too simple set of binary oppositions is what Judith Butler tries to overcome. She says:

> The political assumption that there must be a universal basis for feminism, one which must be found in an identity assumed to exist cross-culturally, often accompanies the notion that the oppression of women has some singular form discernible in the universal or hegemonic structure of patriarchy or masculine domination. The notion of a universal patriarchy has been widely criticized in recent years for its failure to account for the workings of gender oppression in the concrete cultural contexts in which it exists. (p.3)

Within both sexes there are those who do not wish to be associated with the narrow conception of gender which our culture has assumed is the "natural" or "normal" consequence of each of two biological possibilities for humans. Judith Butler breaks with the tradition of classical gender constructions. She can do this by making a key distinction between the terms "sex and "gender":

...whatever biological intractability sex appears to have, gender is culturally constructed: hence gender is neither the casual result of sex nor as seemingly fixed as sex...

If gender is the cultural meanings that the sexed body assumes then a gender cannot be said to follow from sex in any one way. ...it does not follow that the construction of "men" will accrue exclusively to the bodies of males or that women will interpret only female bodies.... Further...there is no reason to assume that genders ought also to remain as two. (p.6)

By separating sex and gender and allowing one to be the intractable biological function and the other to be a cultural construct, more gender combinations are allowed than by our restrictive culture.# One can be of the male sex but the female gender, or of the female sex and the male gender. This first rather simple distinction does not really explode the boundaries of cultural constructs. It could seem, at first, to imply that gender is only a mark of sexual orientation--a question of desire--and to only allow two new genders which are known and not completely accepted into culture. Sex:masculine/gender:feminine could simply define a homosexual man. This is especially true in our culture where homosexuals are considered to be effeminate: slang words for a homosexual man include, "fairy" and "girly man". The same is true for sex:female/gender:masculine. Lesbians are called "butch". Butler paraphrases Monique Wittig:

...the overthrow of compulsory heterosexuality will inaugurate a true humanism of "the person" freed from the shackles of sex. This quote underlines a definite conflation of "sex" with a "desire for the opposite sex." This connotation is inherent in our culture's understanding of sex. Butler, however, is not conflating sex or gender with any kind of heterosexual desire. She suggests the creation of many new gender categories which are regardless of desire.

Our culture's vocabulary is not large enough to allow for the creation of multiple new genders, which helps to explain the numerous quotation marks found in these essays (Judith Butler is not the only feminist who is asking for a redefinition of cultural gender constructions. Inherent to a feminist arguement is the fact that the cultural meanings of male and female will have ot be questioned.# The quotation marks seem to call out, "this is the best word we have got, but I do not wish this word to be understood in the common sense." It is difficult to compare quotes from these various thinkers, because in each case words such as sex and gender, male and female and feminist have different meanings even at different times within their own essays. French-Bulgarian linguist and psycoanalyst Julia Kristeva as paraphrased by Toril Moi, flatly refuses to define ‘femininity', "she prefers to see it as a position....it is simply that which is marginalised by the patriarchal symbolic order." Moi concludes by saying that:

> This rational definition is as shifting as the various forms of patriarchy itself, and allows her to argue that men can also be constructed as marginal to the symbolic order as her analyses of male avant-garde artists...have shown"(p.126-7).

Because of Kristeva's redefinition of terms, or a lack of definition rather, men are allowed into the realm of femininity and possibly into a role useful to, or in conjunction with the feminists. She frees the meaning of the word, femininity, in much the way that is required by Judith Butler to define the various new genders within a language system that is not prepared to acccept such variety. One has to recall that Toril Moi who quoted Kristeva is the same critic who warned against men invading women's only domain of criticism. To return to that first quote, Moi used quotation marks in the phrase, "‘his' male dominated discipline," which seems to indicate that she is aware that ‘his' does not stand for all individual men but instead for the recognized cultural construct of men. Thus, her warning is not to individual man but to the larger construct.

Our language is not prepared to handle the deconstruction of the cultural constructions of male and female. I find it amusing to chart the ways our culture has to use a language which is faulty because it is hetero- and phallocentric to describe new gender categories. A friend of mine has called me: "a lesbian in a man's body". This remark underlines the heterocentric connotations of the word--woman. Calling me a "woman in a man's body" would imply that I am homosexual, or would that I want to get a sex change operation. Thus, I must be a "lesbian in a man's body" to insure the proper message that I am not homosexual but rather do not fit the male gender type. Another friend of mine, a homosexual man, has a favorite response to homophobic heterosexual men: "I am more man than you'll ever be and more woman than you'll ever have." This statement redefines his position in respect to what our culture thinks of a homosexual man. He would stereotypically be seen as less than a man--as being effeminate. My friend's comment does not deny his ties to the feminine, and it reaffirms his position as a man. It emphasizes his relation to both genders and creates a kind of super-gender for him atop both of the two classical genders between which he finds himself straddled. It also implies a mobility in the construction of these two classical genders; he can embody both genders and therefore create a new, hyperbolic, Butlerian gender.

Judith Butler's differentiation between sex and gender pushes the limits of our cultural constructions--a fact which is readily evident in how these ideas push the limitations of language, which is not prepared to handle these various genders. Language becomes an anachronism. It is in this chaos of terms that I, of the male sex, feel I can make my way into feminist discourse, using discourse, not my sex or sexual orientation as my path. Butler is not talking about life, it is discourse which interests her. One cannot confuse her use of the signifiers--sex, gender, male, female--with the signifieds--individuals-- they represent. She is talking in the anti-Platonic world of discourse, or if one wants to use terms coined by Lyotard, she is speaking about the hypertexts of culture and discourse. Note how many times discourse is used as a qualifier in her most complete statement on the cultural limitations on the terms she has tried to free of their archaic meanings (or constructions):

> Whether gender or sex is fixed or free is a function of a discourse which, it will be suggested seeks to set certain limits to analysis or to safeguard certain tenets of humanism as presuppositional to any analysis of gender. The locus of intractability, whether in "sex" or "gender" or in the very meaning of "construction," provides a clue to what cultural possibilities can and cannot become mobilized through any further analysis. The limits of the discursive analysis of gender presuppose and preempt the possibilities of imaginable and realizable gender configurations within culture. That is not to say that any and all possibilities are open, but that the boundaries of analysis suggest the limits of a discursively conditioned experience. These limits are always set within the terms of a hegemonic cultural discourse predicated on binary structures that appear as the language of universal rationality. Constraint is thus built into what language constitutes as the imaginable domain of gender.(p.9)

Our language defines the limits on what is defined as gender in our culture. I f a cultural strangle hold on language is released then a greater freedom of gender variety can be communicated and understood. Butler says:

> ...gender does not denote a substantive being, but a relative point of convergence among culturally and historically specific sets of relations. (p.10)

Gender is not an absolute. In her view it can be taken apart and its make up rearranged to arrive at a greater variety of understanding and possibilities than are allowed by its restrictive meanings now. In the same way that Toril Moi said that "under patriarchy" man should be careful not to intrude on the only territory women have staked out for themselves, I, under these new meanings, can say that in many ways I feel like a woman. I find myself very much at odds with the cultural construct of the masculine--the male domination, the hegemonic power structure. I do not want to be associated with what is becoming an ugly stereotype. I have tried to ride the expanded meaning of the feminine gender into a position within discourse where I can make points about these issues without being called an intruder.

Of course there are substantive aspects of belonging to the female sex which a man will never know. A large field has developed of Women's Health Issues, to overcome phallocentricity in the medical profession, which only underlines the point that women's bodies are very different than men's. I am not in a situation to hypothesize how these physical differences are reflected in cultural differences. Butler seems to believe that the fact that all women share a similar type of orgasm or that other universal experiences such as having a child had little to do with the creation of the restrictive gender type which is culturally available to them. Similarly, man's physical experience does not require the restrictive gender type chosen for him. Butler's opening of at least the term gender to a more varied meanign allows individuals a much more personal construction of gender. Butler's essay is riddled with questions, many of which because no one is free of some cultural construction of gender identity, cannot be answered now or possibly ever. But the key to getting closer to these answers is in mobilizing the restrictions of the culturally constructed genders allowing the individuals in our society the freedom to choose between a greater variety of better tailored gender constructions.
