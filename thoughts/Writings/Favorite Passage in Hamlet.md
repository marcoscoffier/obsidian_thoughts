# Favorite Passage in Hamlet

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Rebecca Laroche  
> Shakespeare  
> Feb. 25, 1993  
> Favorite Passage in Hamlet  

Maybe I am an intellectual snob (hopefully not--yet), but when trying to find my favorite passage in the beginning of Hamlet, I had to find one about which I had heard nothing. Every interesting passage seems to have already been discussed. I felt that if I wrote on one of the "BIGGIES" all I could say was something I had heard from a Highschool teacher or read somewhere. The big passages seem to already be highlighted. They set off some "importance alarm" in my brain, which keeps me from rediscovering them for myself: "Not so, my lord. I am too much in the sun;" "O that this too too sullied flesh would melt...;" "...since brevity is the soul of wit...;" "O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I!" And, of course, "To be or not to be...;" etc.. There are plenty more which are so over-quoted they have become cliché. They even pose problems problems for actors. An actor cannot say "To be or not to be..." and have it mean, or have the feeling that he or Shakespeare would want. The quote has taken on a meaning of its own. I do not mean to take away from the power that these incredible passages have, I just believe that the reaction to them has to occur on a personal level, and that my disscusing one of them here is not going to bring out anything more personal for me or my audience. Anyway, enough preface, here is my passage:

Hamlet [being questioned by Rosencrantz on the whereabouts of Polonius's body]: ...to be demanded of a sponge, what replication should be made by the son of a king?

Rosencrantz: Take you me for a sponge, my Lord?

Hamlet: Ay, sir, that soaks up the King's countenance, his rewards, his authorities. But such officers do the King best service in the end. He keeps them, like an ape in the corner of his jaw, first mouthed then swallowed. When he needs what you have gleaned, it is but sqeezing you and, sponge, you shall be dry again.[Act IV, II]

I have never heard "Take you me for a sponge" quoted before, and I have to say that I don't remember this passage at all from previous readings. Unfortunately, I have not completely succeded in my mission of originality because I have to admit, Tom Stoppard has given me the inclination to notice everytime Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are mentioned, and to appreciate the ridiculous quality of these two characters.

This passage does, however, bring up some noticable points on its own: The sponge metaphor is great. When he first mentions the sponge one can't help but think that this is his madness, but (I'm being stupid here) there is method in it. Hamlet's meaning reminds me of a metaphor-less passage spoken by Gower in Henry V about Pistol, Bardolph and Co.:

...Such fellows are perfect in the great commanders' names: and they will learn you by rote where services were done;––at such and such a sconce, at such a breach, at such a convoy; who came off bravely who was shot, who disgraced, what terms the enemy stood on; and this they con perfectly in the phrase of war, which they trick up with new tuned oaths: ...But you must learn to know such slanders of the age or you may be marvelously mistook.[Act III, IV]

Gower describes those who are near the battle but don't partake in it. Everyone knows at least one of this type. There was a guy on my high school football team who could remember everything that had ever happened in any game since middle school but never played a down. I can't even imagine him playing, but I will always think of him when I think back to playing football. These two passages are my no means parallel; both of them simply conjures up the same image of a type--the sponge-- who just absorbs and regurgitates--information, facts, commands, dirty water--without processing any of it.

The Hamlet passage addresses a slightly more complex situation. Hamlet wants his friends to be on his side on the issue of his father. He wants them to understand his situation and support him. I can no more see them doing this than I can see my friend on the football field. It is impossible to imagine them as the auditors of any of Hamlet's soliloquies. Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are as distant from understanding the delicate dilemas of Hamlet's sensitve character as Pistol and Bardolph are from being captured by the power of Henry the V's battlefield rhetoric. Yet, they have become an integral part of this play if not by participation then by association.
