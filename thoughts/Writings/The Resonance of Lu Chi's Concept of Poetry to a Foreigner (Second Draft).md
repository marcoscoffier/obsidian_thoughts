# The Resonance of Lu Chi's Concept of Poetry to a Foreigner (Second Draft)

My freshman year at Yale a good friend and I decided to explore the true potential of Sterling Memorial Library and went into the stacks to find a book on ancient Chinese jokes. Most of the jokes complied in that volume (which I have not found again since) were dirty. Sitting on the floor, roaring in laughter, too tickled to quiet down for the poor library patrons who were unfortunate enough to have less amusing reasons to be in the stacks at that time, a mythic, universal quality of those anecdotes was revealed to me. Laughter is a genuine response to a certain combination of elements; it cannot be faked. Finding out the mechanisms of a joke always kills it, but can often reveal some very complex and interesting inner workings. I discovered a common bond between me and a man long dead from a culture I new very little about in a country I had never been near, let alone in. What had made him laugh centuries ago was making me laugh then.

My subject for this paper is Chinese poetry not their jokes, but my enjoyment of the poetry can be easily related to my laughter at those jokes. For a foreign reader to have a genuine responce to the poetry, it must contain a universal element. Through the inherent inadequacy of any translation and the multiple layers of distortion which time and cultural differences deposit on the freshness of a poet's word, a universal message must be communicated. I have chosen to work with Lu Chi's Wen Fu, a poem in the "fu" form which though it has many poetic images and metaphors is not really a poem but an essay in verse. I am in no position to comment on the nature of the Chinese language, but I have found that the Chinese appreciation of suggestion over exposition, the compactness in the poetry, as well as my difficulty in understanding how the language operates, makes it very difficult for me to extract concrete notions from the poems considered Chinese lyrics themselves. In the Wen Fu, Lu chi descibes the poetic act and through the more expository nature of the Fu form, has given me the most concrete impression of a Chinese ideal of poetry. Lu Chi begins one of the sections of his poem with the phrase, "writing is in itself a joy." With his passion in my sails, I forge into this paper. Later in the same section he describes a power of poetry:

In a sheet of paper is contained the Infinite,

Throughout Lu Chi's essay, the inspiration for writing poetry and the infinite natural world are compared and often become synonymous. In the first section a series of equivalences are set up:

[The poet's] lament for fleeting life is in observance of the four seasons that ever revolve,
His regard for the myriad growing things inspires in him thoughts as profuse.

As with the fallen leaves in autumn's rigor his heart sinks in grief, So is each tender twig in sweet spring a source of joy. in frost he finds sympathy at moments when his heart is all frigid purity, Or far, far, into the highest clouds he makes his minds abode.# Each line has one of these relations: fleeting life = four seasons that revolve; myriad growing things = thoughts as profuse; fallen leaves = heart sinks with grief... which I am told are emphasized in the Chinese. Each phrase or equation compares an emotion of the poet's to its stimulus in nature. The art making process becomes a dialogue between nature and the poet's emotions. Interestingly enough, in the section entitled "the Working Process," Lu Chi says: "... not until the heart attains calm transparency does thought crystallize/Into such words as no man has before fancied of pronounced." The poet's emotions, so excited by nature in the first section, must be calmed for the thoughts to crystallize and the poem be created. I am not sure if this calm corresponds to other images of creativity which Lu Chi conjures:The short phrase "calm transparency" is one of the most resonant I have come across in all the Chinese poems I have read. It is an excellent metaphor for what seems to be a very Chinese conception of art. If the word expresses the poet's "calm transparency," it really expresses the natural world, nature, through the poet who is described as transparent greatly reducing the possibility of his mediation. I have already mentioned the inherent problem with Lu Chi's belief in "calm transparency" the poetic act is mediated by the poet himself but by the very material of his art which is objective. Honor and integrity become the guards of originality. The code of honor and integrity is strong enough to allow China not to have copyright laws.

Later in the poem in the section entitled " The Working Process," nature becomes a metaphor for the poem.

He makes barren twigs put forth luxuriant foliage as they sway, Or by endless waves he traces to the remote fountainhead. Lu Chi is not making words, but the foliage on twigs. He continues a little later:

When the substance of a composition, trunk of a tree, is by truth sustained,
Style aids it to branch into leafy boughs and bear fruit. It is interesting to note that the character for nature is the same as that for sponteneity which is one of themost respected qualities in a poem. "Indeed" he continues in the third section:

...feeling and expression should never fail to correspond, As each emotional change wears a new complexion on a sensitive face. This exact correspondence between the poet's feelings and his expression in poetry forms the basis for other ideologies in Chinese thought and is so pervasive that many Chinese believe that a poem which seems notional to a Westerner, Wandering on Mount T'ien t'ai, could not be made up Sun Ch'o must have actually climbed the mountain.

I was presented with an example of the Chinese belief in the genuine feeling of the poet when a guest lecturer, a young Chinese man got very angry with a comment he quoted to us from Stephen Owen about Tu Fu. Anger like laughter is often a key to the revelation of inner mechanisms. Owen's comment, as I understood it, was that Tu Fu was always in the company of his poetic imagination and thus not really lonely but rather merely secluded. The jist of his statement being that one can never be truly lonely if one has the creative spirit. I realize that our lecturer was so angry because he thought Owen had disclaimed something in Tu Fu's poetry. He had often recieved an element of loneliness. He thought that Owen was saying that Tu Fu was insincere--that he wasn't really lonely. It is a subtle point but an interesting one. Owen, I believe, was not questioning Tu Fu's sincerity but only stressing the vitality of the creative spirit which could actually become company. He emphasised this by redefining Tu Fu's situation from aloneness to seclusion. He did not mean that there were other people out in the woods with Tu Fu. Our guest lecturer was holding fast to a Chinese tradition that the poets directly express his feelings and that if a poem is about lonliness the poet writing it must have been lonely. Questioning a poet's sincerity in our lecturer's mind is equivalent to questioning his poetic ability, which I do not believe Owen was doing. Tu Fu's poetry is about loneliness, but a loneliness full of poetic imagination.

The genuineness of the poetic experience, the being there, which inspires the poem is very important to the Chinese. I am particularly attracted to this point of view because I have felt its powerful inspiration. I paint outside and have spent hours pushing the colors around on the canvas until the sun hits the landscape in the way which speaks to me and I repaint and complete the image in less than an hour before the sun changes too drastically. My visual subject, the landscape, always comes first, its situation inspires my painting. My inability to paint without nature's inspiration could be seen as come kind of weakness, but I rather enjoy being enslaved by the sun and the clouds. I am always in a dialogue with those elements and find that my emotions are often synchronised with the weather. I don't paint from the imagination, I don't like paintings which come from other's imaginations. I need to recognize a moment in a painting which so real so life like that the work becomes a window to that world before the painter which is no longer present. Realism in painting can be compared to this realism in poetry.

I have to refer his ideas to poetic ideals I know, so I wish to contrast his position to a western one held by the German Romantic critic, Theodoro Adorno, in his essay, "On Lyric Poetry and Society"#. These two thinkers are seperated by time and culture, a 20th century German is being compared to a 3rd century Chinese, but their agendas prove to be similar in many ways. The juxtaposition of these two essays sheds new light onto each. And evolved from an inch sized heart, an endless panorama. This boundless metaphor is echoed in Adorno:

> It is commonly said that a perfect lyric poem must possess totality or universality, must provide the whole within the the bounds of the poem and the infinite within the poet's finitude.(p.42)

One sees rather quickly that their conceptions of ideal poetry are quite similar. The poem captures or expresses ( a difference which will become crucial) the infinite, the universal. Lu Chi's concept of the universal comes from nature, wheras Adorno's is grounded in society--in a universality between individuals. This difference needs to be further explained.

Adorno mentions a similar type of transparency:

> ...immersion in what has taken individual form elevates the lyric poem to the status of something universal by making manifest something not distorted, not grasped, not yet subsumed.(p.38)

This is similar to a problem concerning theories of Western Romantic poetry. Language reveals its own objectivity in Romantic poetry. The poet's most personal utterance must use one of the most objective mediums of any art form. I propose that the problems of language with which modern Lyric criticism has to wrestle are the same for any language and do not become significantly greater when the mediation progresses through two languages rather than one. Language has its own inherent problems common to any language. I must qualify this statement by reminding the reader that this paper is going to address the universal moments in poetry not those which refer to the poetic art, or to the artist's culture. The German Romantic literary critic, Theodoro Adorno, writes that:

... language is itself something double. Through its configurations it assimilates itself completely into subjective impulses; one would almost think it had produced them. But at the same time language remains the medium of concepts, remains that which establishes an inescapable relationship to the universal and society....

At least in the early times before the Tang Dynasty, the Chinese do not seem to acknowledge that language is objective. One of their key beliefs, I am told, is that what the poet feels in his heart will automatically find expression in word. I unfortunately cannot read any of these poet's words and thus am especially conscious of the mediative aspect of language from the Chinese poet's thoughts to my own. Adorno however, mentions a duplicity of language which saves our predicament. He recognizes it as objective but then shows how it is language itself which allows the poet to approach something universal. In his making something universal, the poet does not distort. In Lu Chi's essay hte poet is a window from the universal nature, his inspiration comes undistorted from " the myriad living things" he observes. In Adorno the poet is a window to the universal. The perspective of the essay has changed from the poet to the reader. If I make the change from the painter in dialogue with nature, to the reader in dialogue with the universality captured by the poet, i realize that I haven't made a change at all. The mediation of the language becomes blatantly obvious to the foreign reader in translation, but in Adorno's conception it is this objectivity which gives to the poem its universal nature. He says:

> ...the unselfconsciousness of the subject submitting itself to language as to something objective, and the immediacy and spontaneity of that subject's expression are one and the same: thus language mediates lyric poetry and society in their inner most core. (p.43)

My problem in abapting Adorno to our study of Chinese poetry is to stretch the definition of society to include all humans in the East and West. There are very fundamental similarities between the seperate cultures and I don't believe an entire essay should be devoted to these rather simple comparisons but they are at least worth mentioning. Tao Chien is a heroic figure in Chinese poetry because he left Government office to return to his home in nature. Tao Chien chose to follow his personal taste. He was not known till 300 years after his death. Now he is know as one of the best Chinese poets. Hsieh ling-yun his contemporary who was very popular in his time is not so widely recognized.

...even the resistance to social pressure is not something absolutely in individual; The artistic forces in that resistance, which operate in and through the individual and his spontaneity, are objective forces ...forces, that is, that are part of the constitution of the whole and not at all merely forces of a rigid individuality blindly opposing society.(p.43)

The lyric work hopes to attain universality through unrestrained individuation.(p.38)

The universality of the lyrics substance, however is social in nature. Only one who hears the voice of humankind in the poem's solitude can understand what the poem is saying....(p.38)
