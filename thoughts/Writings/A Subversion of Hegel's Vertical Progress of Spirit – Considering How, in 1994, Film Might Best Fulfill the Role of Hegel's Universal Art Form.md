# A Subversion of Hegel's Vertical Progress of Spirit: Considering How, in 1994, Film Might Best Fulfill the Role of Hegel's Universal Art Form

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof. Holquist  
> Lit 300 sect.--Dahlberg  
> March 21, 1994  
> A Subversion of Hegel's Vertical Progress of Spirit: Considering, How  
> in 1994, Film Might Best Fulfill the Role of Hegel's Universal Art Form  

Hegel argues that certain forms of art are important at a certain time because they encapsulate a particular stage in the History of Mind/Spirit's (geist's) progress toward greater self consciousness. The vertical progress of geist from art to religion to philosophy/truth is not chronological and thus its progression through stages does not necessarily mean that they cancel each other out. Philosophy (the highest stage) existed in ancient Greece just as art (the lowest) is still around today. Hegel seems to be aware of this and has allowed for a horizontal movement of geist-- meaning that many manifestations of it are present at every stage. It is possible to question the idea that geist can actually progress to the spiritual because man can never truly leave the carnal. Human beings are trapped in this dialectic.

Hegel's philosophy operates on a system of dialectics which is superseded or reconciled by a third term. The difference between superseding and reconciling is an important one. Hegel proposes that poetry is the highest art form opening the vertical progression of the spirit towards religion. But he is very hard to corner, and he leaves a great deal of ambiguity as to whether reconciling the dialectic in fact means moving beyond it. My choice for the art form which encapsulates the stage of geist in 1994 is film: an art form which combines the dialectic of art and music and that art which Hegel has placed between and above them, poetry. Film is the ultimate reconciliation of the three sister arts--art, music and poetry, creating a narrative out of each one and allowing them to coexist in a way which locks each into a give and take relation with the others. Film makes narrative out of picture strengthening its position, and gives meaning to music beyond the abstract sensuality of the notes without having to rely upon poetry. Film, I believe, can unseat poetry as the art form which can best duplicate man to himself today. It better fulfills the advantages Hegel has ascribed to poetry over its sister arts. Even though aesthetics plays a relatively small part in Hegel's system, placing film, a combination of the three romantic arts, above any of the three individually has repercussions on the whole. To say that the text which is most representative of our greater self-consciousness of 1994, is not merely poetic, but couples sense perceptions to language, requires dethroning poetry as the queen of the arts and thus at least a partial reversion of vertical progression in Hegel's system. In the area of aesthetics, Hegel has placed poetry above the other art forms. Of all the art forms known to Hegel, poetry is the most spiritual because its material is language which is less sensuous than paint, or marble or sounds. It is precisely in the imprecision of language, its inability to define a concept of the mind, that I wish to find fault in Hegel's system. Hegel's system is defined in language; he concludes that man's primary, and most complete expression of geist is through language; and yet he does not fully study language as the material with which poetry constructs its art. Of course I am using language to battle and find the weaknesses in language, and thus have set up what could be looked at as an impossible challenge. I propose that if Hegel were writing in 1994, he would be more aware of the weaknesses of language, the material of which poetry is made up, and that he would not pick a text which relies upon a single material but rather one, such as film, which draws from many, and combines the strengths of different art forms, for a more complete expression of geist. For Hegel, the ultimate expression of geist is in language, or philosophy. He puts spirituality in a dialectic with sensuality, and chooses language as the best means of expressing geist because its material, words, are less sensuous than painting's color or music's sounds. If there is a weakness in Hegel's thought this is where it lies. Romantic Aesthetics really needs its own vocabulary. This is especially true of Hegel. It is particularly hard to read these texts in translation because their figures are webbed to their language. Some German romantic philosophers even had very strong prejudices about the German language, and believed that anything not in German was not philosophy. Let us now look at some of Hegel's text where he compares the three arts:

Poetry the art of speech, is the third term, the totality, which unites in itself, within the province of the spiritual inner life and on a higher level, the two extremes, i.e the visual arts and music. For on the one hand poetry like music, contains that principle of the self-apprehension of the inner life as inner, which architecture, sculpture and painting lack; while, on the other hand, in the field of inner ideas, intuitions and feelings it broadens out into an objective world which does not altogether lose the determinate character of sculpture and painting. Finally, poetry is more capable than any other art of completely unfolding the totality of an event, a successive series of the changes of the heart's movements and passions, representations, and the complete course of an action.

This quote makes it evident that Hegel views poetry as the mediation between the other arts and that he places it on a higher level. Film of course creates a narrative out of the other two art forms. It gives the ability to music and painting to unfold the totality of an event--take wordless silent film as an example. Before going on to debate the relation of poetry to the other arts, I would like to continue discussing the problem inherent in Hegel's triangular reasoning. He says: it becomes the task of philosophy to supersede the
oppositions...Philosophy affords a reflective insight into the essence of the opposition only in so far as it shows how truth is just the dissolving of opposition and, at that, not in the sense, as may be supposed, that the opposition and its two sides do not exist at all, but that they exist reconciled.(p.379)

Here is stated the problem: Does the reconciliation does actually supersede the two sides? Truth, says Hegel, exists in the reconciled sides. Therefore it follows that their existence is important to the existence of truth. Here, of course, Hegel is discussing philosophy's reconciliation of art and religion, the sensuous vs. the spiritual, but the same reasoning can apply to poetry's reconciliation of painting and music. He makes this statement specifically about art: ..art's vocation is to unveil the truth in the form of sensuous artistic configuration, to set forth the reconciled opposition just mentioned, and so to have its end and aim in itself, in this very setting forth and unveiling.(p.379)

Art, being mired in the sensuous, need not search to progress to the end of moral betterment. "For other ends, " he goes on to say, "like instruction, purification, bettering, financial gain, struggling for fame and honor, have nothing to do with the work of art as such, and do not determine its nature." A statement which gives many artists today something to think about. At any rate, Hegel has freed art from the progression towards moral betterment: It is one of the oppositions which was reconciled by philosophy and he urges it to go on concerned with its own agenda. Art need not concern itself with the vertical progression of spirit. Strengthening a horizontal progression Hegel allows it to proceed independently. I challenge the necessity or possibility of a vertical progression at all in the realm of aesthetics. I cannot, however, properly account for the repercussions this challenge would have on the rest of his holistic vision, but I do see a weakness in the realm of aesthetics. Even after he frees art from vertical progression, he persists in arguing the dominance of poetry over the other arts: We began therefore with architecture, which only strove after the complete representation of spiritual material in a sensuous element, so that art achieved a genuine fusion of form and content only in sculpture: with painting and music, on account of the inwardness and subjectivity of their content, art began to dissolve again the accomplished unification of conception and execution in the field of sense. This latter character poetry displays most strikingly because in its artistic materialization it is essentially to be interpreted as a withdrawal from the real world and as a subordination of that world...(p.394)

He insists on setting up a hierarchy or progression in his system. I am not sure there is a hierarchy at all. Poetry may be the reconciliation of some dialectical differences between painting and music, but this does not necessarily set it above its sisters. To represent poetry as superior, Hegel has glossed over imperfection in its material--language. Surprisingly he seems to lead us to these weaknesses without realizing the importance of his own comments.

Architecture cannot so subordinate the sensuous material to the spiritual content as to be able to form that material into an adequate shape of the spirit; poetry, on the other hand, goes so far in its negative treatment of its sensuous material that it reduces the opposite of heavy spatial matter, namely sound, to a meaningless sign instead of making it, as architecture makes its material, into a meaningful symbol. ...The beautiful mean between these extremes of architecture and poetry is occupied by sculpture, painting and music, because each of these arts works the spiritual content entirely into a natural medium and makes it intelligible alike to sense and spirit. (p.395)

In the very text where Hegel claims poetry's supremacy over the other art-forms, he leads us to poetry's own downfall without quite fleshing out the details. Poetry's material has become a meaningless sign. This separation of the sign form what is communicated is language's ultimate weakness. One cannot be sure of exactly what one is communicating because meaning and the sign are not one and the same thing. Meaning can be lost in the gap between what is meant to be expressed, the sign that is used to express this, and what is understood of that sign. Hegel has previously elaborated the translation of sound to meaning: With music, art abandons the immersion of the spirit in a tangible, visible and directly present shape, in poetry it gives up the opposite element of sound and hearing, at least in so far as this sound is no longer formed into an adequate external object and the sole expression of the subject matter. Therefore the inner life does express itself [in music] but will not find its actual existence in the sensibility(even if more ideal) of the notes, because it seeks this existence solely in itself...the spirit withdraws its content from sounds as such and is manifested by words which do not entirely forsake the element of sound but sink to being a merely external sign of what is being communicated. The musical note being just replete with spiritual ideas becomes the sound of a word, and the word, instead of becoming an end in itself, becomes a self-unindependent means of spiritual expression.(p.390) "Self-unindependent", as awful a word as it is, must mean that spiritual expression in words is dependent upon the system of language. This is a weakness. Hegel is relegating the expression of spirit to words without accounting for what must inevitably be left out as spirit is translated into language. At the very end of the assigned selection, Hegel describes poetry's double duties, each of which is a concession away from the pure expression of spirit:

> (i)Poetry must so organize its inner images that they can be completely adapted to communication in language (ii)it must not leave its linguistic medium in the state in which it is used every day, but it must treat it poetically in order to distinguish it from expressions in prose by the choice placing of words. (p.396)

If man must organize his inner images in order to express them in language, there must be something which he is incapable of bringing out of himself and placing before himself using language. Further, treating language poetically is a sensuous use of that medium. Hegel has placed the realm of the sensuous at odds with that of the spiritual, which is precisely one of the arguments he made for placing poetry above the other two art forms. Poetry is the least sensuous of the triumvirate of the Romantic arts, and now he requires poetry to treat language sensuously. If Hegel can backtrack at the end of his argument to say that language must be treated sensuously, I can go one step further and say that geist cannot be expressed solely in language but needs help from the other more sensuous art forms. I want to bolster this argument by looking at other instances where Hegel brushes near the idea that the material of language may have its faults. He says: … [poetry] must keep steadily in mind that its results are to be made known to the spirit only by communication in language.(p.393) The "only" in this sentence is the affirmation of a limit of poetry's and language's powers of communication. "Only" indicates an exclusion: There are aspects of spirit which cannot be described by language. He also makes statements like:

> The subject matter of the art of speech is the entire world of ideas developed with a wealth of imagination, i.e. the spirit abiding by itself in its own spiritual element and when it moves out to the creation of something external, using only that as a sign, itself different from the subject matter.(p.390)

He cannot be insisting that all ideas are formed as speech. We are beginning to uncover a discrepancy in his thought which is central to the problem of art, especially as he has defined art. Does spirit exist in the inner thoughts or in the creating of something external which communicates it? There is a huge discrepancy between what one can feel or experience or think and what one can express. By narrowing spirit's expression to words, he is greatly limiting that of which spirit can consist. Here is a problem which Hegel often hints at but never rectifies. If imagination must be expressed in words and even thinks in words, its realm must necessarily be curbed.

I am at a disadvantage in trying to clear up this argument because I am reading a translation of Hegel's text. I am not sure of the subtle ways Hegel might be differentiating between imagination and spirit. But this disadvantage is only stronger proof of the point I am trying to make. If I am only positive of what he is trying to say when I understand every subtlety of the definition of the terms he is using, I can never really be sure of anything he says because I can never be sure of every subtlety(nor for that matter can you ever be sure to understand what I am trying to say).

One has only to try to write in another language to realize the imprecision of language as a material for expression. One is never sure of exactly what one is saying in a particular phrase, because one is uncertain about the subtleties of this foreign language. There is no reason why one should be more certain about the subtleties of one's own language. How do I know that the material I am using to communicate an idea will be understandable to any reader when I have no way of knowing that reader's particularities and prejudices? And yet Hegel goes on to say:

Now poetry cuts itself free from this importance of the material, in the general sense that the specific character of its mode of sensuous expression affords no reason any longer for restriction to a specific subject matter and a confined sphere of treatment and presentation. It is therefore not linked exclusively to any specific form of art; on the contrary, it is the universal art which can shape in any way and express any content capable at all of entering the imagination, because its proper material is the imagination itself, that universal foundation of all the particular art-forms and the individual arts.(p.393) How can poetry express any content of the imagination if we are not sure that the imagination functions in language. He has already stated that "the inner life expresses itself in music(see p.4)" so he has acknowledged an aspect of the inner life which operates without the use of language. By his own arguments, poetry cannot be the universal art because it can't express all that is inside--spirit or imagination. Early in the essay he describes the function of art in general:

> The universal and absolute need from which art (on its formal side) springs has its origin in the fact that man is a thinking consciousness, i.e. that man draws out of himself and puts before himself what he is and whatever else is. Things in nature are only immediate and single, while man as spirit duplicates himself, in that (i) he is as things in nature are, but (ii) he is just as much for himself; he intuits himself, represents himself, thinks and only on the strength of this active placing of himself before himself is he spirit.(p.360)

If man is spirit when he duplicates himself and nature, one would assume that he is most spirit when he best duplicates what is inside himself an what he experiences from nature. This statement is very much at odds with one which succeeds it in Hegel's thought, that spirit is best expressed in language. How can one say that man best duplicates himself and nature in the single abstract material of language which he has said communicates using meaningless signs? Hegel describes the role of the universal art form (which he has relegated to poetry): ...the artist acquires his subject matter in himself and is the human spirit actually self determining and considering, meditating, and expressing the infinity of its feelings and situations: nothing that can be living in the human breast is alien to that spirit any more. ...art does not need any longer to represent only what is absolutely at home at one of its specific stages, but everything in which man is capable of being at home.(p.384)

It would seem to me that one would need every possible mode of artistic communication to get the full expression he demands of a universal art form. Film comes closer to performing such a function than poetry because it combines three major art forms. Hegel has mentioned a weakness of art: ...art is restricted in its means of portrayal and can only produce one-sided deceptions, for example a pure appearance of reality for one sense only, and, in fact, if it abides by the formal aim of mere imitation, it provides not the reality of life but only a pretense of life.(p.369)

Film cannot claim to create more than a pretense of life, but it can definitely produce more than a one-sided deception.

It is poetry's and philosophy's fundament in language, or words, just like painting's foundation in visual images, or color, and music's in songs, or notes, which is their main weakness. Film has the ability to combine three types of narratives: one made up of words, one of musical sounds, and (a new development of this century) one of images, and thus can give a more complete expression of human spirit, because each of the art forms of which it is comprised can strengthen the gaps in its neighbor's expression.

Many of Hegel's statements about the strength of poetry as the universal art form seem to me to be even stronger when film is placed as the subject of his remarks. He overestimates the power of language, and underestimates the power of the other art forms. I do not find that language can ever completely describe what the speaker or writer is trying to say--nor can another of Hegel's art forms on its own. Language is no way a complete artistic expression, nor can it cover all the subtleties of the other forms of artistic expression. Film is the ultimate reconciler of these three art forms. It places them in an environment where they have the potential to be equally strong.
