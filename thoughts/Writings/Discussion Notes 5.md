# Discussion Notes #5

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Stephen Anderson  
> Language and Mind  
> Discussion Notes #5  

The study of phonology seems to be taking us away from a study of the mind. Phonology is routed in the physical nature of the vocal apparatus. Pinker however says that one of the major discoveries in modern linguistics is that:

a morpheme may be stored in the mental dictionary in a different form from the one that is ultimately pronounced.(p.178.) Because phonetics takes such careful attention to the physical origins of the sounds which are produced, I am inclined to believe that the nature of these discrepancies is physical and not mental. He also says that phonological rules are not a "mere summary of articulatory laziness." Changes in languages seem to happen constantly. I've gone to France every summer of my life, and every time I go there are some new expressions which I must learn. I find this very exciting, that things can't just stay the same. But, I question whether these changes can tell us anything about the minds of the changers. If one can't prove a rule derived from the physical basis of language production such as the "articulatory laziness", how could one ever prove one about a mental process governing these language changes. Also, since many of these rules refer to the sound environment, the sounds of a particular language. The group of language users makes the changes--no individual user. Language change has not happened until an entire group has adopted the change, otherwise almost any change is possible. I wonder what these group decisions which are certainly more random than the top forty charts can tell the linguist about the structure of the mind.
