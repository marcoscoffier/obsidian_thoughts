# Take Home Final — Lakoff's Categories

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Stephen Anderson  
> Language and Mind  
> May 8, 1995  
> Take Home Final  

In his book, Women, Fire and Dangerous Things George Lakoff sets out to redefine a two thousand year old idea of what categories are. In the preface he says, " this book is about the traditional answers to [questions such as: What is reason? How do we make sense of our experience? What is a conceptual system and how is it organized?...] and about new research that suggests new answers." (p.xi) The traditional view he says, ‘ is a philosophical one. It has come out of two thousand years of philosophizing about the nature of reason. It is still widely believed despite overwhelming empirical evidence against it." (p.xii) He spends most of the first two thirds of Book I describing these traditional ideas and what is wrong with them. Because of this organization this book takes a very long time to evolve. Lakoff goes through most of recent history's ideas about categorization. He meticulously picks apart the Objectivist Paradigm as he calls it. These sections certainly present an impressive synthesis of thinking about categories. But, the reader can feel Lakoff is aiming beyond most of what he brings up for discussion in the first two thirds of Book I. He is bringing it up, merely to shoot it down to show why it is wrong. It is not until a chapter entitled "A New Realism" that he starts talking about what the new ideas are. He has just completed a chapter on Putnam's theorem which he concludes, "What he is saying is that we cannot have a privileged correct description from an externalist perspective." (p.261) He continues:

> The problem is the externalist perspective--the God's eye view. We are not outside of reality. We are part of it, in it. What is needed is not an externalist perspective, but an internalist perspective. It is a perspective that acknowledges that we are organisms functioning as part of reality and that it is impossible for us ever to stand outside it.... What is possible is knowledge of another kind: knowledge from a particular point of view, knowledge which includes the awareness that it is from a particular point of view, and knowledge which grants that other points can be legitimate. (p.261)

He seems to be making the same break with traditional theory that the phenomenologists made with analytical philosophy. Starting with Husserl at the end of the 19th century, the phenomenologists sought to regain for philosophical investigations a sense of reality which began with one's being in the world. Maurice Merleau-Ponty the latest of the founders of phenomenology says in the preface to his major work, Phénoménologie de la perception,:

> Le monde n'est pas un objet dont je possède pars devers moi la loi de la constitution, il est le milieu naturel et le champ de toutes mes pensées et de toutes mes perceptions explicites. La vérité n' «habite» pas seulement l' «homme intérieur» ou plutôt il n'y a pas d'homme intérieur, l'homme est au monde, c'est dans le monde qu'il se connaît. #

Lakoff soon thereafter does make a break with the views of the phenomenologist. He says:

> The experientialist approach is... to attempt to characterize meaning in terms of the nature and experience of the organisms doing the thinking,. "Experience" is thus not taken in the narrow sense of the things that have "happened to happen" to a single individual. Experience is instead construed in the broad sense: the totality of human experience and everything that plays a role in it--the nature of our bodies, genetically inherited capacities, our modes of physical functioning in the world, our social organization, etc. (p.266)

The twentieth century philosopher, or existentialist we could say in the case of Merleau-Ponty, is interested solely in the individual, his quest is not a science. Lakoff's discussion is in the field of cognitive science. He may make a similar move as the phenomenologists, but he reclaims it for science, which is painfully attacked by the phenomenologists. The ‘science' that is attacked by the phenemenologists is the same as that to which Lakoff is directing his arguments. He ends by reclaiming relativism for scientific thought.

I view relativism of the sort that exists as a good thing. Just as the gene pool of a species needs to be kept diverse if the species is to survive under a wide variety of conditions, so I believe that diverse ways of experience are necessary to our survival as a species. I believe that vanishing cultures and languages need to be protected just as vanishing species do. And, like Whorf, I think that we have a lot to learn from other ways of conceptualizing experience that have evolved around the world. (p.337)

I came to this book with a question in mind. In writing my senior essay on twentieth century conceptions of space in France. I ran across the observation by several authors that in French the word "sens" means both direction and meaning. There are many such homonyms in language, but somehow this one seemed to stand out. These where two concepts that were crucial to my essay and in French they were both the same word. What could this tell me about the French? What could other such important paired concepts, the same sound, the same word even but different concepts tell one about human thought, about our conception of the world? Lakoff gets close to answering my question when he speaks of polysemy: The issue of conceptual organization is intimately linked to the issue of polysemy. Polysemy occurs when a single word has more than one meaning--and when those meanings are systematically related. Systematic relationship is crucial here. The two meanings of bank--place where you put your money and the edge of a river are called homonyms. Cases of polysemy are cases like warm which refers both to the temperature and to clothing that makes you feel warm.....

A case of polysemy in Mixtec would be cii, which can mean ‘belly' or ‘under' (p.316)

Lakoff has already described how Mixtec conceives of spatiality by projecting the body on the world. It does not use pronouns like Indo-European languages, but for on top of the mountain would use a construction like ‘mountain-head'. I am afraid that my original French ‘sens', at best, lies between polysemy and being a homonym. Though I am not yet willing to declare it merely a homonym, it is certainly not as systematically related as the Mixtec words. Even if one were to accept ‘sens' as polysemous, this would not tell us very much about how the French, or we humans think.

Lakoff, surprisingly to me, defends Whorf whom I have heard chastised in not a few courses. He states the Kay-Kempton Experiment which documented differences in how English speakers and speakers of Tarahumara perceive of colors and defends Whorf's hypothesis. The fact that English speakers do have two words one for blue and one for green and that in Tarahumara these two colors share the same word, does affects how the speakers of these languages performed in this specific test. The English speakers where more willing to categorize a color as being either blue or green than the speakers of Tarahumara. But this discovery is rather limited Lakoff states and I have to agree, that it would take many more tests and a lot more convincing to prove that language affects how a speaker perceives of the world. I found a section of chapter 18 "The Commensurability Issue" to be particularly interesting. Lakoff lists ways in which thinkers have sought to categorise languages as commensurable. His list includes: translation, understanding, use, framing and organization. Translation becomes a moot comparison, because any language is translatable at least by the standards held by the objectivist philosophers that translation merely retain truth conditions. But the other four conditions seem to be interesting ways of classifying languages. With them one can categorize the slight marvel I had on the realization that the French word ‘sens' means both direction and meaning. There must be millions of such correspondences in the languages of the world, each one preserving its own idiosycracies.# French simply frames the concepts of direction and meaning differently than English. German frames heart, soul and spirit differently containing all those meanings in the word "Geist".
