# Discussion Notes #4

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Stephen Anderson  
> Language and Mind  
> Feb 7, 1995 Discussion notes #4  

In Pinker's chapter "Words, Words, Words" he mentions how someone must learn a new word every ninety minutes since the time they were one in order to attain the literate adult's more than 60,000 listemes. Why then is it so impossible for me to learn certain words? Why have I looked ontological up in the dictionary at least a dozen times, and would still be wary of telling you what it meant. I would hazard a guess in response to this question: that the meanings of words like ontological or phenomenological are very complicated. They attempt to label an entire system of thought and are equivalent to understanding a word like Kantian or Platonism. How can one describe Platonism without going into a whole spiel about forms? The language of literature speak is a whole world of its own. Is it really a language in the sense that everyone initiated to it can understand what is mean by Wordsworthian? Can that poet's entire life's work be contained in a word? Or does one have to understand that when Harold Bloom uses the word it stands for his own understanding of Wordsworth? Can a single word represent Harold Bloom's understanding of Wordsworth?

I found the fact that a deaf child will disassociate the pointing finger from the sign for ‘you' or ‘me' and confuse them like the speaking child confuses the words to be the strongest proof against onomatopoeia that I have ever heard. If the deaf child can remove the significance of pointing to his chest to talk of himself, no sign can refer directly visually or auditorily to something in the real world. One is told of the ‘arbitrariness of the sign' in language learning, but proof as blatant as this is still quite startling. Chinese characters then were never picture writing. One has to wonder how much was being said in heirglyphics. How far could those images have departed from

representation.
