# Thoughts on the NEA

> [!note] About this file
> Proposals for the National Endowment for the Arts' Visual Arts Program (better PR, a catalogue of grantees, a travelling grantee show). Undated.

There are a few things which I have been mulling over:

!) The Visual Arts Program and the NEA need better PR. Why is there no E.L. Doctorow of the visual arts to wow congress into giving more money? There is no reason why the art world could not create superstars like the other disciplines. The NEA is well positioned to give the public access to the future stars of the art world. PR equals education and thus fits very much in line with the Endowment's mission to bring art and awareness of the arts to the public.

There are two options I see as crucial right now because PR is crucial. I would love to help makin either of these suggestions an actuality
1) Organize a catalog of the grantees: bios and their work--I can't believe that one does not exist. The artists and work chosen by the NEA should be public knowledge, and could be nationally known. Plus such a cataloge, from such a prominent yet underknow organization could spark alot of inerest in the arts. The Endowment has made some amazing things possible. People should know about these things.

2) Advertise-- I have a very patchy idea of who the past grantees have been or what their work was like. I am sure most American's have an even murkier idea than me. I feel that the NEA has been afraid to go public because they sense a public disapproval of contemporary art, and that perhaps going public would result in its only being able to fund Federally o.k. art which is contrary to the mission that the artists who make up the panels want. They want emerging artists in need to get help, perhaps artists who have been unable to sell because their work is in development or does not fit a pre-existing sellable category. Yet, the panels also seem to be keenly aware that they want their grantees to represent a cross-section of the American people, the flower painters, the ethnic minorities, the realists the abstract painters, just not the "obscene". The panels and the council painstakingly pick the best of each year and no one outside a small circle has any idea who and what was chosen. The Americans should be able to see these carefully chosen cross sections of our society.

The secrecy has run the NEA into its worst nightmare. By concentrating on getting money to artists, rather than inform the public of its incredible track record, and tooting its own horn, the NEA has lef itself absolutly vulnerable to those who would have disapproved no matter what the NEA funded-- those who have hunted out the most inflamatory works as representive of the whole oragnization. No one knows enough to counter this image. The works are only inflammatory because the public doesn't know enough to begin with. Minding your business has not worked: the NEA has to scream its own praises, or it may die.

.

There is a way to keep the NEA peer panel in tact even if Congress denies artists individual grants. Give awards, but no money. Rather than money, the NEA could give the artist recognition of the highest sort. It could organize and advertise an exhibition, a group show of all the grantees, which might start in Washington and travel to a changing set of museums . Large, public openning with Congress and the President, seeing the bright new artists. If the grantee exhibitions were adveritsed properly then they could bring noteriety to museums who had won the grants to recieve the travelling show.

I propose a plan which I think should be implemented even if grants to individuals are saved:

--keep the NEA Visual Arts Program Peer panel in tact. A group of nationally renowned artists, critics, and people in the arts would be getting together to pick individual artists for recognition. --They would give the artists notoreity, a group show, and a catalog through which their work can become nationally known.

--The NEA would get recognition in the public eye. Increased public awareness of what they do and who gets recognized.

--This increased recognition and awareness would foster a national debate about the arts. Perhaps with more notoriety the panels would be less likely to pick an artist whose work, would offend the taxpayers who fund them. The controvesial work may be a tiny portion of the work the NEA funds, but it is the work which is know to the public. The NEA has to let taxpayers know of the non-controversial work which is funded and all the wonderful programs which never produce controversial work. This discussion is leading directly into the issue of pornography which the NEA and the Visual Arts Department has to address. It is causing all the problems.

There can be a difference between privately and publicly funded art. The taxpayer has a right to not want his money to go towards funding what he thinks is pornography. The grantee has a responcibility to the public which has funded a part of his artistic development. You have the right in this country to say whatever you want, but do you have the right to use taxpayer's money to say it? The European countries who give so much more money to the arts also have many more restrictions on their grants.

There will always be people in the country who dissagree on what should and should not be funded, what is essential is that discussion be possible. I don't think anyone working in NEA realises how little the public really knows about what it does. I have always been an art lover, arts event goer, and an art and literature major, and I had no idea of the power and scope of the NEA's programs, nor do I know what or who has been funded. The glossy images of the Whitney Biennial stick in the public's mind, this is a curated show which at its conception was to take the pulse of the artworld every two years. The NEA better situated to make a similar statement and not merely a theoretical one. Of course everyone would realize that the NEA's selection is a public selection and falls within certain restrictions (just like the Whitney's curated show does).
