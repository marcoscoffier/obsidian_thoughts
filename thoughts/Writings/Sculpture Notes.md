# Sculpture Notes

> [!note] About this file
> Starts as notes toward a sculpture (a sunlit morning in the bathroom) and turns into the mountain-and-Cézanne passage that reappears in the senior essay.

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> September 3rd 1994  

It was a beautiful day in the bathroom this morning. The sun takes on a certain clarity in a white room with alot of porcelain and chrome-- the teal blue shower curtains refracted their color on much of the white and the sun fell in crisp slices on the tiled floor and was diluted in the shower spray.

-- "there is no way of my conveying the happiness I feel under this warm water knowing a bright day awaits me.

--I could describe it in a poem but that would be far from the experience: someone else would read it in an armchair on a rainy day. Actually placing someone in the shower does not guarantee that they will find the experience that makes them happy.

Of course this moment would be gone now, had I not written it down. I don't have the experience now that I had then, but I recall it. At best it is a memory now reawakened by reading my record of it.

If I climb a mountain, my view of it is ever changing, sometimes just a path and the next highest point looming before me, then a glimpse of the summit, or the highest part I can see from where ever I find myself. When I arrive at the top of the mountain, my view of it radically changes. If I flew around the mountain it would again look quite different. From the plane I couldn't see the human sized events along the path which were such an essential part of my concept of the mountain when I climbed it. The airplane rips one from seeing the world habitually. The jump to seeing the world from the sky is easily as great as the visual understanding which developed perspective painting or which the cubists tried to make by painting many sides of an object at once. The move is probably closer to that of the cubists. Because understanding of a cubist image requires a conceptual understanding (which is natural in many cultures) that the image isn't being looked at from one vantage point. The viewer has to make a conceptual leap from the fractured two dimensional image which he can look at from only one position in space to a three dimensional object which the painter is circling around. Upon completing one of these trips to the top or around the mountain, I have appropriated in a similar way that the painter appropriates a scene in front of him by moving his brush through it. It is the continuity of the journey which is crucial to appropriating the mountain in both the painter's and the hiker's endeavors.# My view is different from that of someone who has seen snap-shots of my trip. A string of photographs could never recreate my experience of the mountain. First of all one can never photograph "steepness" or other similar effects. If you stand at the top of a cliff and are overtaken by the height, decide to take a picture, the photograph can never relay that feeling. Steepness or height is an experience. It has everything to do with your sense of gravity and position related to the view which delivers the experience. Also, a non-sequential series of experiences (such as if you blind folded a viewer only to remove the mask at certain points, and flew him or drove him to different spots) creates no concept of this real space. The only difference in Cézanne's and my conception of the mountain is that I have moved my body around the mountain itself, where-as he moved his brush through his visual field which contained it. Merleau-Ponty even says that:

> Dans la perception primordiale, ces distinctions du toucher et de la vue sont inconnues. C'est la science du corps humain qui nous apprend ensuite à distinguer nos sens. La chose vécue n'est pas retrouvée ou construite à partir des données des sens mais s'offre d'emblée comme le centre d'où elles rayonnent.#

Cézanne and I have undergone almost the same process. Each of our actions creates a continuum of experience which defines what we can appropriate by them.
