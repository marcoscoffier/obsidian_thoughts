# Kazimir Malevich's New Realism (Draft)

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Katerina Clark  
> Lit. 410: The Avant Garde  
> Yale University  
> Nov. 4, 1994  
> Kazimir Malevich's New Realism  

I know it is not a good idea to start an essay for a class by saying that the person whom I am going to discuss makes me angry, but an avant garde artist as subject matter poses a special problem. Retaliatory anger is inherent to the reception of their works. Much of the avant garde (especially the French) expected and even sought this reaction to their work. Anger marks an individual as being separate from these avant-garde artists and is part of the process of "épatage"-- forcing understanding by shocking--often they are not even concerned with there being any understanding. The anger is a sign post for the viewer--something precious to him has been insulted and violated. When somebody throws a punch at something you love, threatening to ruin it, you get angry. The angry viewer gains the most from the avant-garde when he picks apart his anger to see what caused this uncontrollable and thus genuine emotion and through a complicated series of steps reconstructs a more specific idea of what it was he loved about art in the first place. The "that's not art!" reaction of the conservative causes the question, "what then is art?" The avant-garde artist becomes a tool for the viewer's greater understanding of himself.

It is almost a ridiculous situation to be studying these artists ( a term which they are very conscious of having redefined) in a class at Yale University, in the heart of the academy, the precise place which the avant garde roared against. But this academy is certainly not the same one which was around at the turn of the century when those artists were being most infuriating. Like an adolescent, the avant garde artist makes blanket statements to spite anyone who cares the slightest bit about art. The academic must stand in a parental position with respect to this artist. He cannot become one of his cronies, and must study him at a distance to glean what he can from him. What the avant garde artist, in many cases, fails to realize is that the forces that uphold social conventions like a Humanities department, or a tendency for a certain type of realism in art, are identical to those which they are trying to invoke to destroy those same structures. At their very best, those structures which are often reunited under the term the academy (because the academy sets the standards of taste which they all espouse) honor precisely what the avant garde honors at its best. The academy loves the life and change the avant-garde artists flaunt and slowly incorporates them into the core of their disciplines, at the same rate that a parent releases responsibility to a child. The avant-garde artist, like the child, fails to realize how much he owes the parental figure and screams half-baked insults against it. He also litters his works with infuriating bits of drivel to avoid being immediately incorporated into the system which he is so avidly fighting against. The "academy" and the museums, however, are the only organizations in the world which glorify pure thought. When the avant garde artist takes an adolescent swing at the "academy", the cruelest retaliation the academy could undertake would be simply not to notice. The lightweight, however is aiming his blows at the heart of the academy, and making it look like a fool--it usually reacts. No other structure in the world cares to support or even note such shenanigans. Thus the academy is necessary for the avant garde artist to exist. If it weren't for the academy, no one would care about any of their acts, which are for the most part, acts of retaliation. Without something to rile against, there would be no avant garde.

My anger towards Malevich is two fold. He makes me mad for glossing over the power and life which is present and alive if we care to look fot it in the of the past; and at the same time he espouses techiniques which are the down fall of any academy: he seeks to find a set of rules by which to make art. Art cannot be made purely from any kind of technical agenda It is of interest, however, to look at one moment when he contradicts himself and over generalizes the past. The statements he makes about the nature of art in general, what it should be are quite good though, I would argue ahdly original. Early in the essay he says:

Their [the masters of Rome and Greece] knowledge was taken away from nature into closed studios, where pictures were manufactured for many centuries.

That is why their art stopped short.

They closed the doors behind them, thereby destroying their contact with nature.

And the moment when they were gripped by the idealization of form should be considered the collapse of real art.

Because art should not advance toward abbreviation or simplification, but towards complexity.#

His blatant generalization of the past is almost ridiculous--the history of art is not about idealizing forms, but rather of making them more complex. He has got the process of art backwards. Giotto's figures which look highly idealized to us now were considered to be the height of realism in his day. Giotto made a break from the even more idealized Byzantine forms of his predecessors. Velazquez and Rembrandt certainly did not try to idealize forms, though Ingress may have and choosing Ingress to be representative of a general trend in all art history is a travesty if there ever was one. Malevich's travesty is that he is so absolutely unconscious of the complexity and variety of life which is present in the artof the generations who came before him that he can allow himself the most painful reductions of subject matter and terrible simplifications of art and life. He blatantly violates his own statement that art should strive towards complexity even within this very essay. This was the first of the most important statements Malevich makes about art--about what art should be. I presume that he expects his new art, Suprematism to fit these aspirations. We will see how badly he fails at this one. I wish to continue through these moments. He later says:

Between the art of creating and the art of repeating there is a great difference.

To create means to live, forever and forever creating newer and newer things.

This is the age old argument. How can a work of art be completely original? Even the avant-garde artists were far from being completely original. The statement itself is hardly original. One can never escape all repeated pattern, it is inherent to our existence: the earth spins on its axis once a day; a human can only go so long without food. One can recall when Jacobson quoted Spencer at the end of his essay "On Realism in Art." Spencer states how important poetic rhythms are to that art. If one is traveling on a bumpy road there are so many random shocks that one ceases to take note of them; if on the other hand the shocks are regular, the body can be prepared to receive them and the tiniest variations from the rhythm can be felt. This is a perfect metaphor for the avant-garde artist's relationship to convention. He needs something to violate or he will just make a unnoticeable shocks indistinguishable from others. It is quite perplexing that Malevich's act of ultimate creation is the most easily repeatable, one which does not lend itself to any further creation, but we will get to that. Finally he makes a specific argument for his own art.

The forms of Suprematism, the new painterly realism, already testify to the construction of forms out of nothing, discovered by intuitive reason.# We must remember that just a few moments ago he chastised art for having tried to idealize forms and now he advocates departing from nature's forms because that is the only way for an artist to create a pure form. My first reaction to this statement was to say that there is no more idealized form than the square, but I realize that he is making a distinction between ideal forms--the square or geometric shape--and idealized forms--symbolic figures who somehow embody a set of idealized values or physical characteristics. He continues his explanation: Intuitive form should arise out of nothing.

Just as reason, creating things for everyday life, extracts them from nothing and perfects them.

Thus the forms of utilitarian reason are superior to any depictions in pictures.

They are superior because they are alive and have proceeded from material that has been given a new form for the new life.

Here is the Divine ordering crystals to assume another form of existence. Here is the miracle. . . .

There should be a miracle in the creation of art, as well. Malevich claims that man's only free creation is a simple geometric shape-- a square. Whether geometry is a creation is quite debatable: the Greeks, and many mathematicians since have, thought that geometry exposed transcendental truths--these are not truths which were created by men, but only discovered by them. Since when, I must ask, is a square something new? Here we run head on into Malevich's problem: he has cut himself off from all avenues of variety. He has allowed himself only the slightest possible subject matter for painting. Purity and plenty have never been synonymous and certainly aren't here. Even if one does take Malevich's square as the only subject suitable for painting absolutely created by humans, his creation looks rather shabby next to nature's oeuvre. He compares himself directly to nature so it is not really out of line to compare his creation to that of nature herself. His creation is quite pathetic in comparison. Perhaps we have here an unexpected example of humanity's inconsequentiality beside nature. Malevich has taken great pains to make a painting which doesn't copy nature ( though it may copy other paintings) to make something pure. He says:

There is no longer love of the little nooks, there is no longer love for which the truth of art was betrayed.

The square is not a subconscious form. It is the creation of intuitive reason.

The face of the new art.

The square is a living regal infant.

The first step of pure creation in art. Before it there were naive distortions and copies of nature.

. . . .

In the art of Suprematism, forms will live, like all living forms of nature.

This is the rhetoric of religious asceticism, almost paradoxical compared with the rest of the avant garde. No one, however, loves Malevich's little nooks. He proclaims that nature's nooks are no longer, but he has certainly not proposed anything which might be worthy of replacing them. His hatred of convention has gone a step too far. He does in fact make a statement which is quite new in many ways:

The galloping of a horse can be transmitted in a single tone of pencil. But it is impossible to transmit the movement of red green, or blue masses with a single pencil.

Painters should abandon subject matter and objects if they wish to be pure painters.

The demand to achieve the dynamics of painterly plasticity points to the impulse of painterly masses to emerge from the object and arrive at color as an end in itself, at the domination of purely painterly forms as ends in themselves over content and things, at nonobjective Suprematism--at the new painterly realism, at absolute creation.

In the whole history of art, color never played the role of a tangible force of perception as Malevich describes it. He adds:

Art is the ability to create a construction that derives not from the interrelation between form and color and not on the basis of aesthetic taste in a constructions compositional beauty, but on the basis of weight , speed, and direction of movement.

He has simply replaced a series of values which have traditionally been associated with painting and replaced them with a set of new ones, which are no further from imitating nature than the superseded ones. Malevich insists that his pictures be pure because anything beyond the pure interaction of color would be borrowing a subject from nature--If the painting gives a feeling say of pain or peace then it must represent something in nature. Any association of color and or shapes to an emotion is a reference to nature. If someone says that a Rothko reminds them of looking into a landscape(sunset or seascape are usual reactions) the painting is conjuring a visual memory which is blatantly referential. But even if they say the painting is peaceful, they are referencing nature. When you ask a group of people to draw a "peaceful" line a very high percentage will make it horizontal. Psychologists have stated that this is because all humans sleep lying down and thus associate a prostrate position in sleep, a horizontal, with the peacefulness of sleep--a cow probably doesn't think a horizontal is peaceful. Similarly, a "V" shape or a diagonal gives the impression of motion because we are all habituated with gravity and immediately associate the bottom of a picture plane with a floor plane and a tilted shape on this plane as being imbalanced wanting to fall towards the bottom edge. Malevich has certainly not thoguht through the consequences of his statements, or attempted to draw a line when an effect in painting--weight pr movement are two he chose--would stop referin to nature and become pure.

Even if I give up the fight and choose not to challenge him on the conceptual difficulties of having a "pure" art--the brush strokes for example, or the fact that he is still in the convention of making easel paintings--which later less inventive artists trying to become more pure than Malevich, have taken it upon themselves to overcome --even if we consider Malevich a success, we certainly have to ask what has he succeeded in doing. What is glorious if anything about it? It certainly doesn't compare to nature. A pure, cleansed work of art makes the human power of creation look rather pathetic next to nature's awesome variety. He says that his art is the first step and one has to wonder where the next step can be with all the restrictions he has imposed on himself, especially the limited subject matter he has chosen to use. Would he allow himself the passion of the abstract expressionists as a development of his ideas?(One can keep in the back of one's mind that Malevich returned to representative painting in the 1930's)

My reader may wonder why I have under-taken a systematic destruction of Malevich's agenda. What does it bring me? Is this purely a retaliation on my part, trying to make this dead man angry, to shame him for having taken a cheap shot at something I love? I simply wanted to deflate some of the self aggrandizement in his message. He has not ended art, thank God, there is the academy and people who get angry at such destructive plans undertaken with almost religious ferocity. The avant garde's blind nihilist attack (a phrase which is redundant I'm sure) is precisely similar to the cause of every religious war: the butting heads of rather short-sighted fanatics, who care about their beliefs or ideas to the extreme that it makes them blind and anyone but the leader of the religion, absolutely unoriginal. Though it seems to be the rule for many major religions in the world, it is thankfully not a formula for good art. The ideas of Malevich and Albers and the rest of the purists who wanted to use only painterly elements to make paintings now make up the first couple of chapters in any introductory art book. The resulting elucidation of the perceptual forces at work in painting have been expanded upon by psychologists and have been used to reread the entire canon of world art, to show how conscious artists through the ages have been of these forces without their ever having been written down. It is interesting to note that these books rarely use Supremacist paintings as examples, but rather use the Supremacists ideas among others to refer to old master paintings, bringing those back to life.

obably one of the easiest to incorporate back into the academy. He does not reject all conventions the way some artists did. He was trying to make a new realism, to rebuild a new tradition really, which is so similar to the academy's perpetual agenda that he cannot help but be almost immediately assumed into its ranks. His essay " From Cubism and Futurism to Suprematism" infuriated me almost more than other avant garde texts have a tendency to do, so it is quite easy for me to include him into the ranks of the avant-garde. I have overcome a lot of my anger, and have come to understand the logic in Malevich's essay to which I overreacted at first. I am not going to write about how he generalized the art of the past, unwilling to admit any creativity in the thousands of years of art which came before him. I realize that this is not where his argument lies, so it would be ridiculous to chastise him for something which he has so obviously skimmed over. He rushes over the history of art not caring to specify his argument so that he can get to the part where he outlines the steps Supremacists have taken beyond the Futurists-- where he outlines the goals of his art. I won't challenge him on his insults to the history of art which so angered me, but rather I will take my argument to the heart of his own beliefs.
