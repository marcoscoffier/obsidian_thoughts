# Richard Diebenkorn: Painting against the Grain

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Wienberg  
> 20th c. American Art  
> Michael Lobel--section  
> Dec. 6, 1993  
> Richard Diebenkorn: Painting against the Grain  

One of the most interesting polarities in art is between representation, at one end of the stick and, abstraction, at the other end, and I've found myself all over that stick.--Richard Diebenkorn#

Richard Diebenkorn is a California artist who after developing a name for himself as an abstract expressionist, switched to figuration toward the end of 1955, the time when abstract expressionism was at its high point in the New York art world. Diebenkorn was grouped along with two friends David Park and Elmer Bishoff, by Paul Mills, director of the Oakland Art Museum at that time, into a school or style of Bay Area Figuration. Their style was described as "applying an abstract expressionist use of paint to realistic subject matter."# John Gruen who had some of the most wonderful interviews with Diebenkorn said that:

> Individuals and institutions with an investment in abstract expressionism tended to feel that the return to figuration was a defection from the mainstream of contemporary work. Those who had always questioned abstraction or expressive abstraction felt a validation in the defection of serious artists and particularly in that of a fast rising figure such as Richard Diebenkorn.#

After a little more than a decade of figurative painting Diebenkorn switched back to abstraction in 1968 as decisively as he when he made the first transition. This second transition dated and destroyed many of the arguments made supporting his switch to figuration as a battle won in the war against the domination of abstraction in the art world of the fifties and sixties. Diebenkorn didn't make either of these switches to bring attention to himself, or to raise the value of his art work or even to make any kind of statement about art (actions which seem to consume the bulk of the efforts of most of the New York Art world and much of art in America today). He has said, in one of his most adamant statements, "Of course, I don't go into the studio with the idea of ‘saying' something-- that's ludicrous."# Each of his transitions was made because he felt he needed to make a change, at a particular part of his career, for himself. Herschel Chipp says that:

> Richard Diebenkorn was one of a small group of young San Franciscans who...saw the personal feeling of the artist as the sole motivation [for revolting against the prevailing influences of Synthetic Cubism and a lethargic adaption of the Mexican muralist's style.#

This statement is a bit misleading, because Diebenkorn hates to have himself associated with a school, but Herschel Chipp is right that Diebenkorn's art is personally motivated. John Gruen recounts a statement of Diebenkorn's:

> We didn't know we were forming a school,not until Paul Mills came around and told us we were a new movement--the California Figurative Painters! Well, I just hated this thing of getting people together and forming a school. I remember feeling wildly threatened by that.#

Diebenkorn is annoyed because he sees his art as his personal enterprise and being labeled as part of a school throws him into the midst of the "style" wars which rack the art world. Diebenkorn made a statement about the reception of his early figurative pictures, which describes something very much like the reaction one would expect from an avant-garde work: ...during the ‘50s abstract painting was a religion , and it didn't admit to any backsliding to representational imagery. I mean when the three of us-- Park , Bishoff and I -- had exhibitions of our representational stuff in New York, we were attacked you wouldn't believe the incredible things people said to me!#

I believe it is this reaction which made critics want to make a movement out of this return to figuration. (It is interesting to notte that the reaction is of the connaisseurs not among the ingorants) If one sees art as a battle of styles then this step is only logical. Sidney Tillim counters:

> Representation is an impulse not a style. Yet the tendency today is to judge it in terms of received ideas about modernism and, therefore style. A straightforward approach to representation, to art which quotes the Renaissance rather than comic books, is dismissed as academic, while the possibilities of a new narrative art are not even imagined. Instead sociology is substituted for literature, trends for history and topicality for quality. The past is denied a role in the vision of contemporary experience.#

I want to call Diebenkorn a "violent personalist" a statement coined by Harold Bloom referring to himself and by which he means that he is not concerned with poets whose motives are sociopolitical unless they are first and foremost concerned with making an excellent poem which is deeply meaningful to the individual. Diebenkorn has set himself apart from reigning styles and trends of his days to pursue his art. (I will discuss his indebtedness to the trends of the past later in this essay) Michael Kimmelman, chief art critic of the New York Times has said, "when the New York crowd zigged [Diebenkorn] zagged."\#But, Diebenkorn has always sold very well and had a remarkable career financially especially considering that he could not be associated with the major art trends of his generation (he came after the first generation abstract expressionists), and has always bred comments such as Dore Ashton's:

> He appears to me to be a born painter.#

and Hilton Kramer's:

> Painting seems, in [Diebenkorn's] case, not an option but the mode of thought he was born into. (About how many American painters can that be said I wonder?)#

Diebenkorn, has described the motivations for his change from abstraction:

> I came to mistrust my desire to explode the picture. At one time the common device of using the super-emotional to get ‘in gear' with a painting used to serve me for access to painting too, but I mistrust that now. Something was missing in the process-- I sensed an emptiness as though I were a performer. I felt the need for an art that was more contemplative and possibly even in the nature of problem solving. It was almost as though I could do too much too easily. There was nothing hard to come up against. And suddenly the figure paintings furnished a lot of this.#

There is a definite reference to the action painters--the performers-- which he mentions as a absence. He has managed to master what Clement Greenberg, whom Diebenkorn read carefully, claims is essential to abstract art:

> The purely plastic or abstract qualities of the work of art are the only ones that count. Emphasize the medium and its difficulties, and at once the purely plastic, the proper, values of art come to the fore. Over power the medium to the point where all sense of resistance disappears and the adventitious uses of art become more important.#

Diebenkorn has eliminated the resistance of the medium but finds that art making is now too easy he felt that he needed soem of that resistance. He read Greenberg's writings closely and I believe that Diebenkorn's art makes a lot of sense in respect to these arguments. Greenberg says later in "Towards a Newer Laocoon":

> It suffices to say that there is nothing in the nature of abstract art which compels it to be so. The imperative comes from history, from the age in conjunction with a particular moment reached in a particular tradition of art. This conjunction holds the artist in a vise from which at the present moment he can escape only by surrendering his ambition and returning to a stale past. This is the difficulty for those who are dissatisfied with abstract art.... Abstract art cannot be disposed of by simple minded evasion. Or by negation. We can only dispose of abstract art by assimilating it and fighting our way through it.#

Diebenkorn seems to be trying to go beyond abstraction in his return to figuration, to use what he has learned in abstraction and apply it to a new kind of figuration. He has also said:

> You see I was trying to demonstrate something to myself-- that I wasn't going to get stuck in any dumb rut. I felt I could move on to something else. I got a kind of a thrill out of doing that . I said, "I can leave all of this behind."#

There is a rather large discrepancy in the way Diebenkorn talks about his work and the way it is discussed amongst the art critics. There can be no better source on an artist than the words of the artist himself, but an artist like Diebenkorn who allows his emotions to guide his creation process is not always the best source on placing his oeuvre in some kind of historical concept, because he cannot separate himself from his work. The reception of even a personalist artist's work is important to place it into a larger framework, in which it must attain a place or lose its importance and significance. This first statement is by Stanley Tillim, a figurative painter who wrote a series of articles supporting a "new realism" starting in 1961:

> ...the case of Richard Diebenkorn, a crucial personality in the history of new "realism,' is very instructive. Diebenkorn received his rhetoric entirely from Abstract Expressionism while his sense of subject, basically intimiste, resisted the moral elevation implicit in Abstract Expressionism's ‘sweeping' painterly grandeur. I suspect that his return recently to abstraction was compelled by the fact that he could not fulfill a rhetoric to which his subject matter was essentially antagonistic. Diebenkorn's rhetorical style elevated his subject matter to a plane of significance greater than anything accomplished by Pop rhetoric, but his painterly instinct apparently insisted it was not enough. His reversion to abstraction then, is, I think, more a matter of uncertainty rather than a conviction and is not actually contradictory.#

I think it is important here to turn to some of the artwork itself. The most obvious cases of antagonism between the subject matter and the Abstract Expressionist painterly grandeur is in the figurative paintings. He was very interested in how the the presence of a figure which will automatically draw much of the attention in a picture would affect his abstract spaces. The figures often seem to rupture the two dimensional painting space such as in "Girl with Three Coffee Cups(p.91)," in Yale's collection "Girl on a Terrace(p.115)"and most explicitly in "Woman in Profile" were the figure is separated from the space by a window. She cannot blend in with the shapes because she separated from the two dimensional picture space by a window. There can be no all over feeling of paint handling like there was in the abstractins because the figure always separates from the space. This is not always the case in Diebenkorn's paintings. In "Seated Woman, Green Interior(p.130)" Girl Smoking (p.131)" and "Woman at a Table in a Strong Light (p.126)" the shapes of the figures and their surroundings work together much more coherently. Herschel B. Chipp wrote an article "Diebenkorn Paints a Picture" where the creation of "Woman by the Ocean" a painting which falls in the the same category as "Woman in Profile" et al., is documented. The process of reworking described in the making of this figure painting is the same as in his abstractions. Diebenkorn paints waiting for the chance element which makes the painting work and then tries to capitalize on that. The figure does not seem to fit into this mode of painting. It is a form which must be completed. That essential chance element can be lost in the completion.

I find the most effective use of the figure to be in his drawings which are taken directly from observation, unlike the paintings which he developed the same way he would an abstract canvasWhen Diebenkorn paints or draws directly from the figure, something else goes on. He cannot move the figure around as willingly as in the other paintings nor can he change the sex and placement etc. The shapes must be organized in his eye. He cannot react to what is going on in his painting but must react to what he sees. The dialogue is not between him and the painting but between him and his visual experience of the model. Images like "Seated Woman 1967(p.125)," and Reclining Woman, 1964 (p.125)" seem much more complete and beautiful to me than any of the others I have mentioned. The shapes which describe the figure come to have to power of the abstract ones in his earlier paintings. (There is a wonderful book by John Elderfield, The Drawings of Richard Diebenkorn which is on order by the Yale Library system which is full of many more such examples) Diebenkorn seems to move much more fluidly from abstraction to landscape and still life than he did to figure painting. As I have mentioned his abstractions have always been referred to as abstract landscapes. Even his later paintings all of which comprise a series called Ocean Park much more complete abstractions, consistently get compared to a California light, and even shapes which recall the architecture of his California neighborhoods. A painting like "Seawall (p.103) or "Chabot Valley(p.87)" has the completed unchangeable feel of his abstractions. He does not have the option of moving the shapes around at will--the dark green triangle in the middle of "Seawall" wouldn't make sense anywhere else it represents some kind of shadow off the wall, but one wouldn't want to move it. This is exactly what he looks for in an image:

> All I want is for a set of configurations to stay put. I want them to look as though they were meant to be there. That's my idea of ‘meaning.' When that finally happens well then the picture is done.#

Diebenkorn does not have to fight to do this in these landscapes he can find the shapes in nature. His still lives are the same way, he can set them up to create the composition he desires in the painting and then uses his talent to complete the painting. A striking similarity can be drawn between "Table Top/ Cane Chair(p.136)" and "Berkeley No.42, 1955 (p.78)"the smaller shapes to the right in the lower white half of the abstract painting become the letters in the representational one. Diebenkorn's rhetoric remains consistent throughout his career. His best figurative paintings echo the most aspects of his earlier abstractions. His early abstractions were often referred to as Abstract Landscapes a term he resented because he would constantly try to eliminate any form of representation which would creep into these paintings. Many of his early works are even referred to by the images people, tend to see in them, "The Green Huntsman(p.53)" "The Archer(p.56)," "Motorcycle Wreak(p.42)" and "The Disintegrating Pig(p.39)" are four famous examples. He eventually gave up ruining pictures to eliminate the representation which had worked his way into the image and instead tried to emphasize these aspects when they were present. later in the Ocean Park Series the groups of parallel lines parallel to the edges of his paintings which are so common, are reminiscent of the framing devices of doors and windows in the interiors of his figurative period.

The second statement I would like to discuss is much less clear than Sidney Tillim's. I cannot say that Hilton Kramer is an advocate of abstraction, but he is definitely looking for something else in art. He says:

> Diebenkorn's sensibility seems about equally divided between something peculiarly American--a sense of preference for the emphatic, at the expense of fine discriminations, that is given overt expression in the surface rhetoric of his pictures--and something that looks like an allegiance to the great tradition, which is the custodian of the language of fine discriminations. This sensitivity is animated by high ambition, but the ambition is compromised (increasingly, I think) by the division. Traditionally the leverage that American artists have enjoyed in confronting tradition--which is European tradition--has been existential.... They brought an experience to art that was employed to deal with it. What Diebenkorn has done --and in this he may be taken as representative of a generation-- is to trade this existential leverage (or rather identify with it) an aesthetic idea, namely Abstract Expressionism, which was itself deeply in debt to the artistic tradition it is called upon to confront. He has thus sealed off a source of power that even a talent of his power cannot do without.#

The "existential leverage" he mentions is simply the virtue of our being American we don't have to identify with a school or tradition of European Art. This gives the American artist a kind of freedom from tradition which allowed a painter like Jackson Pollock to happen and thrilled the likes of Matisse and DuChamps when they visited the U.S. The more avant-garde of the two (being almost its definition, decided to stay. Diebenkorn, according to Hilton Kramer is not in touch with this "American" quality:

> Beneath the fresh, hip, energetic surface of his paintings is an artist dreaming of the masters#

Only in modern time could such a statement be taken as a kind of an insult. I thought that perhaps it is his dreams of the masters which caused him to turn to figuration, Matisse and Picasso both backed off complete abstraction to a type of figuration. But Diebenkorn's move towards figuration is also his action which is the closest to the existential American leverage Hilton Kramer mentioned. Diebenkorn did not care what the art world was doing, and made his switch which prompted the fuss over a new style, the Bay Area School etc., but I cannot help but see his return to the regular Ocean Park series as a weak move in comparison. I do find the paintings in the series to be some of his best, the most refined and sometimes the most moving, but I miss the inquiry and questioning about art, his art, which went into his figurative years.

Paul Mills the man who originally declared the Bay Area Figurative's School made a remark which may at first seem very condescending but it brings to light a fundamental aspect of art:

There are two kinds of significant painters in the world--the talented and the untalented. among the untalented a few have the strength of character, the vision, and sometimes just the stubbornness to create some germinal form of new art structure. Cezanne, for instance, was one of the ‘untalented.' In comparison to the figure drawings of his contemporaries, his are obviously rather inept. Certainly Jackson Pollock for all his immense effect on modern painting, was ‘untalented.' Somehow this often seems true of those who initiate the most monumental changes in direction in painting.

Diebenkorn, on the other hand, belongs to the world of the talented: those who can draw beautifully, who have an unfailing sense of color and brushstroke, who have--pardon the expression--taste....I am a person who likes a bit of talent in a painter. whatever the theoretical significance of a work, it gets a bit academic without the sensuous, less describable satisfactions of looking at superb painting.#

Critics and artists can be drawn to Diebenkorn by his talent, in the same way that others can be turned off by his lack of innovation. Mills goes on to say that:

> "talent can... be as much of a problem as an asset. For every artist with something to say but the inability to say it well there are two who could say something well if they just had something to say...{those] who first dazzle one with their brilliance but later cloy with their repetition of successful formulas."#

I'm afraid that I feel this repetition in the Ocean Park series. I wish I could see as much variety and search there as in the figurative work. But I will always be drawn to and moved by his paintings because they are in touch with a sensitivity and care for the personal which I find truly lacking in much American art today.
