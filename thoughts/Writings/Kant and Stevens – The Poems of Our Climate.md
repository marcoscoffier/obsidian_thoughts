# Kant and Stevens: The Poems of Our Climate

The Poems of Our Climate

I

Clear water in a brilliant bowl,
Pink and white carnations. The light
In the room more like a snowy air,
Reflecting snow. A newly-fallen snow
At the end of winter when afternoons return.
Pink and white carnations--one desires
So much more than that. The day itself
Is simplified: a bowl of white,
cold, a cold porcelain, low and round,
With nothing more than the carnations there.

II

Say even that this complete simplicity
Stripped one of all one's torments, concealed
The evilly compounded vital I
And made it fresh in a world of white,
A world of clear water, brilliant edged,
Still one would want more, one would need more,
More than a world of white and snowy scents.

III

There would still remain the never resting mind,
So that one would want to escape, come back
To what had been so long composed.
The imperfect is our paradise.
Note that, in this bitterness, delight,
Since the imperfect is so hot in us,
Lies in flawed words and stubborn sounds.

--Wallace Stevens

Wallace Stevens and Kant: From the Beautiful to the Sublime

I have chosen to discuss Wallace Stevens' "The Poems of Our Climate" in the terms of Kant's "Analytic of the Beautiful" which is found in his Critique of Judgment. Because of the complexity of Kant's argument and definitions, and the length restrictions on this paper, I can only concentrate on the first stanza of this poem, and on a few of the categorizations Kant makes about the beautiful. I realized that I would have to mention the sublime because my attraction to this poem has much more to do with this category than that which Kant defines as the beautiful, but my discussion of the sublime will be far less thorough than that on the beautiful. The assignment for this essay was to defend a favorite poem as beautiful in terms of Kant's aesthetics. Wallace Stevens' poem is itself the description of the contemplation of an object upon which a judgment of taste is to be made, a flower arrangement. The poem is essentially the description by the poet of his reaction to this object and the atmosphere which surrounds it. Stevens' poem, therefore, acts like a lens through which I, the reader, can by using the poet's eyes and his sensibilities, enjoy his flower arrangement and its climate. I cannot separate my enjoyment of the poem in itself from his view of this flower arrangement and thus must examine his reaction to the flowers, and why I find it beautiful before I can study my reaction to his poem as an object of beauty in itself. His reaction precedes and defines everything which I can find beautiful in this poem. In order for this discussion to make any sense, we have to be sure about when we are discussing a natural object and when our discussion is of an art object. To begin this discussion, I will treat Stevens' flowers as a natural object and the poem as a work of art. The flowers, especially in the latter two stanzas, could be seen as a work of art because they are in an arrangement, and the word "composed" in the final stanza clues us off to the fact that at that point the flowers are some kind of art work, but in the first stanza, Stevens does not really treat them so, and thus they will serve the purpose of natural objects to begin our discussion. The first stanza of Stevens' poem describes the flower arrangement itself, (rather than the poets meditations upon the arrangement) more precisely than the two succeeding verses: Clear water in a brilliant bowl,
Pink and white carnations. The light
In the room more like a snowy air,
Reflecting snow. A newly-fallen snow
At the end of winter when afternoons return.

Pink and white carnations--one desires
So much more than that. The day itself
Is simplified: a bowl of white,
Cold, a cold porcelain, low and round,
With nothing more than the carnations there.

It is important to note that the description of the flowers contains very few adjectives: clear, pink, white, cold...etc.. The description of the object is very sparse. There are pink and white carnations in a porcelain bowl. The snow metaphor describes the air around the flowers. One could not possibly recreate the physical scene with the bowl and the flowers, because the description leaves out so much. The description is as sparse and as simple as the poet tells us the scene is. The only information we, as readers, receive is the poet's mediated version of this scene and (more importantly for our purposes) of this object. This may seem like a trivial point but it becomes very important when we start talking in Kantian terms.

Before I can begin an analysis of the poem, I have to give some preliminaries of Kant's philosophy. Kant's aesthetics are rooted in his theory of knowledge where he has divided knowing or perception into two categories: the sensual, which consists of a physical reaction to the material world, and the mind or reason, where these perceptions are organized into a meaningful whole. In his "First Moment. Of the Judgment of Taste, According to Quality" he makes a distinction between the good, the pleasant and the beautiful. He says:

> That which gratifies a man is called pleasant; that which merely pleases him is called beautiful; that which is esteemed or approved by him, i.e. that to which he accords an objective worth is good.(p.251) The pleasant is that which gratifies the first category (sensual), the good the second (mind/reason). The beautiful is more elusive. It is much harder to define. He spends the rest of the essay discussing and categorizing this third element; so much so that he really problemetizes our ability to allow anything (especially an art object) to be beautiful. I will make this point much clearer shortly. Kant goes on to say: We may say that, of these three kinds of satisfaction, that of taste in the beautiful is alone a disinterested and free satisfaction; for no interest either of sense or of reason here forces our assent.(p.251)

The beautiful, he says, does not satisfy a desire the way the pleasant and the good do and can therefore not be affected by interest. The satisfactions in the pleasant and the good are both bound up with interest, only the judgment of taste and thus of beauty is disinterested. He has previously linked interest to desire:

> The satisfaction which we combine with the representation of the existence of an object is called "interest". Such satisfaction always has reference to the faculty of desire...(p.248)

Because Wallace Stevens gets no direct satisfaction of a desire from looking at this flower arrangement, it, to make a preliminary judgment, falls into the category of the beautiful rather than that of the pleasant or the good. These flowers cannot be pleasant the way an apple can be pleasant when it gratifies one's hunger, nor can it please the reason and thus be considered good. Kant makes an important statement which allows us to consider Stevens' poem itself as an object of beauty: Now when the question is if a thing is beautiful, we do not want to know whether anything depends or can depend on the existence of the thing, whether for myself or anyone else, but how we judge it by mere observation (intuition or reflection)...in saying it is beautiful...I am concerned, not with that on which I depend on the existence of the object, but with that which I make out of this representation in myself.(p.248-9)

If beauty is a disinterested judgment it does not depend on a physical object to exist. This statement allows a poem to be considered beautiful. Otherwise one would have to ask how a poem could be beautiful at all? This poem is a recording of Stevens' "mere observation," which we can consider beautiful because that is precisely what Kant says he is concerned with in the beautiful. The reader cannot recreate the scene before Wallace Stevens, and thus our judgment of this poem cannot depend on the scene as the object of our satisfaction. Kant allows us to call the poem beautiful in itself without us seeing the object to which it refers. Stevens' poem is not as much a description of the flowers as it is a description of that which is going on in his mind when he is confronted with this flower arrangement and its setting. Wallace Stevens does not have to describe the flower arrangement for us to feel its beauty. Its effects and reverberations in his poem become our vehicle to a disinterested satisfaction for us. Thus as a preliminary judgment, both the poem and the flowers are objects of beauty--the former a work of art (or representation) the latter a natural object.

Kant in his "Second Moment," goes on to state that the beautiful, unlike the pleasant, is universal. Here begins the most problematic part of his specifications. He says:

> ... [man] must not call it beautiful if it merely pleases him. Many things may have for him charm and pleasantness--no one troubles himself at that--but if he gives out anything as beautiful, he supposes in others the same satisfaction; he judges not merely for himself but for everyone, and speaks of beauty as if it were a property of things.(p.252)

I have to ask how does one discover what is beautiful for everyone? In order to make a point, I would now like to use Stevens' flower arrangement as though it were a work of art. Wallace Stevens had a hobby of making flower arrangements. He obviously has a more sensitive eye than I do for the beauty of a flower arrangement. Let us say that I hate flower arrangements, that I find them tacky and trite. If I passed by his flowers I would never see the beauty in them, but if he tells me about the beauty he sees in these flowers, I come to an appreciation of their beauty without having to see them. In fact if I were confronted with them, their presence could subvert his description of them, because I could just look at them and say, "they're not beautiful, or simple." My judgment of his poem is disinterested and therefore I can call it beautiful. I do not need the object to get the satisfaction of my judgment of taste I can say that his description of the flowers convinces me of their beauty, but that does not mean that the next time I am confronted with pink and white carnations, I will find them beautiful. In the same manner, I cannot say that poems by Stevens' are beautiful because I can never be sure of absolute consensus. If Stevens cannot convince me that pink and white carnation arrangements are beautiful, I cannot convince you that poems by Wallace Stevens are beautiful. Kant upholds this statement when he says: ...there can be no rule according to which anyone is forced to recognize anything as beautiful. We cannot press [upon others] by the aid of any reasons or fundamental propositions our judgment that a coat, a house, or a flower is beautiful. (p.254)

And yet Kant maintains that taste is universal:

> ...we cannot say that each man has his own particular taste. For this would be as much as to say that there is no taste whatever, i.e., no aesthetical judgment which can make rightful claim upon everyone's assent.(p.253)

Earlier, I made it a special point to say that I wished to treat Stevens' flowers as a work of art. It is much harder to assume anything universal about an art object. The type of art object which concerns this paper is an individual's reaction to something in his world (I'm taking a pseudo-Platonic stance, the second distancing from the forms is far enough), in this case Stevens has reacted to a flower arrangement. We are on the edge of elucidating a deep contradiction in Kant which cannot be resolved. The beautiful, as defined by Kant, will become utterly elusive. Kant says just a little earlier that a man must not call something beautiful "if it merely pleases him....--but if he gives out something as beautiful, he supposes in others the same satisfaction." I have already asked how can one know everyone's aesthetical judgment? This theory presupposes an amount of consensus in people's judgments, which I cannot justify in reference to art works. I agree that all humans share certain experiences, but these are very few. It is hard to prove any beyond the fact that we all have come from a woman's womb, and possibly that we are all in the same predicament because we all exist on this earth with a limited number of common reactions to our world. Thus the concepts which are inherent to us all seem to all relate to nature, or our experience of the world, and the only truly universal beauty is that which we might recognize in a natural object. The beautiful in an art work can only exist within a group which shares an aesthetic judgment which can make rightful claim upon all the members of that group. It is impossible to conceive of an art object which would be universal enough to be considered beautiful by all humans. In order for an object of art to be beautiful at all, its universality has to be confined to a specific group within which there can be many more similarities between individuals than one can assume for humanity in general. Kant divides beauty into two categories which will help further this discussion:

> There are two kinds of beauty: free beauty or merely dependent beauty.... the first presupposes no concept of what the object ought to be; the second does presuppose such a concept and the perfection of the object in accordance therewith.(p.256)

It seems that if the beautiful must presuppose the consensus of others it is by definition a dependent beauty, and yet right after making the statement that the beautiful must be universal, Kant introduces a category of the beautiful which does no refer to any concept-- that of free beauty. Kant, however, never makes the statement I have suggested which might save him this contradiction: He never blatantly associates free beauty with natural beauty and dependent with the beauty of a work of art. He has thus set up a contradiction: How can beauty be universal and free? Kant makes no statement about the universality of a free beauty. He does say that flowers are free beauties, but I, as I have in a previous example, can very easily say that I do not think flowers are beautiful. Picking flowers to be beautiful is an arbitrary judgment. Kant says that everything which does not presuppose a concept of what the object ought to be (and perfection therewith) is beautiful. I, therefore, can just as easily say that shit or dirt is beautiful. They both occur in nature like flowers and have no preconcieved notion of perfection. Kant has given us no way of classifying something or anything as a free beauty, yet he chooses a stereotypical idea of beauty to make an example of a free beauty. Kant never mentions how one could pick out what is a free beauty, and he does not say that everything in nature is a free beauty.

If he did mean just that which he seems to hint at in theory though not in his examples, he could be accredited with beginning of what is now a rather popular idea about beauty. If one says that everything in nature is beautiful, then he/she could just as easily be saying that nothing is beautiful and thus shit can make its way into the realm of the beautiful. If everything in nature is beautiful, then the only way of singling out any one thingand use it to pass on the satisfaction inherent in the beautiful is through a work of art. I posit that the the medium which allows us to highlight a natural object and emphasize its beauty as we see it, and thus raise it above everything to the position the beautiful should take, is precisely the artistic medium--which is dependent upon the aesthetical judgment of a group to be beautiful in itself. Only art can say that a flower is more beautiful than shit or vice versa. I could write a poem about shit which could produce the disinterested satisfaction of the beautiful in you, if you find my poem beautiful (I am reminded of several passages in Joyce's Ulysses). There are similarly many problems in describing an object of dependent beauty, which in this discussion I am going to call a work of art. As soon as one has categorized an object as belonging to a kind of beauty, it is no longer beautiful. As soon as I say that an object is beautiful because I can identify the aesthetic taste or concept shared by a group which upholds this object as a perfect example of beauty, I have removed myself from among those who share this aesthetic taste. I have exposed myself to other concepts of taste in order to classify that which had been my own. If I say that a statue is beautiful because it is a perfect example of the eighteenth century French ideal, I leave open the question: What is my ideal? I must know of other ideals to identify this particular statue as eighteenth century French, so I have to ask which ideal is mine? What is the late twentieth century American universal ideal of beauty? It seems to be an unfortunate situation of this age that we have been exposed to so many different ideals of beauty, that one can no longer claim one for one's own. Western hegemony is being overcome and there is no new universal. The possibility of art being beautiful by Kant's definition (meaning that it must also be universal) has been destroyed. Though I believe that a feeling of beauty is something which all humans share, I find it impossible to track down any one object which would invariably elicit this response from us all, today. Wallace Stevens seems to realize that he cannot convince us of the beauty of his flower arrangement. He does not describe to us the perfectly balanced symmetry of pink and white the way one of his Renaissance predecessors might, nor does he mention the exquisite natural perfection of the flowers. He sees beyond the small group of whom he may be a part( those who find flower arrangements beautiful) and does not describe its beauty. He goes beyond it and describes the climate of this scene--which as I have mentioned in a previous argument is more universal--we are all confronted by our situation of existence in the world. Kant also goes beyond the object to the boundless expanses around it when he defines the category of the sublime:

> The beautiful in nature is connected with the form of the object, which consists in having definite boundaries. The sublime, on the other hand, is to be found in a formless object, so far as in it or by occasion of it boundlessness is represented...(p.261)

The sublime, he says, moves us but requires no object (or its object is boundless) and does not produce pleasure like the beautiful, but moves us because when confronted by it, we are caught between pleasure and pain. The reason that I feel compelled to mention this final category is that it is much closer to the most captivating part of Stevens' poem, which is not so much about the flower arrangement as it is about the atmosphere in which the flowers are placed. In the first stanza he says:

The light

In the room more like a snowy air,
Reflecting snow. A newly-fallen snow
At the end of winter when afternoons return.

One has to ask, what is a snowy air reflecting snow. It is a very precise, yet intangible almost imperceivable image. It could be described by other words used more frequently in the poem especially clear, cold, and simplicity, which give us a more complete sense of what I find to be the most memorable image,feeling, concept(?) in this poem. This feeling is certainly not bound up in the form of the flowers, but permeates the entire atmosphere around them. One cannot say that the experience of this feeling is one of pleasure, it is cold and empty, thus it seems definitely to lie in the realm of the sublime.

Kant has ousted the possibility of there existing a beautiful object, it seems we are left with only the sublime to truly move us. In wonderfully precise and delicate way ( to which I can associate because I share somse ground in the poetic tradition which finds his work beautiful), Stevens has described a sublime setting, the only category in Kant's aesthetics which can move man. He seems to have successfully worked his way out of the dilemna posed by our analysis of Kant's theory, and yet he calls out against the sparseness of this modern condition when he repeats, "one desires so much more than that."

What is interesting about literature and art in general is not how precisely it mimics nature, because it cannot, but that it is an individual's particular way of mimicking nature. Art can never be nature. A work of art is an artist's or poet's view of what is beautiful--his personal way of mediating an image or idea through a word or an image. Confronted with Steven's pink and white carnations, I probably would not find them to be beautiful. But when I see them through his eyes--when he tells me how he finds something to be beautiful-- it becomes beautiful for me.

I wish to analyze the poet's reaction in terms of Kant's aesthetics, because I believe that his fine description of an elusive something about these flowers is very much in common with Kant's definition of the elusive beautiful and maybe even the more elusive sublime:

Discussing the representation of a representation would infuriate Plato, but since we are taking a course on literature, which is an art of representation, we have to be able to discuss the beauty or interest of different levels of representation. We have already accepted that we are discussing the concept of literature. We have placed ourselves in the world of "play" defined by Schiller which is distanced from the realities of sense and mind.
