# John the Baptist by Nardo di Cione and Taddeo di Bartolo

> [!quote]- Cover sheet
> Marco Scoffier  
> Prof. Gilbert  
> Pioneers of the Renaissance  
> March 1, 1993  
> John the Baptist by Nardo di Cione and Taddeo di Bartolo  

I want to compare two paintings of John the Baptist, one by Nardo di Cione, a Florentine, the other by Taddeo di Bartolo who lived in Siena. Each of these paintings is one of a pair which hangs in the Yale gallery. Their respective partners are St. Jerome for Taddeo and and St. Peter for Nardo. I was attracted to each of these pairs of works not by their subject matter but for their aesthetic value. The fact that they share a subject makes the comparison easier. I was attracted to the humanness of Taddeo-- which is most evident in the fleshy, bony and even veinyness of John the Baptist's arm and hand and the expression in both his and Jerome's face--, and the carefully balanced beauty of Nardo-- especially the soft undulating texture of St. Peter's robes, a texture so luscious that it cannot be ascribed to this world. The subtle blending of the highlights and the overall synthesized balance of Nardo's paintings contrasts sharply with the brutal changes and decidedly human imbalance of Taddeo's. Each of their John's has long dark hair, wears a hair shirt or suit, holds a staff with a cross on top in their left hand and crosses their right forearm across their body. The viewer feel's directed to the right of each painting, by John's gaze, the forearm and the turn of the body. The pose and iconography seem typical of John the Baptist portraits of the time.# The camel's hair shirt is the adornment of a prophet according to the New Catholic Encyclopedia, and was given to John when he was young. Taddeo's John holds a scroll which is part of the iconography of the prophet.

The general pose of the accompanying Saints is remarkably similar. They are both more frontal than their Johns, especially Nardo's St. Peter whose feet are on the same plane (parallel to the picture plane) and who looks directly at us. The head of Taddeo's St. Jerome is tilted to one side but his body is not turned the way his John the Baptist's is. Both Jerome and Peter hold, a quilt pen and a key, respectively, in their right hand and a book, presumably the bible, in their left. St. Jerome was the most learned man of his day (he lived from 345-419 A.C.), and wrote many scriptural documents, thus the pen is a part of his iconography as are the Cardinal's robes and hat. One of his most famous stories is of how he cured a lion's paw who then became his friend for life. There is a definite friendliness in Taddeo's painting, which I take to be deliberate after discovering the personality of the saint. St. Peter (the rock on whom Christ built his church) holds the key to God's kingdom. No personality can be felt in this portrait, there is an air of religious formalism and symmetry about it. Taddeo's works have a human quality, Nardo's that of the divine. I will elaborate on this distinction shortly
Nardo's figures are represented in full length, and are in a frame which points at the top. Taddeo's less than half length busts are on square panels which are damaged around the sides and have no frame. One cannot doubt that Nardo's Paintings are in their original shape the structure of the works extends right to the frames-- there is an impressed decoration in the gilding which runs along the frame . One cannot be sure about Taddeo's. The figures could have been cut off or the paintings cropped from any of the four sides. I haven't done any research to try to find if any cropping actually occurred, but it is the doubt, the ambiguity that lies about the boundaries of Taddeo's works which I find representative of other aspects of his paintings. The hair of his John is too red, it is not the same color as his beard and does not fit in with any overall feeling of the painting. Unlike Nardo we are constantly jarred by incongruities in Taddeo's painting. The darks of John's robes are too abrupt. They are concentrated about the left shoulder, which we guess he may have been trying get to recede but the contrast is too sharp and feels unnatural. It looks like there is a red stain on his left shoulder. The small sliver of red between his right upper arm and his rib cage, which should be dark because it is underneath the piece of his robe which falls over that shoulder is on the other hand too light. All of these incongruities could be attributed to the way the paint is preserved, but I do not believe so; there are other examples evident elsewhere. The fleshy and bony realism which I was attracted to in the arm and the hands is contradicted and is prevented from becoming truly modeled by a dark cartoon-like outline. Nardo's human features are outlined as well but his forms blend into it. The same brown is used in the darks of the flesh and in the outline, which allows them to work together. Taddeo uses greenish-grey darks and pink highlights to model his human features. The combination of these colors is radical compared to Nardo, who only uses different hues of the same color for each different part of the painting. Such a combination could only arise in someone who is willing to let things stick out and who is not looking for an overall refined feeling in his painting. Taddeo's knuckles want to jump out of their static outline. The muscles in his arm can almost live on their own. There is a fleshy reality in these features which far surpasses Nardo's.

Nardo's figures are much more balanced. There are no glaring incontinuities. Everything is carefully blended. Even the movement of John's pose to the right is balanced by a piece of his robe which is flapping off to the left and his trailing right foot which turns the pose back to the left. The major movement, more precisely is not only to the right but upwards to the cross of his staff. The robe pulls you upwards from his extended right foot and towards his left hand into what ends up being a path which runs parallel to the staff. All of the creases in the robe follow this general direction. His hand points to the cross and at the culmination of these converging rays is that of his gaze which goes above and beyond the head of the staff, toward heaven. The cuff of his left hand is folded over to reveal the rich blue lining of the robe. From this hand is a cascading sliver of blue which falls down the length of the staff and accentuates it. There is an overall feeling of flowing and beauty. The movements are carefully developed to work together, to accentuate the staff-- the religious devotion, and to balance the image-- to make it visually symmetric. The human features are muted; they do not interfere with the grand motions of the image. The staffs are represented in completely different ways. Nardo's is gilded which separates it from the parts were it overlaps the figure, but blends into the background as it separated from it either by a thin black line or an impressed decoration (like that on the halo's). The fact that this religious icon is largely indifferentiable from the decoration of the background is rather interesting. One cannot allow it to exist on the same level as the figure. It doesn't even rest on the floor, but extends off the bottom of the picture plane. It remains purely a religious symbol which cannot be treated in the same way as the figure. Taddeo's staff is not nearly so interesting. It is thin and flimsy, and doesn't even take up all of his grasp. He is holding the parchment with the same hand and holds both loosely though his fingers are rigid and separated. There doesn't seem to be any reason for the strange way he is holding these objects. The cross bar of the crucifix at the head of the staff does not lie parallel to the slope of the shoulders as does Nardo's, but juts abruptly upwards. It cannot lie comfortably in the composition but seems to be wrenching itself out of any static position.

John the Baptist's blessing is one of the most important parts of his iconography. He Baptized Christ. He blessed Christ in the name of the Lord, and thus one can assume that the way his blessing is represented in these two paintings is of key importance. Nardo's figure looks and blesses upwards--towards the heavens. Taddeo's blessing seems to be particular. He looks directly at us and holds his hand almost completely overturned as though he were cradling something near his breast (one feels that you could put a change purse or something in his palm). His blessing does not seem to be directed any where, not up or across like most John the Baptist portraits, or even at us like Giotto's (see note 1), but contained within himself. One wonders if Taddeo's hands weren't so expressive, whether such a slight change of gesture would be noticed. The expression in Taddeo's work is the ultimate result of the realism of his features. Of all the blessings I saw, his arm hangs the lowest, as though he were about to drop the blessing all together. There is a feeling of relaxation in this gesture. I have also mentioned how he holds the staff so loosely. One cannot say that he is about to drop it because his fingers are so rigid, but he does not hold it firmly, it could fall back and forth within his grip. John's mouth is half way open. Combined with the relaxing gestures and the direct stare, I get the impression that he could drop all this religious iconography and talk to me. He does not clutch on to it as the definition of his character. There is definitely communication possible between the viewer and this saint which is not possible with Nardo's. So far I have developed the ideas that Nardo's works show and overall flowing unity and symmetry which accentuates the religious icon, and that Taddeo's is almost exactly the opposite, he does not have unity or balance, but instead shows human emotion which draws attention away from the religious icon. These points seem upheld by the two accompanying paintings. Nardo's St. Peter looks directly but does not inspire any communication-- if anything his mouth seems tightly closed. His eyes don't even draw our attention though they look straight ahead.# Our attention is brought instead to the Bible in his hand, sharp black on yellow, and in the dead center of the painting. The gesture of the upheld key is even muted by being placed in a small blue sliver of robe. The completely frontal pose, the folds in the cloth and the sharp black on yellow centers all of our attention on the Bible, the religious icon. Taddeo's St. Jerome similarly upholds the inferences we have made about Taddeo. Jerome's frontalness is disrupted by the tilt of his head towards his pen. The raised pen and tilted head seem to become one motion. Again the eyes catch our attention. We are intrigued by his expression; he is asking, inviting and inspires conversation almost more than his companion. One gets the aforementioned feeling of friendliness. Jerome's book is separated from the main action of the painting. It falls out of notice at the bottom of the panel. What attracted me to each of these paintings is exactly what the other artist chose not to concentrate on. Taddeo concentrates on the human features, and tends to neglect the drapery, whereas Nardo mutes the human features to the level of his drapery which takes on the expressive quality of his work. Nardo's drapery is the most three dimensional part of his work especially around the cuffs and were it wraps around the backs of the bodies. Taddeo's drapery is hardly three dimensional, St. Jerome's cardinal's cloak is completely two dimensional. The slits for Jerome's arms and the cuffs of John's robe do not account for the three dimensionality he has created in the hands and arms and are both too narrow. They like the sharp black outlines seem to contradict the three dimensionality he had created in the hands arms and face.

I would like to end this paper with what a few art historians have had to say about these artists. Neither quote refers directly to the paintings we have been studying, but they do seem to uphold the observations I have made about these works. Bruce Cole writes in his book, Sienese Painting in the Age of the Renaissance, that Taddeo was not imitated and found "little echo" in later Sienese art, because his "work approached reality too closely," and his "holy figures seemed demystified by their nearness." He also says that "No other contemporary so fully developed the figure or paid so much attention to drawing."# These comments seem perfectly in line with the human expression, asymmetry and de-emphasized religious icons we have noticed in his SS. John and Jerome. Richard Offner gives more esoteric description of Nardo's work. He claims they have, "a plasticity incidental to the description of shape, rather than the direct bodying forth of plastic existence." We have noticed the three dimensionality of his robes, but they never take on a human existence they are held vibrating in their own reality inside the symmetric, balanced framework he has maintained throughout the entire painting. Offner also described Nardo as "the most romantic artist of his age," and a "poet", and says that his figures "betray an inner flutter," and are "feminine at heart."# I do not wish to elaborate on Offner's statements, which do open up a lot of possible discussion, especially the part about being "feminine" (which is a rather dated statement) but only wish to show that they support observations I have made about Nardo's work. The soft subtle blended folds of cloth in Nardo's do take on a feminine quality when compared with the knuckly musculature of Taddeo. Taddeo di Bartolo created a human reality which scared off imitators, which arose out of his ability to leave things unbalanced and contrasting in his paintings. Nardo di Cione has created a balanced beauty which lives in a world of its own and connects to the divine quality of his subjects.
